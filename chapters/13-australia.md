# Australia

## Generation 0: The Oxford Hotel and the Schools (1858–1899)

**Thursday, 31 August 1882. Circular Quay, Sydney.**

The steamer *Rotomahana* casts off for New Zealand carrying the first representative rugby team New South Wales has ever sent anywhere. There are sixteen men aboard. There were supposed to be more.

Three weeks earlier the colony had played its first-ever match against Queensland, and afterwards more than thirty of the best players in New South Wales had put their names forward for the tour. Eighteen were chosen. Then the list began to shrink — not through injury or loss of form, but because, as the New Zealand historians R. H. Chester and N. A. C. McMillan record it, several of them "had problems obtaining leave." Three more — C. Rygate, F. Butler and I. Logan — dropped out at the last moment. What sailed was sixteen, including the man who was captain, manager and selector at once, **Ted Raper**, and a secretary who was also one of the forwards, **R. Thallon**.

Six of the sixteen came from the University club. Three each came from Wallaroo, Redfern and Balmain, and one from St Leonard's.

Hold those two facts together, because the whole chapter is in them. A month away from work, unpaid, at the other end of the Tasman Sea, was not a thing every good footballer in Sydney could afford — and the men who could afford it came disproportionately from one club, attached to one university. Two years later, when New Zealand assembled its own first representative side, it suffered withdrawals for the same reason: work. The difference is not the problem. The difference is what happened next, and who was left holding the game afterwards.

They were not, in the event, very good. The *Rotomahana* ran aground coming up Auckland harbour on 6 September, so the reception committee had to be dropped aboard by the Devonport ferry at half past nine in the morning; the visitors were ferried ashore to the Royal Mail Hotel, where the locals thought them light in weight. At the Auckland Domain that Saturday, playing four half-hour spells, they lost 7–0. Raper went off with a cut head.

Now go back, because by 1882 the argument that shapes Australian rugby for the next hundred and forty years has already been lost — in another city, in another code, by a man who learned his football at Rugby School.

---

### The letter that took the continent away

On **10 July 1858** a twenty-two-year-old cricketer wrote to *Bell's Life in Victoria* suggesting that cricketers ought to take up some sort of football in the winter to keep fit.

His name was **Thomas Wentworth Wills**. He had been born on the Molonglo Plains in New South Wales in 1835, educated in Melbourne until 1852, and then sent to England — to **Rugby School**, where, in the Australian Dictionary of Biography's summary, "he played football and captained the cricket XI." He came home in 1856 a considerable cricketer, played for Victoria, and in 1857–58 was secretary of the Melbourne Cricket Club.

The response to his letter, the ADB records, "enabled him, his cousin **H. C. A. Harrison**, and others to draw up rules for a football game later to be known as Victorian or Australian Rules."

It is worth pausing on how odd that is, given everything else in this book. The **cricket club as incubator** is one of the oldest threads here — the mechanism that produced rugby and association football in Montevideo, in Buenos Aires, in Valparaíso, wherever British commerce put down a clubhouse and a winter to fill. In Melbourne the same mechanism, worked by a man who had learned the game at Rugby itself, produced neither of those codes. It produced a third one. And that third code took Melbourne, and then Adelaide, and then Perth, and for a while Brisbane too.

Why he did not simply import what he had played at school is the sort of question that attracts tidy answers. The most commonly repeated is that Wills thought the Melbourne ground too hard to fall on. It is a good story and it may be true; it is not in the ADB's account, and it should be treated as tradition rather than record. What is not in doubt is the outcome. **Before rugby in Australia had a single club, its principal domestic rival already existed, and had been founded by a Rugby School old boy out of a cricket club.**

Australian rugby union has spent its entire history as a two-city game. This is where that starts. Not in 1907, when the professionals left; not in the 1990s, when four codes were selling themselves to the same schoolboys. In 1858, in a letter to a sporting paper.

Wills himself came to a bad end — he drank, and in 1880 he took his own life. He is remembered in the other code.

### Sydney, and the boys who chose

Rugby in Sydney arrives, as it does nearly everywhere in this book, without ceremony and without a clear first day.

The **Albert Cricket Club** published football laws alongside the laws of cricket in its annual report for 1860 — the cricket-club incubator again, this time doing what it does elsewhere — and by the report of 1862–63 those laws are described as "the Laws of Football as played at Rugby school." Clubs follow. Which one was first is genuinely contested and should stay that way: the **Sydney Football Club**, founded on 30 May 1865 and formally constituted a week later, has the better documentary claim, while the **Sydney University** club is often given the honour and is sometimes dated to 1863 — a date for which, as the most careful of the amateur historians of the game concedes, "no reliable evidence has been found to date." The book has been here before, at Le Havre. The honest position is that Sydney had rugby clubs from the middle 1860s and that the race for primacy is a later enthusiasm.

What can be dated is who taught it to boys, and the answer is not what a reader of the England chapter would expect.

**Newington College** opened in 1863. Its first headmaster, George Metcalfe, had come from Melbourne Grammar, and he introduced the game he knew: Australian rules. Rugby appears to have arrived under his successor. In **1869** Newington is reported to have played Sydney University under Rugby rules — said to be the first rugby match played by a regular school side in Australia — and in **1870** Newington played **The King's School**, in what is usually called the first inter-school rugby fixture in the country.

So even in Sydney, the code was not inherited. It was chosen, by schoolmasters, one school at a time. On the other side of the continent the same thing was happening with the opposite result: rugby was introduced at **Hale School** in Perth by its headmaster in 1878 and organised into competition by his successor, **Thomas Breama Beuttler** — another Rugby School old boy — and then, by about 1885, Western Australian enthusiasm shifted to the Victorian code and rugby quietly died out there.

Melbourne, Sydney, Perth, and shortly Brisbane: four cities, four staffrooms, and a continent divided between two codes on the strength of what a handful of schoolmasters decided to teach. This is the mechanism the rest of the chapter runs on, and it is worth naming plainly now: in Australia, the institution that decided what code a boy would play was **a school**, and usually a particular kind of school.

### The Wallaroo, the Oxford Hotel, and the men who could get leave

The club that mattered most in early Sydney was founded in **1870** by a young clerk in the New South Wales public service named **Richard Aldous Arnold**, who captained it as well. The Australian Dictionary of Biography's entry on him is four sentences long on the subject and does the chapter's work for it:

> "An enthusiastic sportsman, Arnold help to organize Rugby football in the colony. He founded in 1870 and captained the Wallaroo Club and from 1874 was an office-bearer of the Southern Union (later New South Wales Rugby Football Union)."

Two details from earlier in the same entry matter as much. Arnold "was educated at **Rugby School, England**." And of his rise through the public service — clerk of select committees in February 1873, clerk of records that May, his salary risen from the £200 he started on to £400, two brothers joining him on the staff — the ADB observes only that "advancement was rapid **while his father was Speaker**."

The man who founded Australia's first great rugby club and helped run its first union had been to Rugby, and was the son of the Speaker of the Legislative Assembly.

On **28 July 1874**, at the **Oxford Hotel** in Sydney, delegates from the city's clubs formed the **Southern Rugby Football Union** — the first governing body for the game in Australia, and one of the earliest anywhere, three years after the RFU and before Ireland, Wales or New Zealand had theirs. (Accounts differ on how many clubs were in the room; the figure is not worth pretending to.) In **1892** it renamed itself the **New South Wales Rugby Union**.

Who played? The Dictionary of Biography is, again, the most honest witness available, because it was not compiled to make a point about rugby:

- **Percy Brereton Colquhoun**, a solicitor's son, at **Newington College** from 1881 where he captained the rugby and cricket teams; joined the **University Football Club** in 1885; toured New Zealand with New South Wales in 1886 as the side's leading scorer; "by 1887 he was rated as the outstanding three-quarter in Australia"; played for the colony until 1896; admitted as a solicitor in 1891; sat in the Legislative Assembly for Mosman from 1913.
- **Leslie Wade**, a civil engineer's son, educated at All Saints College, Bathurst and **The King's School, Parramatta**; played for New South Wales against Queensland in 1883 and 1888 and against Britain in 1888; later built the Cataract Dam.
- **Adolph Feez**, a Rockhampton merchant's son sent south to **The King's School**; represented Queensland twice in 1885; a Brisbane solicitor, and a founder of the Brisbane Hunt Club and the Queensland Polo Association.

King's Parramatta, Newington, Sydney Grammar, All Saints Bathurst, the University; and afterwards solicitors, barristers, engineers, a Speaker's son, two members of parliament. There is no need to argue from this. It is simply the list.

### Queensland joins, and the code goes north

Queensland came to rugby late, and the manner of its arrival is better documented than most origin stories in this book, because a historian of the code has pinned it exactly. **Peter Horton** dates the official beginning of rugby union in Queensland to **27 May 1882** — and records that the first formal game was played

> "as an addendum, to a game of Melbourne rules football between teams from the Brisbane Football Club and the Wallaroos (**both primarily played the Victorian game**)."

Queensland's first rugby match was an extra fixture tacked onto the end of an Australian rules match, by two clubs who were Australian rules clubs.

Seven years later, Horton writes, Queensland had hosted two international touring sides — a British team, and the **New Zealand Natives**, the private, largely Māori venture whose journey round the world the previous chapter of this book follows — and "Rugby had become the premier football code in Queensland, **usurping the previously dominant Australian football code**."

Seven years, from an addendum to the premier code of a colony. Nothing about the game itself changed in those seven years. What changed was who took it up, and in Brisbane the schools were the ground on which the two codes fought.

On **12 August 1882**, in Sydney, **New South Wales played Queensland** for the first time, winning by 28 points to 4. It was the first intercolonial rugby match in Australia, and it is the fixture from which everything else in this chapter's institutional history flows: the impetus it created produced the **Northern Rugby Union**, incorporated on **2 November 1883**, which in **1892** — the same year as its southern counterpart — became the **Queensland Rugby Union**.

Two colonies, two unions, one game, and a rivalry that predates the country by nineteen years.

### 1888: the first tourists, and how they paid for themselves

In 1888 a British team arrived, the first ever to leave the British Isles. It was not sent by any union. It was assembled by three cricket promoters — **Alfred Shaw**, **Arthur Shrewsbury** and **James Lillywhite** — as a commercial venture, which is why the England chapter treats it as the moment the amateur union first met the market it pretended did not exist.

Two things happened to it in Australia. Its captain, **Robert Seddon**, who had won three England caps the year before, **drowned in a sculling accident on the Hunter River** in New South Wales, and was replaced by **A. E. Stoddart**. And to help cover its costs the party played a number of **Australian Rules** matches in Victoria and South Australia.

There is the whole absurdity of amateurism in one tour: a team of nominal amateurs, organised for profit by cricket professionals, paying its way through the season by playing a rival code in cities where rugby could not draw a crowd.

### Gunsler's Café, 30 March 1892

While the unions were organising the men, the schools organised themselves.

At **Gunsler's Café**, near Circular Quay, on **30 March 1892**, representatives of The King's School, St Ignatius' College, St Joseph's College, All Saints College Bathurst and the North Shore Grammar School met, in the words of their own association's history, "to consider the advisability of concerted action by the Great Public Schools in drawing up regulations in athletic contests." On 12 April, Sydney Grammar School, Newington College and Cooerwull Academy joined them. At a third meeting on 28 April, so did St Patrick's Goulburn, St Stanislaus' Bathurst and The Scots College.

That is the **Athletic Association of the Great Public Schools**, and its rugby competition began in the same year. The game was listed as "Football" and stayed so until 2006.

Note the spread of them — Anglican, Catholic, Presbyterian, Methodist and non-denominational, and, at the time, all but one taking boarders. This is not one church's game and it never was. It is one class's game, held across the denominations, which is precisely the distinction the book keeps making: **the sorting institution is not the faith. It is the fee.**

In **1896** the President of the **New South Wales Rugby Football Union** presented the schools' First Grade trophy, engraved "School Challenge Trophy" — replacing one that The King's School had won four years running from 1892. From 1899 a Combined GPS side played the visiting English teams. The governing body of the colony's rugby and its private-school system were, by the end of the century, formally coupled: the union gave the schools their silverware, and the schools gave the union its players.

And then there is one line in the association's own history which requires no assistance from anybody:

> "Sydney Boy's High School applied for membership in March 1894, but they were not admitted until 14 February, 1906."

*Great Public Schools*, in New South Wales as in England, means private schools. The one state school that asked to join waited **nearly twelve years**.

### The first Tests, in somebody else's colours

On **24 June 1899**, at the Sydney Cricket Ground, a combined New South Wales and Queensland side played a touring British team captained by a London clergyman, the **Rev. Matthew Mullineux**. Australia won, 13–3. It was the first Test match the country ever played, and it remains one of the better afternoons in this chapter.

Mullineux, to his credit, responded by dropping himself. Britain won the remaining three — **11–0** at the Exhibition Ground in Brisbane on 22 July, **11–10** at the SCG on 5 August, **13–0** there again on 12 August — and took the series 3–1.

There was, at the time, no such thing as an Australian jersey, because there was not yet an Australia. Federation was still a year and a half away. By one account, the side played the Tests in New South Wales in **blue**, and the Test in Queensland in **maroon**, with the Australian coat of arms on the chest — wearing, in each city, the colours of the colony that happened to be hosting.

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1899 the game has everything an institution needs. It has two unions, twenty-five years old and sixteen; it has an intercolonial rivalry older than the nation; it has a Test record that opens with a win; it has a school system that produces players in an orderly annual supply and a governing body that hands that system its trophies.

What it does not have is any particular hold on the men who were not at those schools.

That had not yet been tested, because nothing had come along to test it. The colony's best-known athletes were amateurs in the strict sense — solicitors, engineers, public servants with a father in the Speaker's chair — and they were also, not coincidentally, the men who could take a month off to sail to Auckland. Everyone else played, if they played, in the same competitions and under the same laws, and no one had yet offered them a reason not to.

In August 1907 somebody would. He would be the son of a Balmain boatbuilder, educated at a public school in the ordinary sense of the words, and he would be the best footballer in the country.

## Generation 1: Messenger's Wage (1900–1914)

**August 1907. Sydney.**

A twenty-four-year-old centre three-quarter from Balmain plays three games of football against a touring team of New Zealand professionals, and is paid **one hundred and eighty pounds**.

To put the figure where it belongs, imperfectly: when Richard Arnold founded the Wallaroo club he was a public-service clerk on £200 a year, and thought himself well placed. That was forty years earlier and wages had moved, so the comparison will not bear much weight. It does not need to. **Dally Messenger** had just been handed, for three afternoons, a sum of money that a man in a respectable salaried job would recognise as most of a year's work — for doing the thing he was already the best in the country at doing for nothing.

He was expelled from rugby union immediately. He sailed with the New Zealanders for Britain, was the outstanding player of their tour, and came home in April 1908 — in the Australian Dictionary of Biography's lovely phrase — "with £200 in his pocket and well fitted out with new clothes."

That is the whole generation. Everything else is consequence.

---

### The boatshed and the baker's son

Herbert Henry Messenger was born at Balmain on 12 April 1883, the third son of **Charles Amos Messenger**, a boatbuilder from Middlesex. They called him Dally after W. B. Dalley. He went to **Double Bay Public School** and then into the family boatshed, which stood, as the ADB puts it, "in the down-at-heel fishing and ferrying enclave within the otherwise upper-class harbour-side eastern suburbs" — a working pocket inside a rich district, which is a fair description of where he sat in the game as well.

His father and grandfather had been champion scullers. He was a champion canoeist himself between 1899 and 1905, a decent cricketer, a sailor of eighteen-footers. From about 1900 he played rugby union for the Warrigal club, and in 1905 he joined Eastern Suburbs. His tactical philosophy is recorded: *"Just bung the ball out to me, boys, as quickly as you can."*

Now set beside him the man who captained Australia's rugby union team on its first tour of Britain the following year.

**Herbert Michael Moran** was born at Darlington in 1885, the second son of **Michael Moran, an Irish-born baker**. He went to **Darlington Public School** — and then to St Aloysius' College, and briefly to **St Joseph's College, Hunters Hill**, and then to the University of Sydney, where he took his medical degree in 1907. He played for Rose Bay and then for the university. He captained the first Wallabies. He then took his fellowship of the Royal College of Surgeons at Edinburgh, and spent his life as a cancer surgeon and one of the early Australian practitioners of radium therapy.

A boatbuilder's son and a baker's son. Both of them started at a state primary school. This is the point at which the tidy version of Australian rugby — the private-school game against the working-man's game — stops being adequate, and something more exact has to be said.

The difference between Messenger and Moran is not that one was poor and one was rich. It is that a ladder existed, and one of them was standing on it. Moran went from Darlington Public School to a Catholic college to a Great Public School to a university to a profession, and every rung of that ladder had rugby union on it. Messenger left school for his father's boatshed, and no institution came to collect him.

So when somebody finally offered him a wage, there was nothing in his life that had a claim on him strong enough to say no. **Rugby has no class. Institutions do — and the institution that mattered here was the one that had never bothered to turn up.**

### The month everything happened

Through 1907 the New Zealand Rugby Union was dealing with a Wellington postal clerk named Albert Baskerville, who had signed up a squad of professionals to play the northern English game. What matters here is the route. Baskerville's men — the **All Golds** — sailed by way of Sydney, and played three matches there in August 1907 before going on to England.

They arrived in a city that was ready for them. In the same month, the **New South Wales Rugby Football League** was formed. Messenger played against the New Zealanders, took his £180, and left with them.

The men behind the new code were not, on the whole, footballers. **Victor Trumper**, the greatest batsman Australia had yet produced, was a sports-store keeper in George Street in partnership with **J. J. Giltinan**; the Dictionary of Biography records, in a paragraph otherwise about his shops, that "his repute, association with Giltinan and friendship with 'Dally' Messenger were important in the foundation of Rugby League," and that he served as "a fallible, and brief, treasurer of the league." (The body was formed in 1907; the competition first played in 1908, which is the date the ADB gives. Both are true of different things.)

The first premiership season kicked off in 1908. Messenger captained Eastern Suburbs to second place. And in August he sailed for England again, this time with the first **Kangaroos**.

Which meant that in the season of 1908–09 there were **two Australian football teams touring Britain at the same time, playing two versions of the same game — one of them paid, and one of them not.**

### Light blue, with a waratah

The unpaid one had the better time of it.

Fifteen Australian rugby union players reached London late in 1908 and found themselves entered in the Olympic Games. Only Great Britain and Australia had taken up the invitation, so the gold medal came down to a single match against **Cornwall**, the English county champions. Australia won it **32–3**. The English press had taken to calling the tourists the **Wallabies**, and the name stuck to the country's rugby union team permanently.

Two details from that afternoon are worth keeping.

The first is who led them out. Moran, the captain, was injured for much of the tour, as was the vice-captain Freddy Woods, so the man who captained Australia seventeen times on that tour — including in the Olympic final — was **Christopher Hobart McKivat**, the fifth surviving of ten children of an Irish-born farmer at Burrawang, who had learned his football in the senior grade at **Bowen Bros Tannery's team in Orange**. He had been picked for a tour of New Zealand in 1901 and had to turn it down. When he was picked in 1908, his workmates at the Farmers' & Dairymen's Milk Co. clubbed together and gave him **an inscribed gold watch**.

Keep that watch in mind whenever this chapter is tempted to say that Australian rugby union was a private-school game and leave it there. It was, increasingly, and the machinery that made it so is the subject of the chapter. But in 1908 the man holding the Olympic gold medal was a tannery footballer from a country town whose mates at the milk company had to buy him a going-away present.

The second detail is the jersey. Australia won that Olympic gold medal, in the Australian Olympic Committee's own words, wearing not green and gold but "**light-blue jerseys with a waratah on the left breast**."

Nine years earlier, in the first Tests, there had been no Australian jersey because there was no Australia. By 1908 the country was seven years old. Its Olympic champions were still playing in the colours of New South Wales.

There was one more member of that party worth following. **Danny Carroll**, twenty years old and the baby of the side, born in Melbourne, playing for St George while he studied dentistry, scored fifteen tries on the tour and two of them against Cornwall. He went back to North America with the Australian team in 1912 and stayed there, served as a lieutenant in the United States Army in the war, and in 1920 at Antwerp was playing coach of the **American** side that won the Olympic rugby title. He coached the Americans to it again in 1924. He remains the only Australian to have won Olympic gold for two countries — and he did it in a sport his own country had already begun to give away.

### September 1909: fourteen men

The Wallabies came home in 1909 as Olympic champions, and within weeks the amateur game lost fourteen of them at a stroke.

In August 1909 the hotel licensee **James Joynton Smith** put up the money for a series of matches between the returning Wallabies and the Kangaroos — union's Olympic gold medallists against the professionals — with the proceeds going to the South Sydney Hospital, which he founded the following year. It was, on its face, a charity fixture.

**Fourteen of the Wallabies played in it.** McKivat, who was reportedly paid £150, "was expelled with the others from the amateur code."

Fourteen. Including the man who had captained the side to its Olympic gold less than a year earlier.

That is not a drift of players toward money over a decade. It is a single administrative act, in a single season, and it is the same mechanism the Rugby Football Union had used in England in 1895 — the amateur bar working exactly as designed, and taking with it, in this case, fourteen men from a gold-medal touring party — including its Olympic-final captain. The union enforced its own principle and paid for it in the only currency that matters to a football code, which is players.

McKivat went on to captain the second Kangaroos in Britain in 1911–12, playing thirty-two of thirty-six matches. He was a labourer, an engine driver, a storeman and a weigh-clerk; he married a tailoress; he was, by general agreement, "Australia's finest half-back in either Rugby code." He is buried at Botany, in the same cemetery as Messenger.

### The league builds what the union already had

What league did next is the part of this story that the union's version tends to skip, and it is the reason the code has never been dislodged from Sydney since.

It went and got the schools.

In June 1909 a Newcastle miner's son named **Edward Rennix Larkin** resigned from the New South Wales police to become the first full-time secretary of the rugby league. He had been to St Benedict's, Chippendale, and then to **St Joseph's College, Hunters Hill** — one of the schools that had sat down at Gunsler's Café in 1892 to found the Great Public Schools association — and he had played rugby union for Newtown and for Australia against New Zealand in 1903. He was an organiser of real ability, and he was blunt about what he thought he was selling: he believed, the ADB records, in **"honest professionalism as against quasi amateur football."**

In **1913 he persuaded the Marist Brothers' schools to play rugby league.**

There it is again — the mechanism this chapter runs on, working now for the other side. Union had the Great Public Schools and the trophy the union president had given them. League went to the Catholic teaching orders and got the boys who were not at Hunters Hill. Within a few years, in the ADB's summary of Larkin's work, "the code came to be the dominant winter sport in Sydney."

And it built its institutions out of the labour movement, quite openly. The president of South Sydney from 1908 was **Fred Flowers** — a house-painter and plasterer, delegate of the United Painters' Trade Society to the Trades and Labor Council, one of the founders of the Labor Electoral League and the first president of the Political Labor League, which is to say of the New South Wales Labor Party. He was patron of the league from 1910 and the first chairman of its Australian Board of Control in 1924. Larkin's career, the ADB notes, "was an early indication of the link between it and the Labor Party." He was elected to parliament himself.

So by 1913, in two cities, there were two codes of rugby with two complete sets of institutions: a union attached to the Great Public Schools, the universities and the professions; and a league attached to the Catholic schools, the trade unions and the Labor Party. Neither was more Australian than the other. Both were assembled by men who had grown up together and often at the same schools.

**Herbert Moran and Ted Larkin had both been at St Joseph's, Hunters Hill.** One captained the Wallabies. The other made the rugby league.

### What this generation leaves behind

Then the war took a great many of them.

Larkin enlisted, and was killed at Gallipoli in 1915; a tablet to him went up in the Legislative Assembly in November of that year. His death, the ADB observes drily, "helped to counteract accusations that the code was unpatriotic for continuing grade competition during World War I" — because league kept playing, and union did not, and Australia would be arguing about that for a long time afterwards. Victor Trumper was dead of kidney disease by June 1915, aged thirty-seven.

And on the morning of **25 April 1915**, on Baby 700 above Anzac Cove, a major of the 1st Battalion was shot through the head "while kneeling in order to show his men how to take better aim at a Turk."

**Blair Inskip Swannell** was a Buckinghamshire gentleman farmer's son who had gone to sea in his mid-teens, toured Australia with the British team in 1899 and again in 1904, and simply stayed. He played for North Sydney, and in September 1905 he played a Test for Australia in New Zealand — making him one of only two men, with **Tom Richards**, to represent both Britain and Australia. Moran, who played with and against him, wrote that Swannell's "conception of Rugby was one of trained violence."

He also coached the **St Joseph's College** first XV to three consecutive Great Public Schools premierships between 1905 and 1907, and he was secretary of the **Metropolitan Rugby Union from 1909 to 1911** — which is to say that he ran Sydney's amateur game through the precise years it was haemorrhaging players to the professionals.

And here the tidy version of this story fails again, because Swannell was not a reactionary defending a principle he had never examined. He was, in his biographer's words, "a strong advocate for rugby's amateur status" who was "**also critical of the game's failure to meet the reasonable costs incurred by first-grade players**."

That is the whole tragedy of amateurism in one clause, said out loud by the man administering it in the city where it was losing. The costs were real. The union would not meet them. It expelled the men who found somebody who would, and it went on believing that the principle was the thing that mattered. Swannell told Charles Bean, before the landing, that he would "play this game as he had played Rugby football — with his whole heart," and then he did, and was dead before the day was out.

What the generation leaves behind is a settled arrangement and an unsettled question.

The settled arrangement is that Australia now has two rugby codes, permanently, in the same two states, sharing the same cities and often the same families. The union has the schools that produce a reliable annual crop of players and a governing body coupled to them. The league has the crowds.

The unsettled question is Queensland, where the schools have not yet finished choosing. In Sydney the split of 1907 cost union its best player, then fourteen of its Olympic champions, and left it standing. In Brisbane the same split was about to cost it everything, because the Great Public Schools of Queensland were about to do what the Great Public Schools of New South Wales never did.

They were going to change codes.


## Generation 2: The Ten-Year Hibernation (1919–1932)

**1923. London.**

A Queensland Rhodes scholar at New College, Oxford, one of the outstanding inside-backs in the world, is suspended by the Rugby Football Union as a suspected professional. The charge is that he has played rugby league in Queensland.

He is exonerated. The reason given, in the Australian Dictionary of Biography's summary of the case, is one of the most remarkable sentences in the history of amateurism:

> "**he was exonerated when it was shown that there was no Rugby Union available at the time.**"

**Tommy Lawton** had not defected. He had not been bought. He had played the only football that existed in the state he came from — because in Queensland, rugby union had stopped.

---

### How a code dies

It went quickly, and the war did most of it.

In 1919 Lawton, home from the Australian Imperial Force and enrolled at the University of Queensland, played for his state against New South Wales in what the ADB calls "an unsuccessful effort to revive the amateur code in the north **after a wartime hiatus**." It did not work. The Queensland Rugby Union went under that year and did not come back for a decade.

And in 1920 the Great Public Schools of Queensland — Brisbane Grammar, Nudgee, Churchie and the rest — switched codes. The association's own centenary history says it without drama, in a paragraph about when Churchie first entered a team: "At the time, **rugby league was played, and it was not until 1928 that the GPS schools played rugby union**."

Eight years. In the state that had contested the very first intercolonial, the private schools that had taken up rugby against the Victorian code in the 1880s spent most of the 1920s playing the professional code, and the amateur game had no organisation at all.

It is worth being careful about what this proves, because the tidy version is available and it is wrong. The schools did not kill Queensland rugby union; the union went under first, in 1919, and the schools followed it in 1920. What the decade actually demonstrates is something narrower and stranger:

**The union could die without the schools. It could not be brought back without them.**

That is the whole thesis of this book stated as a mechanism rather than an argument. A football code is not held up by the people who love it or the rules it is played under. It is held up by institutions that deliver boys to it every year in an orderly fashion, and when those institutions turn their attention elsewhere, the code does not decline. It stops.

### Meanwhile, one state carries a country

For those ten years, Australian rugby union was New South Wales.

Not metaphorically — administratively. There was no functioning national body worth the name and no second state to play against, so the New South Wales union simply *was* the game, and the side it sent abroad was, for practical purposes, Australia. In 1921 a young Sydney Grammar man named **Johnny Wallace** pulled on what the ADB calls "the waratah-crested jersey" for a New South Wales tour of New Zealand.

Wallace is a useful man to follow, because he is the Gen 0 pattern still running at full efficiency twenty years on. A storekeeper's son from Macksville; Sydney Grammar School, where he played rugby, rowed in three successive winning Head of the River eights and was senior prefect in 1919; St Andrew's College and the University of Sydney; then a **Rhodes scholarship** in 1922, New College, Oxford, four rugby blues, and the Bar at Lincoln's Inn.

And, because he was "of Caledonian stock" and happened to be at Oxford, something delightful: Wallace was picked, along with **Ian Smith from Melbourne** and two other Oxford friends, to play for **Scotland**. He won nine caps, was on the winning side in seven of them, and the Oxford three-quarter line was a principal strength of Scotland's **1925 Grand Slam**.

The Scotland chapter of this book tells that Grand Slam as the summit of Murrayfield's golden decade. It is worth adding, from this side of the world, that two of its three-quarters were Australian-born — one of them a Sydney Grammar boy — and that he would captain Australia's next touring team.

Because in 1927 Wallace did exactly that. The side that left for Britain, France and Canada that year was called **the Waratahs**, and it was a New South Wales team, because there was not much else to pick from. It toured for eight months. It beat **Ireland, Wales and France**, and lost to **Scotland and England**. Wallace's teams played, in the ADB's description, "exciting, open football with forwards and backs linking efficiently."

**In 1986, nearly sixty years later, Australia retrospectively decided that the Waratahs had been a national side all along, and capped them.** A country reached back and annexed the only team its largest state had been able to field while the game was dead in its second-largest.

The Waratahs carried a five-eighth from Brisbane Grammar who could not play for Queensland because Queensland had no union — Lawton, by then out of Oxford and playing for Western Suburbs in Sydney. They also carried a twenty-one-year-old centre from Randwick named **Cyril Towers**, who had learned to kick playing junior **Australian rules** in Melbourne, gone to a state intermediate high school and then to Christian Brothers', Waverley, and who kept a diary of the tour. It records, among other things, a day's duck-shooting at Sandringham with **King George V**. It was published in a banking magazine when he got home.

Towers went on to play 233 first-grade games for Randwick and eighty-two for New South Wales, scoring forty-nine tries — a state record that outlived him. He and his friend Wally Meagher had been taught one doctrine at school and never abandoned it: **run at all times, never kick.**

### 1928, and the men who went and got the schools back

The revival came from two directions at once, and both were institutional.

In **1928** the Queensland Great Public Schools returned to rugby union. In the same year, according to his ADB entry, **Thomas Welsby** "helped to revive the code," and in 1929 the Queensland Rugby Union was reconstituted with Welsby as its president — an office he held until 1939. He donated a cup, which still carries his name.

Welsby is the closing argument of this generation and it would be hard to invent him. He was born at Ipswich in 1858, the son of a carpenter who became a contractor. He went to Ipswich Boys' Grammar, joined the Bank of New South Wales at fifteen, became a public accountant, joined the Brisbane Stock Exchange, directed companies, and sailed. And in **August 1882 he was manager and half-back for Queensland's first intercolonial rugby union team, in Sydney** — the match this chapter opened its institutional history with, two generations ago.

**The same man played in Queensland's first representative rugby match and, forty-six years later, brought the code back from the dead.**

Lawton came home too — to Brisbane, probably in 1929 — and "greatly assisted the revival of the Queensland Rugby Union organization." He had been suspended in 1923 for playing the only football his state had. He was now rebuilding the thing whose absence had been his defence.

And in that same year, 1929, he captained Australia against New Zealand and won **all three Tests**. The ADB, writing decades later, called it "a feat still unsurpassed."

Read those two facts in the order they happened. Queensland's union came back in 1928 and 1929; and in 1929 an Australian side captained by a Queenslander beat the All Blacks three times out of three. The country had spent a decade playing with one arm.

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1932 the shape of Australian rugby for the rest of the century is set, and it is not the shape anyone would have chosen.

Union has survived, and it has survived because two state unions and a network of private schools in two cities were willing to carry it. That is a narrow base, and it has now been demonstrated to be a fragile one: everybody has watched what happened in Queensland when the schools looked away for eight years. The lesson union took from the 1920s was not *we must broaden*. It was *we must never lose the schools again* — and it never did.

League, meanwhile, has spent the decade doing what Larkin started: taking the Catholic schools, the working suburbs and the crowds, in both states, without interruption.

There is still no national governing body. Australia has been playing Test rugby for thirty-three years, has won an Olympic gold medal, has beaten the All Blacks three times in a season and has had a touring side retrospectively declared national — and there is no Australian Rugby Union. New South Wales and Queensland run the game between them, as they have since 1874 and 1883.

That will not be fixed until 1949, by which time another war will have come and gone, and another generation of Wallabies will have sailed for Britain and never played a match.


## Generation 3: The Union Forms (1933–1961)

**3 September 1939. Torquay, Devon.**

The Australian rugby union team is filling sandbags.

They had docked at Plymouth the previous day, at the end of a voyage from Sydney, to begin a tour of Britain. Germany invaded Poland while they were at sea. Britain declared war the morning after they berthed, and the tour — thirty-odd matches, months of it, the thing they had spent a year preparing for — simply ceased to exist before a ball was kicked.

They stayed a fortnight and made themselves useful. On 15 September they were presented to King George VI and Queen Elizabeth, and then they got back on a ship. The voyage home was worse than the voyage out; the vessel zigzagged to throw off submarines.

Somebody — the record does not say who — arranged a match in **Bombay** on the way, against a scratch local side. The Wallabies won it 21–0.

They had crossed the world and back to play one game of football, and they had played it in India, and the reason they played it at all was so that the men who had been picked to represent Australia could put the jersey on once before they went home to enlist.

---

### The team that never played

The 1939 Wallabies are the strangest of all Australian touring sides, and they matter to this chapter for a reason beyond pathos: they are the last full expression of the old arrangement.

Look at who was on that boat. **Bill McLean**, a twenty-one-year-old engineering draughtsman from Ipswich, son of a refreshment-room manager who had himself played for Australia in 1904–05. He had gone to **Norman Park State School**, then to **Brisbane State High**, then to **St Laurence's College** — which is to say he was a Brisbane schoolboy in precisely the years the Queensland Great Public Schools were changing back to rugby union. He had played **rugby league for Queensland State Schools** in 1931 and 1932 and junior rugby union in 1933 and 1934. He came into the Queensland side through the **YMCA team** and then the **GPS Old Boys' club**.

One boy, both codes, a state school and a Catholic college, a works team and an old boys' club. That is Australian football in one biography, and it is a useful corrective to hold in mind through everything that follows.

McLean went to the war, became a commando, and came back. "In uniform or not," said Trevor Allan, who played against him for New South Wales and later beside him for Australia, "he still looked every inch a commando."

### The captain who never played either

In 1946 McLean captained Australia's first postwar tour, to New Zealand. They lost all three Tests, which was to be expected of a side assembled from men who had spent six years doing something else.

The following year he took the Wallabies to Britain, Ireland, France and North America — the tour the 1939 side never had. With the manager **Arnold Tancred**, he ran the team harder than Australian sides had been run before: ninety-minute sessions without a break, and a **scrum machine**, which was then a novelty.

On **2 October 1947**, at Twickenham, playing against Combined Services, McLean was hit by three tacklers at once and broke his leg — a spiral fracture of the tibia and fibula, and spectators high in the stand said afterwards they had heard the bones go. There were no substitutes. He refused to leave.

> **"I'm the captain of Australia. I'm staying! If somebody runs by, I'll get him."**

Australia won three of the five Tests on that tour. Their captain played in none of them.

There is a temptation to make McLean's leg into a metaphor for Australian rugby union in this period and it should probably be resisted, except that the fit is so exact. Here is a code that has prepared properly, travelled a very long way, and is good enough — and which keeps being stopped by things that have nothing to do with how well it plays.

### 1949: a country acquires a governing body

For seventy-five years, Australian rugby union had been run by New South Wales and Queensland, separately, with the occasional joint selection committee when somebody had to be sent abroad. There had never been a national organisation. The 1908 Olympic champions, the 1927–28 Waratahs, the 1939 sandbag-fillers — none of them had been picked by an Australian body, because none existed.

In **1949** one finally did. The **Australian Rugby Football Union** came into being that year; among its first officers was **Tom Blue**, a Queensland delegate from 1949 to 1952 and the union's deputy chairman in 1949 and 1951.

The reason it happened when it did is instructive, if the standard account is right: the state unions had agreed in 1947 that the game needed one administrative body, and the decisive push is said to have come in 1948, when the International Board invited **Australia** — rather than a New South Wales representative — to take a seat. On that telling, the country built a national union because somebody offered it a chair and there was nobody entitled to sit in it. It is a story worth flagging as the version the game tells about itself, since the governing body's own account of it is the only one available.

Either way, the sequence is the point. **England had a union in 1871, New Zealand in 1892, and Australia — a Test nation since 1899, Olympic champion in 1908 — in 1949.** For half a century the country had been represented internationally by an arrangement rather than an institution.

And then, in the same year, it won something. On **3 September 1949**, Australia beat New Zealand **11–6**, and took the Bledisloe Cup on New Zealand soil. Twelve hours later, half a world away, New Zealand's first-choice side lost the third Test in Johannesburg 9–3; the All Blacks played six Tests that year and lost all six. It was the worst day in New Zealand's rugby history and one of the better ones in Australia's, and the two facts are the same fact seen from opposite ends — as the New Zealand chapter of this book tells it from the other side.

Australia had first taken the Bledisloe in **1934**, off a win and a draw, with Cyril Towers in the centre. But 1949 was the first time it did so as a country with a union of its own.

### Meanwhile, the other code is enormous

While union was assembling its committee, rugby league in Sydney and Brisbane was doing what it had been doing since Larkin: filling grounds.

Its emblematic figure is **Clive Churchill**, and his path is worth setting beside Bill McLean's, because they are almost exactly contemporary and almost exactly opposite.

Churchill was born at Merewether, outside Newcastle, in 1927, the son of a clerk. He went to **St Joseph's Convent** and then to the **Marist Brothers' High School at Hamilton** — one of the Marist schools that Ted Larkin had persuaded to play rugby league in 1913, delivering, thirty-four years later, exactly what Larkin had built the pipeline for. He thought for a while that he had a vocation as a Marist Brother. He failed his Leaving certificate and became a **tyre moulder**. As a schoolboy he played without boots.

In 1947 South Sydney's patron signed him for **£12 10s. a match**. The club he joined was, in his biographer's description, "**pride of the League**" — winner of the first premiership in 1908 and ten more since, "embedded in a red-blooded working-class district, based on Redfern, with passionate and widespread supporters."

He was five foot eight and ten and a half stone. He captained Australia to an Ashes win in July 1954, played thirty-seven Tests, and later coached South Sydney to four premierships. In 1982 he was seriously hurt when his liquor shop was robbed. He died of cancer in 1985, three months after a testimonial dinner raised fifty thousand dollars for him. There is a stand named after him at the Sydney Cricket Ground, and the medal for the best player in the grand final carries his name.

Set the two careers side by side and the institutional argument makes itself. McLean: state school, Catholic college, GPS Old Boys, engineering draughtsman, unpaid, captain of Australia, broken leg at Twickenham, publican. Churchill: convent school, Marist Brothers, tyre moulder, twelve pounds ten a match, captain of Australia, four premierships as a coach, a stand at the SCG.

Neither man was born into the class the other's code is supposed to belong to. They were sorted by which school got them, and by which code was standing there afterwards with a contract.

### The one who did not come back

And then there is the third path, which this chapter has not shown yet and which will matter enormously later.

**Brian Bevan** was born at Waverley in 1924, a printer's son. He went to **Bondi Public** and then to **Randwick Boys' Intermediate High School** — Cyril Towers's school — and he played **rugby union**, well enough to represent the New South Wales Schoolboys at it in 1937 and 1938. He left at fifteen to become a compositor, played league for Eastern Suburbs, and in May 1942 joined the Royal Australian Naval Reserve as a stoker.

In 1945 his ship, the cruiser *Australia*, was sent to **Plymouth** to be refitted — the same port where the 1939 Wallabies had docked into a war. An expatriate league player named Bill Shankland got him a trial at **Warrington**.

He stayed for seventeen years. He played **620 matches for Warrington and scored 740 tries**. In all he scored **796 tries in first-class British rugby league, which is a world record**, and on 14 August 1948 against Wigan he scored what English league still calls the try of the century, zig-zagging a hundred and twenty-five yards from behind his own line. He went bald early, lost several teeth, and played with his knees wrapped in thick bandages. Afterwards he was a Ministry of Defence policeman in southern England. He died at Southport in 1991, and there is a statue of him at Warrington's ground.

The greatest try-scorer in the history of rugby league was an Australian who learned the game at a Sydney state school, played union for his state's schoolboys, and spent his entire senior career in Lancashire because a war sent his ship to the right port.

### What this generation leaves behind

By the end of the 1950s Australian rugby union has, for the first time, all the equipment of a national sport: a governing body, a Test record against everybody worth playing, a Bledisloe Cup in the cabinet, and a schools system in two states delivering players on schedule.

What it does not have is depth, and it is about to find out what that costs. There are two states. There is no third. The best players who are not caught by the schools go to league, which pays, and a few — like Bevan — go further than that and never return. The union's answer to all of this remains what it has been since 1908: the amateur principle, enforced.

For the moment nobody is asking hard questions about what that principle is for.

That changes in 1962, when a young Queensland winger becomes one of the first Aboriginal men to play rugby union for Australia, is picked for a tour of South Africa, and is told what he will have to agree to be in order to go.


## Generation 4: The Tour and the Ceiling (1962–1978)

**26 May 1962. The Exhibition Ground, Brisbane.**

A twenty-two-year-old law student from Eidsvold wins his first cap on the wing for Australia against New Zealand. He has played three matches for Queensland. He is one of the earliest Aboriginal men to play rugby union for his country.

He will play one more Test, at the Sydney Cricket Ground nine days later. Australia will lose both. And then, at twenty-three, in possession of a Test career that has barely begun, he will stop — because of the terms on which he is invited to continue.

Nine years later this same ground will be ringed by police under a state of emergency, and rugby union will be the most divisive subject in Australia.

---

### The boy Churchie sent

**Lloyd Clive McDermott** was born at Eidsvold in Queensland on 11 November 1939, a Mununjali and Waka Waka man and, as the ABC put it in his obituary, the son of a farm labourer.

He was fast. Fast enough that it got him a scholarship to the **Anglican Church Grammar School** in Brisbane — Churchie — which is a name this chapter has used before. Churchie was one of the Queensland Great Public Schools that spent the 1920s playing rugby league, and one of the schools whose return to rugby union at the end of that decade brought the Queensland Rugby Union back from the dead.

At Churchie he won the open 100 yards and the 220 yards in 1957, making him the fastest schoolboy sprinter in the Queensland GPS, and he played three seasons in the First XV. He went on to read law at the University of Queensland, where the university club took him as a wing. He played for Queensland against the touring Fijians in 1961. Three state games later he was a Wallaby.

Follow the machinery. A farm labourer's son from a country town in Queensland reaches the Australian rugby union team by way of a scholarship to a private Anglican school in Brisbane, its athletics carnival, its First XV, a university and a university club. That is the pipeline this chapter has been describing since Gunsler's Café in 1892, working exactly as designed, and delivering a player it would never have found otherwise.

Then, in 1963, Australia was to tour South Africa.

Under apartheid, McDermott could only be selected on one condition: that he tour as an **honorary white**. Not as himself. The classification was the price of the seat on the plane.

He made himself unavailable, and never played international rugby again.

There is a version of this story in which the institution is simply the villain, and it is not quite right. Churchie found him and taught him and gave him the game. The University of Queensland gave him the law. What the institution did was deliver him to a fixture list that had a condition attached to it — and then decline, along with almost everybody else in Australian sport in 1963, to regard that condition as its own problem.

McDermott went on to work in the Commonwealth Deputy Crown Solicitor's Office and, in 1972, became **the first Indigenous Australian admitted as a barrister**. He later founded the Lloyd McDermott Rugby Development Team, whose Indigenous sevens tournament is named for the Ella brothers. He died at his home in Sydney in April 2019, aged seventy-nine.

He had made his stand eight years before the argument reached everybody else.

### 1971: the tour that split the country

The Springboks came in June 1971 and stayed until August.

They arrived into a country that, on the numbers, was overwhelmingly happy to have them. Before the tour began, according to the Global Nonviolent Action Database's account of the campaign, **only 7 per cent of Australians opposed maintaining sporting ties with South Africa**. The tour had the full support of the Australian government. Planning against it had begun in 1969, the moment it was announced, and its organisers were realistic about their chances.

What followed was not really about rugby, and everyone involved knew it — which is why it belongs in this chapter, because rugby union was the thing the argument was conducted through.

The trade unions moved first, and with precision. The ACTU banned the servicing of aircraft carrying the Springboks. Union members refused to supply liquor to hotels housing them. In Adelaide the party was locked out of normal accommodation altogether and slept in a street-level motel behind mattresses stacked against the plate glass, while things came through the windows all night.

And the factory that made police batons declined to make any for the duration of the tour.

As one union official put it: if capitalists could choose where to invest their money, unions could certainly express their feelings by choosing where to invest their labour.

In Queensland, Premier **Joh Bjelke-Petersen** declared a **state of emergency**. The Brisbane Test was moved from Ballymore, whose open surroundings made it impossible to police, to the Exhibition Ground, which had a brick wall around it. Country police were brought into the city. Outside the **Tower Mill Hotel** on Wickham Terrace, where the South Africans were staying, advancing police drove the crowd backwards down a slope; bodies tumbled and arrests were made among the bloodied. A young university student named **Peter Beattie**, who would later be premier of Queensland, was beaten and arrested.

Over seven hundred people were arrested during the tour.

### From inside the team

The Wallabies of 1971 have talked about it since, and their testimony is the part of this story that a purely political account misses.

**Anthony Abrahams**, a lock, had toured South Africa with Australia in 1969 and had spent that tour going out at night to meet anti-apartheid groups — until, as his room-mate Geoff Shaw remembered it, "there was the strong hint that if he went to another meeting he'd be locked up." In 1971 Abrahams declared himself unavailable for selection. He was one of **seven Australian players** who withdrew and joined the protest, and their withdrawal mattered out of all proportion to the numbers, because until then a great many Australians had held that sport and politics were separate things and that the players themselves would say so.

**Geoff Shaw**, a centre from Kiama who had been the youngest Wallaby on the 1969 tour, played. He has described what that cost:

> "That tour split communities and families. I got a few letters dropped on the doorstep in Kiama saying 'you'll be shot' if you play. It split my own family. My older sister took the view I had a right to choose to play and my younger sister said it was wrong and I shouldn't. My mum was really lovely. She was a high school teacher. **Her logic was simple — I had the right to play just as others had the right to throw stones at me.**"

Both sisters are in this chapter and neither is a villain. That is the shape of 1971 in Australia, and any telling that flattens it into good and bad people is describing something that did not happen.

Shaw also remembered the day before the first Test at the Sydney Cricket Ground, when the players watched police laying out on trestle tables the things they expected to be thrown: smoke bombs, lengths of chain with nails through them, firecrackers, ballbearings. And he remembered asking why the protesters had been let into the ground at all.

> "One country rugby official told me they were all paying a dollar and **rugby needed the money**."

There is the amateur game in one sentence. It sold tickets to the people who had come to stop it, because it could not afford not to.

The Springboks won all three Tests — 19–11, 14–6 and 18–6, and by nine tries to two in an era when a try was worth three points. At the Exhibition Ground, behind Bjelke-Petersen's emergency orders, the match was played in what one witness called an eerie calm.

### What the tour actually changed

It did not stop the tour. It changed the country.

By the end of the winter, more than a third of Australians opposed sporting ties with South Africa — up from seven per cent. Attendances had fallen and the tour lost money. And the South African cricket team, due to follow the Springboks, did not come: the Australian Cricket Board cancelled the tour, and — this is the part that mattered — it explained the decision on the grounds of race rather than on the grounds of cost or public order, which it could easily have chosen instead.

No South African national side toured Australia again until apartheid was over.

Ten years later New Zealand would go through its own version of this, and go through it far more violently, and the New Zealand chapter of this book tells that story at length. It is worth recording here that Australia had the rehearsal in 1971, and that a Queensland premier used emergency powers to get a rugby match played, and that seven Wallabies had already refused to be part of it — eight years after a wing from Eidsvold had refused something similar on his own.

### Meanwhile, the ceiling

Underneath the politics, the ordinary problem of this generation was that union could not keep people.

The amateur principle was intact and enforced, and it meant what it had always meant: a good player with no money and no profession behind him had somewhere else to go. Rugby league's outstanding figure in these years was **Ken Irvine**, a butcher's son from Cammeray who went to the **Marist Brothers' Boys' School at Mosman**, worked briefly as a beer plumber, and was spotted at a schools carnival by a North Sydney official. He scored around three hundred tries in about three hundred and forty games, and thirty-three in thirty-one Tests. At Dubbo in 1963 he ran a hundred yards in 9.3 seconds, a world professional sprint record.

Marist Brothers, Mosman. Note the school, because it is the third time. Ted Larkin persuaded the Marist Brothers' schools to play rugby league in 1913. Clive Churchill came out of Marist Brothers, Hamilton, in the 1940s. Ken Irvine came out of Marist Brothers, Mosman, in the 1950s. One decision by one administrator, still producing Australian internationals two generations later.

That is what an institution is, and it is why this chapter keeps returning to schools rather than to classes. Union had the Great Public Schools. League had the Catholic teaching orders. Both pipelines ran for decades without anybody having to argue about it again.

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1978 Australian rugby union has been forced to answer, in public, what it is for — and has come out of it better than it might have. It went on the 1971 tour and it was split by it; some of its players refused, and the country moved a long way in one winter.

What it has not solved is the thing underneath. It is still a two-state game with a two-city base, still amateur, still watching its best athletes go to a code that pays them, and still entirely dependent on a school system that decides each year how many players it feels like producing.

And the answer, when it arrives, comes from none of those schools.

It comes from a state high school in south-eastern Sydney, and from three brothers of the same family — an Aboriginal family, from La Perouse — who between them are about to change how Australia plays the game and who is allowed to be seen playing it.


## Generation 5: The Ella Brothers, and the Origin (1979–1991)

**8 July 1980. Lang Park, Brisbane.**

Arthur Beetson, born at Roma in western Queensland and by then struggling to hold a first-grade place at Parramatta, runs out to captain Queensland against New South Wales.

Almost every good Queenslander of his generation has spent his career in Sydney, because that is where the money is, and under the old rules a man played for the state whose club paid him. Beetson has spent years being one of the New South Welshmen who beat Queensland. Tonight he is not, because somebody has changed the rule.

Queensland win 20–10.

The rule is that a player represents the state where he **came from** — where he was born, or first played senior football — rather than the state that happens to be employing him. It is called State of Origin, and it works immediately.

Here is what makes it worth the opening of a generation in a book about rugby union: **in the same five years, both Australian codes independently arrived at the same question — where is a player actually from? — and both answered it in ways that changed them permanently.**

---

### The fettler's son who asked it

**Ron McAuliffe** was born in Brisbane in 1918 and adopted as a baby by a **fettler** — a man who maintained railway track. He went to **St Joseph's College, Gregory Terrace**, which is the school that produced J. A. Fihelly, the Irish-born Labor minister who helped found rugby league in Queensland two generations before. He worked in the Queensland Railways audit office from 1936, played league for Sandgate and Northern Suburbs, ran professionally as a sprinter, went to the war and was at **Tobruk**, and came home to become a career organiser for the Labor Party.

And before any of the administration, according to his Dictionary of Biography entry, he "had been first associated with the **shift-workers' league** that, under the umbrella of the Brisbane Rugby League, began **Sunday football**."

Readers of the Ireland chapter of this book will recognise that immediately. Limerick's rugby became an inner-city and working-class game and Cork's did not, and the historian Liam O'Callaghan's explanation is not culture or class or founders but the fixture list: Limerick got **Sunday junior fixtures from the late 1880s**, because Saturday was a working day, and Cork had nobody to organise them.

Here is the same variable, sixty years later and eleven thousand miles away, being applied deliberately by a railway clerk in Brisbane so that men on shifts could play. It is the least romantic mechanism in sport and it keeps deciding everything.

McAuliffe chaired the Queensland Rugby League from 1970 to 1985. By 1980, interstate football was dying — a match at Leichhardt Oval that May is said to have drawn barely sixteen hundred people — because everyone knew Queensland could not win a fair fight against a New South Wales side stocked with Queenslanders. So he argued for a series in which men played for where they had started. There was, his biographer records, "much scepticism."

Then Beetson led Queensland out at Lang Park and the scepticism ended in eighty minutes.

### La Perouse, Matraville, and a sportsmaster

Now the other code, asking the same question about the same kind of player, and arriving somewhere quite different.

At La Perouse, on the northern shore of Botany Bay, an Aboriginal family was raising **seven boys and five girls**. Three of the boys — **Mark**, his twin **Glen**, and **Gary** — went to **Matraville High School**.

Matraville was a state high school. It was not a Great Public School; it was not a Catholic college; it had no old boys' club and no trophy presented by a union president. What it had was a sports master named **Geoff Mould**, who coached rugby union.

That is the entire mechanism, and after five generations of this chapter tracking Gunsler's Café and King's Parramatta and Churchie and Nudgee, it is worth stating plainly: **the finest back Australia has ever produced came out of a public school because one teacher there happened to coach the game.** Mark Ella had, by his own account, flirted with rugby league first. Matraville is why he did not go.

Mould's team won the **Waratah Shield** in 1976 and 1977 and went through the brothers' school years unbeaten. In 1977 all three Ellas were picked for the **Australian Schoolboys**, who toured England, Ireland, Wales, Holland, France and Japan and **did not lose a match**. In 1978 all three walked into **Randwick** together, and Mark won five successive premierships there.

The way they played was the thing. It came out of Matraville and out of Randwick's long-standing doctrine — the one Cyril Towers had carried since his own schooldays fifty years earlier, and always credited to Arthur Hennessy: **run at all times, never kick.** Flat alignment, hands, support runners, the ball moving before the defence has finished arriving.

Ella made his Test debut against New Zealand in Sydney in 1980, kicking a field goal in a 13–9 win. He always said his mother fainted when she was told. He was captain of Australia by 1982 and Young Australian of the Year the same year.

### The Grand Slam, and the walk away

In 1984 Australia toured Britain and Ireland under a new coach, **Alan Jones**, who took the captaincy off Ella and gave it to **Andrew Slack**, on the reasoning that the job was getting in the way of Ella's football.

Whatever one makes of that, it worked. Australia beat **England, Ireland, Scotland and Wales** on the same tour — the Grand Slam — and Ella **scored a try in every one of the four Tests**, which no touring player had ever done.

Then, a few months later, aged twenty-five, he retired.

> "Why did I retire? I had better things to do. I never wanted to play forever."

He turned down what were described as huge offers to switch to rugby league, and he never came back to international rugby.

Set that against the opening of this chapter's first generation and the arc closes. In August 1907, **Dally Messenger** — a boatbuilder's son from a public school, with no institution behind him and nothing to hold him — was offered £180 and took it, and rugby union lost the best player in the country. In 1984, **Mark Ella** — an Aboriginal boy from La Perouse, out of a state high school — was offered a great deal more, and did not take it, and rugby union lost him anyway, to nothing at all except the rest of his life.

Seventy-seven years apart, the same code, the same kind of boy, the same offer, opposite answers, identical result on the field.

### 1987, and then 1991

The World Cup that Australia and New Zealand had spent years persuading the northern unions to allow was played in 1987, with Australia's **Sir Nicholas Shehadie** as one of its three joint chairmen. Australia lost the semi-final at **Concord Oval** in Sydney to France, 30–24, in a match the France chapter of this book treats as the finest afternoon in French rugby history, and then lost the third-place play-off to Wales 22–21.

Four years later they went back and won it.

**2 November 1991. Twickenham.** Australia had beaten Ireland in the quarter-final with a last-gasp **Michael Lynagh** try, and New Zealand in the semi-final. **David Campese** had scored six tries and would be player of the tournament.

Lynagh kicked a penalty. Three minutes later, in the 28th minute, **Willie Ofahengaue** took an England lineout five metres from the Australian line and the prop **Tony Daly** went over from the drive. Lynagh converted. It was the only try of the match.

England, who had bludgeoned their way to the final and then chose to run the ball in it — a decision the England chapter of this book examines from the other side, and which has followed them ever since — could not get there. In the seventieth minute Campese stopped what looked like an England try with a **deliberate knock-on**; the penalty cost Australia three points, which was a great deal less than a try and a conversion, and everybody understood the trade.

**Australia 12, England 6.** Nick Farr-Jones lifted the trophy; Bob Dwyer had coached them. Farr-Jones said afterwards that the side had been physically drained by it.

Australia were world champions, ninety-two years after their first Test and forty-two after they had got round to forming a national union.

### What this generation leaves behind

It is the best period Australian rugby union has ever had, and the striking thing is where the people came from.

The World Cup-winning side was coached by a man from a Sydney club scene, and the decade's defining player had come out of a state high school in the suburbs. The other code, in the same years, had solved its own biggest structural problem by inventing a competition based entirely on the idea that where a man began matters more than who is paying him now.

Both codes were, by 1991, better and bigger than they had ever been. Both were about to be bought.

Because within four years, the same two men — one of them the richest media proprietor in Australia, the other the most ambitious in the world — would come for both of them at once, in the same months, with the same chequebooks. Rugby league would be split in half. Rugby union would stop being amateur after a hundred years.

It happened in a single year, and it happened to both games simultaneously, and this chapter tells it as one event, because that is what it was.


## Generation 6: Two Wars in One Year (1992–2003)

**6 February 1995. Sydney.**

The clubs of the Australian Rugby League are in a room together, and a motion is put. Every club is to sign a document called a Loyalty Agreement, binding it to the existing competition. Any club that does not sign will be expelled.

They had already signed something similar in November 1994 — Commitment Agreements, five years apiece, executed in almost identical form within days of one another. The reason for both documents is the same, and everybody in the room knows it, because it has been explained to them. Rupert Murdoch is building a pay-television business and needs a football competition to put on it, and his people are out in the city signing up players.

The Federal Court would later record, dryly, that the club officials "could hardly fail to understand" what they were being asked to do: "to join all other clubs in undertaking not to participate in an unauthorised competition." Somebody at the meeting said the clubs had to **stick together**.

Within two years those agreements would be declared void, the code would split into two competitions playing at the same time, and by the end of it one of the founding clubs of Australian rugby league would be thrown out of the game altogether.

Meanwhile, in the same city, in the same months, the other code was being bought as well.

---

### One year, two codes, four men

The great set-piece of 1995 is usually told twice, in two separate books. It should be told once.

**Rupert Murdoch** needed live football for pay television and set up **Super League** in opposition to the Australian Rugby League, whose rights **Kerry Packer** held. Murdoch's people went out among the players; in Peter FitzSimons's much-quoted account, they signed them "in carparks at midnight."

When Super League began taking rugby union players too, Packer asked for a counter, and a Sydney lawyer named **Geoff Levy** supplied one: a global professional rugby union competition, with a former Wallaby, **Ross Turnbull**, doing the recruiting. That was the **World Rugby Corporation**, and the New Zealand chapter of this book tells what happened to it, because New Zealand is where it very nearly succeeded.

But note where all of this originates. Two Australian media proprietors; a Sydney lawyer; a former Australian international; contracts signed in Australian carparks; and the whole thing litigated through the Australian Federal Court. **The year the world's oldest amateur sport stopped being amateur, the decisive fighting happened in Sydney, and it happened to both codes at once.**

Union's establishment won its war. The three southern unions went to Murdoch themselves, announced **SANZAR** at Ellis Park on 23 June 1995 with a television deal worth five hundred and fifty-five million American dollars, and then signed their players before the World Rugby Corporation could. On **26 August 1995** the International Rugby Board met in Paris and declared the game open. A hundred years almost to the day after the George Hotel.

Australia kept its players and got a professional competition. League was less lucky.

### The war, and what it cost

News Limited took the ARL to the Federal Court, arguing that the Commitment and Loyalty Agreements breached the Trade Practices Act. On **23 February 1996**, Justice Burchett rejected the case.

The Full Federal Court reversed him. It held that the agreements were **exclusionary provisions**, unlawful under section 45, and — since the exclusionary terms could not be severed without changing the contracts fundamentally — that they were **void**. Leave to appeal to the High Court was refused.

So in **1997** Australian rugby league played two separate premierships at the same time: an ARL competition and a Super League competition, in a country of eighteen million people with one strong league market. In **1998** the two sides gave up and merged into the **National Rugby League**, a joint venture between the Australian Rugby League and News Limited.

And the merger came with a condition. The new competition would have **fourteen teams, not seventeen**, and the clubs would be ranked for suitability.

**South Sydney was ranked fifteenth.**

### Redfern goes to court

This chapter has met South Sydney twice already. It was the club whose president from 1908 was **Fred Flowers**, a house-painter, delegate of the United Painters' Trade Society and the first president of the New South Wales Labor Party. It was the club that signed **Clive Churchill**, a Newcastle tyre moulder, for twelve pounds ten a match, and which his biographer described as "pride of the League," "embedded in a red-blooded working-class district, based on Redfern." It had won the first premiership in 1908 and ten more by 1947.

In 1999 it was told it would not be in the national competition the following year, for finishing fifteenth in a list with fourteen places on it, and it went to law.

The record is a straight line and worth setting out, because few sporting disputes have been fought this far. An injunction to let Souths play in 2000 was **dismissed by Justice Hely on 9 December 1999**. At trial, **Justice Finn rejected the claim on 3 November 2000**. On **6 July 2001**, the Full Federal Court — Justices Moore and Merkel, with Justice Heerey dissenting — **overturned Finn** and held the fourteen-team term *was* an exclusionary provision. And in **2003**, the **High Court of Australia reversed that too, four to one.**

The majority held that "purpose" under the Act is a *subjective* test, and that News and the ARL had not subjectively set out to exclude any particular club. Chief Justice Gleeson noted that in a competition of this kind "exclusivity is a necessary feature," and that the fourteen-team term existed "to define the size of the competition."

The dissent was **Justice Michael Kirby**, and it is worth quoting, because it says something about this whole generation that no rugby writer has put better:

> "As is usually the case in such matters, the restricting competitors assert that the best interests of the market or game or participants happen conveniently to coincide with their own best economic interests."

Souths were readmitted in 2002, after a public campaign, before the case had finished running. But the ruling stands, and the point stands with it. A club that had been built by a trade unionist in 1908 and carried by a tyre moulder in 1947 spent the turn of the century arguing, in the highest court in the country, about whether a television company was allowed to decide it did not exist.

### The best team in the world

Union, in these same years, was having the best decade it has ever had — and, for once, was building rather than merely surviving.

The **Super 12** began in 1996 with three Australian teams: the **Reds** in Brisbane, the **Waratahs** in Sydney, and — this is the genuinely new thing — the **Brumbies**, in **Canberra**. For the first time since the Southern Rugby Football Union met at the Oxford Hotel in 1874, the Australian game had a serious third centre. It was in neither of the two rugby states' capitals, and it had no Great Public Schools system feeding it at all.

Then came the trophies.

On **6 November 1999**, at the Millennium Stadium in Cardiff, Australia beat **France 35–12** to win a second World Cup. **Matt Burke** kicked seven penalties; **Ben Tune** and **Owen Finegan** scored the tries; **John Eales** captained the side and **Rod Macqueen** coached it. France, who had produced the greatest afternoon in their history a week earlier by beating New Zealand, had nothing left.

Two years later the **British and Irish Lions** came, and Australia beat them **2–1**: the first time Australia had ever won a series against the Lions. The deciding Test in Sydney was level at 23–23 with twelve minutes to go before Burke kicked two more penalties and the lock **Justin Harrison** stole a lineout to finish it, 29–23.

And in 2003 the World Cup came to Australia. They reached the final at Telstra Stadium and lost it to England, 20–17, in the last seconds of extra time — a match this book has already told from the other end, and which does not need retelling here except to note the symmetry: England had lost a World Cup final to Australia in 1991 by declining to play the game that had got them there, and won one in 2003 by doing exactly what they had always done.

### What this generation leaves behind

Australia ends the period with two World Cups, a Lions series, a home final, three professional teams and a genuine third city.

It also ends it having watched, at close range, what the money actually does. In 1995 two men with television networks reached into both Australian football codes at once. Union came out of it with a professional competition and a decade of trophies. League came out of it with a war, two competitions in one season, a merger, and a founding club fighting for its life in the Federal Court for four years.

Nobody in Australian sport was going to be naive about broadcasters again.

But the thing that would actually squeeze rugby union over the next fifteen years was not a media proprietor. It was arithmetic. Australia is the only country in this book whose union competes with **three** other football codes for the same fourteen-year-olds — league, Australian rules, and now a soccer code with a World Cup behind it — and professionalism had just turned every one of those codes into a career.

The Great Public Schools could still deliver players. What nobody had thought about was who would be bidding for them at the school gate.


## Generation 7: Four Codes, One Market (2004–2019)

**11 August 2017. Sydney.**

The Australian Rugby Union announces that it is discontinuing the licence of the **Western Force**.

Perth had had a professional rugby union team for eleven years. It was the only one outside New South Wales, Queensland, the Australian Capital Territory and Victoria — the only one in the western half of the continent, and the only one in a city with no Great Public Schools rugby system behind it at all. Super Rugby was contracting from eighteen teams to fifteen, South Africa had already given up two, and Australia had to surrender one.

The players' association put out a statement calling it "**the darkest day in the history of Australian rugby**," and accusing the governing body of "**abandoning the game's national footprint**." Thousands of people rallied in Perth. The ARU's chief executive said he would stand aside as soon as a replacement could be found.

Three months later a Senate committee reported on how the decision had been made, and its findings were worse than anything the protesters had alleged.

---

### What the Senate found

The inquiry was established by the West Australian senator **Linda Reynolds** and reported on **15 November 2017**. Two of its findings matter here.

The first is about sequence. The committee found that the decision to cut the Force **had effectively been made on 9 April** — the day before the ARU met Rugby WA, and, in the ABC's summary of the report, "well before it looked into the books of the Force and the Melbourne Rebels." The financial justification, in other words, appears to have followed the decision rather than produced it.

The second is about the money. **Andrew Forrest**, the Western Australian mining magnate, offered **fifty million dollars** to keep the team: underwriting the Force for eight years, **six million dollars for grassroots rugby**, and **twenty million** to compensate the Super Rugby competition for the cost of retaining the franchise. When he met the ARU chairman to finalise it, the offer was knocked back.

Senator Reynolds's own summary of the ARU's conduct was blunt: the body had cut the Force "without appropriate explanation or justification," and "even before the inquiry had commenced hearing evidence, it was clear ARU representatives were **resentful and contemptuous, even dismissive** of this Senate inquiry."

The committee recommended that the corporate regulator review transactions involving the Melbourne Rebels — a club whose owner had sold his share to the Victorian Rugby Union for **one dollar** a week before the axe fell, which is precisely what stopped the ARU from closing the Rebels instead — and investigate the ARU's own annual reports.

This chapter has spent a century and a half describing a sport carried in two states by two school systems. In 1996 it had briefly acquired a third centre, and then a fourth and a fifth. In 2017 it gave one back, and the one it gave back was the furthest from everything the game had ever depended on.

### Four codes, fourteen-year-olds

The reason union was contracting is not complicated, and it is the condition that makes Australia different from every other country in this book.

Everywhere else, rugby union competes with association football, and sometimes with one domestic rival. In Australia it competes with **three** codes that all pay: rugby league, which took the working class in 1908 and never gave it back; Australian rules, which began with a Rugby School old boy's letter in 1858 and owns Victoria, South Australia, Western Australia and Tasmania; and soccer. Every one of them is fully professional. Every one of them is looking at the same fourteen-year-olds in the same two states.

Union's structural answer has never changed since Gunsler's Café in 1892. It has the Great Public Schools, and the schools keep producing players. What professionalism did was put three other buyers at the school gate with money in their hands — and union, which had the smallest domestic competition of the four, was rarely the highest bidder.

### 2019

The other event of this generation that everyone remembers is not about money at all, and it is worth telling with both parties quoted rather than summarised.

In April 2019 the Wallabies' full-back **Israel Folau** posted on Instagram that hell awaited "drunks, homosexuals, adulterers, liars, fornicators, thieves, atheists and idolaters." A three-person panel found a **high-level breach** of the players' code of conduct, and on **17 May 2019** his four-year, four-million-dollar contract was terminated.

Rugby Australia's chief executive, **Raelene Castle**, put the governing body's case:

> "Rugby Australia did not choose to be in this situation, but Rugby Australia's position remains that Israel, through his actions, left us with no choice but to pursue the course of action resulting in today's outcome."

She added that the decision "will change the landscape for sport across Australia and perhaps internationally."

Folau put his:

> "As Australians, we are born with certain rights, including the right to freedom of religion and the right to freedom of expression… Upholding my religious beliefs should not prevent my ability to work or play for my club and country."

He brought proceedings in the Federal Circuit Court. In December 2019 the parties settled confidentially, with a joint statement recording that his commentary had been based on genuinely held religious beliefs, and Rugby Australia apologising for any hurt caused to the Folaus.

There is no version of this in which everybody comes out well, and this book is not going to pretend otherwise. What can be said, and belongs in a chapter about institutions, is that a governing body which had already cut a team in Perth, already been rebuked by a Senate committee, and was already outspent by three other football codes in its own country, spent the last months of the decade in court with its best-known player.

### 1908, and then 2016

And then there is the part of this generation that is not decline at all, and that a chapter organised around men would miss entirely.

Rugby returned to the Olympic Games at **Rio in 2016**, after a ninety-two-year absence, in the sevens format. Australia's women beat **Colombia 53–0** and **Fiji 36–0**, drew **12–12** with the United States to top their group, then beat **Spain 24–0** in the quarter-final and **Canada 17–5** in the semi.

In the final they beat **New Zealand, 24–17**. **Ellia Green** scored her third try of the tournament. It was **the first Olympic gold medal ever awarded in rugby sevens**.

The Australian Olympic Committee's own account of rugby at the Games opens by noting that "Australia achieved early success in rugby union at the Olympic Games in London 1908," when the Wallabies beat Great Britain 32–3 for the country's only gold medal of those Games.

Hold the two together, because they are the same sentence a hundred and eight years apart. **Australia's first Olympic gold medal was won at rugby, in 1908, by fifteen men playing in New South Wales light blue with a waratah on the chest. Its next was won at rugby, in 2016, by women.**

Everything this chapter has described — the schools, the codes, the amateur bar, the two states, the boy who took the money and the boy who did not — happened inside a game that, for its first century, did not seriously admit that half the population played it. The Wallaroos had played their first Test in the 1990s. In 2016 the women's sevens team went out and won the thing the men had last won before the First World War.

### What this generation leaves behind

On the field, the results were respectable and never quite enough: a World Cup final in 2015, lost to a New Zealand side that became the first country to retain the trophy — the New Zealand chapter tells that team from the inside — reached by way of a quarter-final against Scotland settled by a late penalty that the Scots have never stopped talking about, and this book records from their end too.

Off it, the balance sheet was the story. By the end of 2019 Rugby Australia had cut a professional team, been rebuked by a Senate committee, turned down fifty million dollars, settled with its most famous player, and watched three richer codes recruit in its own schoolyards.

There was no fat left anywhere in the system.

Which is a difficult position from which to enter a year in which nobody in the world is allowed to play sport at all.


## Generation 8: The Host (2020–2026)

**September 2023. Lyon.**

Australia lose to Wales by 40 points to 6.

It is the worst performance by an Australian side in the history of the World Cup, and it is not the worst thing that happens to them at that tournament, because they also lose to **Fiji**. They arrive having won nothing in the Rugby Championship, and they leave without reaching the quarter-finals.

**It is the first time Australia have ever failed to get out of their pool.** Not the first since 1987, when they reached the semi-finals; not the first in a generation. The first time in the ten tournaments since the competition Australia helped invent began in 1987.

Two months later the head coach resigned, and Rugby Australia accepted it on 25 November.

That is the bottom. What is interesting about this generation is what the bottom was made of, and how quickly it was climbed out of.

---

### The year nobody played

In March 2020 sport stopped.

For a governing body with no reserves, competing against three richer codes, having just cut a professional team and settled with its most famous player, a season without gate receipts was close to terminal. Rugby Australia went into the pandemic thin and came out of it in debt — including an **eighty-million-dollar loan** taken on in 2023, which tells you how long the shadow lasted.

Everything else in this generation happens against that number.

### Buying a player back

In March 2023, Rugby Australia and the New South Wales Waratahs announced that they had signed a nineteen-year-old outside back from the **Sydney Roosters** named **Joseph Sua'ali'i**. He would leave the National Rugby League in **October 2024** and stay in rugby union "until at least the end of the 2027 Rugby World Cup."

Stop here, because this is the moment the chapter has been walking towards for eight generations.

In **August 1907**, a boatbuilder's son from Balmain was paid £180 to play three games against a touring professional side, and rugby union expelled him, and the code lost the best player in the country and then fourteen of its Olympic champions after him. For the next eighty-eight years union's central institutional commitment was that it would not pay, and its central institutional experience was watching people leave because of it.

In **2024**, rugby union paid a great deal of money to take a player **out** of rugby league, specifically in order to win a home World Cup.

That is not a reversal of fortune. It is a reversal of the entire logic. The amateur bar was built to stop money moving between the codes; it is now union's chequebook doing the moving, in the opposite direction, and nobody thinks it strange.

And there is one more detail, from Rugby Australia's own announcement of the signing:

> "Suaalii made his name in both sports growing up, playing for **the Kings School** along with representing **the GPS 1st XV**."

**The King's School, Parramatta.**

The school that played Newington College in 1870 in what is usually called the first inter-school rugby match in Australia. The school that educated Leslie Wade and Adolph Feez, who played for New South Wales and Queensland in the 1880s. The school whose representatives sat down at Gunsler's Café near Circular Quay on 30 March 1892 to found the Athletic Association of the Great Public Schools, and whose First XV then won the schools' premiership four years running.

The most expensive signing in the history of Australian rugby union came out of the same school as the men who founded the competition, one hundred and fifty-four years later.

### 2025, and the count at the gate

The **British and Irish Lions** came in 2025 and won the series, sealing it in two Tests: 27–19 in the first, and then 29–26 in Melbourne, having come back from eighteen points down, with a contested Hugo Keenan try in the last minute.

Australia lost. Australian rugby, on the other hand, had the best year it has ever had at the turnstile — and one number tells the story of the whole modern game here.

The second Test was played at the **Melbourne Cricket Ground**, in front of **90,307 people**, a record attendance for a Lions match anywhere.

The Melbourne Cricket Ground. The home of the club whose secretary, in July 1858, wrote a letter to *Bell's Life in Victoria* suggesting that cricketers take up a winter game — and thereby set going the code that took Melbourne, Adelaide, Perth and Brisbane away from rugby before rugby in Australia had a governing body at all.

Rugby union sold out Tom Wills's ground.

### 22 April 2026

Rugby Australia announced its accounts, and they were the best in its history.

A **profit of $70.6 million**, against a **loss of $36.8 million** the year before. The remainder of the **$80 million loan** from 2023 paid off, leaving the organisation **debt free**, with **$31.4 million in cash**. It was more than double the previous record, which had followed the 2003 home World Cup, and far above the $19 million that came out of the 2013 Lions tour.

The chief executive, **Phil Waugh**, described it as "a very important year… correcting the balance sheet and setting ourselves up for success into the run to the 2027 home World Cup."

Eight years after a Senate committee found that the governing body had cut a professional team apparently before examining the finances, and had turned down fifty million dollars to keep it, the same governing body was solvent because a touring side from Britain and Ireland had filled the MCG.

### Where things stood in mid-2026

**18 July 2026. Perth.**

Australia beat **Italy 57–10** in front of a sold-out crowd, running in nine tries to two. The lock **Josh Canham** scored three of them. They led 19–0 inside twelve minutes.

It was their first win of the inaugural **Nations Championship**, after losing to **Ireland 33–31** and to **France 42–26**. And it was the last match in charge for **Joe Schmidt**, who had taken the job in the wake of the 2023 World Cup and finished with **twelve wins from thirty-one Tests** — a record whose best days were a **38–22 win over South Africa at Ellis Park** in August 2025 and a **42–37 win over England at Twickenham** in 2024. He handed over to **Les Kiss**. In November the Wallabies were to resume the Championship at Twickenham, Murrayfield and the Millennium Stadium.

Note the ground. **Perth** — where, nine years earlier, Rugby Australia had cut the only professional team in the western half of the country, and been told by a Senate committee that it had done so without adequate justification and been contemptuous when asked about it. In July 2026 the national side played there in front of a full house.

And note the date. On the same afternoon, at Eden Park in Auckland, New Zealand were beating Ireland 40–21 in the same competition — as the previous chapter of this book records. The two countries that had to talk the northern unions into holding a World Cup at all, playing on the same day, fourteen months out from a tournament of twenty-four teams that Australia will host from **1 October to 13 November 2027**. Australia will also host the **Women's Rugby World Cup in 2029**.

### What this chapter leaves behind

Australia is the control case, and the experiment is worth restating now that it has run its full length.

Two British colonies, in the same corner of the world, at the same time, with the same rulebook posted out from the same country. New Zealand's game was carried by provincial unions and country clubs and men who moved towns for work, and it never acquired a class character at all. Australia's was carried by two associations of private schools in two cities, and it acquired one immediately and has never lost it.

Nothing about the sport did that. The difference is entirely in what was standing there to catch the boys.

And the schools were never the villains of it. They were, repeatedly, the only thing holding the game up. When Queensland's Great Public Schools went to rugby league in 1920 the state's union had already gone under, and it did not come back until they did, in 1928 and 1929. When a farm labourer's son from Eidsvold turned out to be the fastest schoolboy in Queensland, it was Churchie's scholarship that took him to the Wallabies. When Rugby Australia needed a player badly enough in 2023 to buy one out of the other code, the player they bought had been to King's.

What the schools could never do was reach the boys who were not at them. That was the whole of the opening the professionals walked through in 1907, and it has never closed. Dally Messenger went to Double Bay Public School and took the money. Clive Churchill went to the Marist Brothers and took the money. Brian Bevan went to Bondi Public, played union for the New South Wales Schoolboys, and ended up the greatest try-scorer in the history of rugby league, in Lancashire, because a war sent his ship to Plymouth. Ken Irvine went to the Marist Brothers too, because Ted Larkin had gone and got those schools for league in 1913 and they were still delivering sixty years later.

The exceptions are what make the argument, rather than breaking it. Chris McKivat learned the game at a tannery in Orange and captained an Olympic gold medal side. Tom Blue, a Goodyear salesman posted to Dubbo, helped to start a country union there. And three brothers from La Perouse went to a state high school in south-eastern Sydney where a sports master named Geoff Mould happened to coach rugby union — and Mark Ella scored a try in all four Tests of a Grand Slam and then walked away at twenty-five, turning down the money that Messenger had taken seventy-seven years earlier.

Every one of those men was delivered to his code by an institution, and in almost every case the institution was a school, and in a decisive number of cases it was one teacher inside one school. The Queensland schools took a whole state back to rugby union in 1928, and Thomas Welsby and Tommy Lawton rebuilt its union around them. Geoff Mould at Matraville gave it the Ellas. Ted Larkin gave rugby league the Marist Brothers in 1913 and it has never had to ask again.

Rugby has no class. Institutions do — and in Australia the institution was a headmaster, and the country got two national football codes out of the difference.
