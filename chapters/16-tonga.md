# Tonga

## Generation 0: The Sending Church (1826–1923)

**7 August 1831.**

There is a feast the day before, which is how Tonga marks a thing that cannot be undone. On the day itself more than two thousand people come to a chapel to watch one man be baptised. Three of his children are baptised with him: **Sālote**, **David** and **Josiah**.

The missionary **John Thomas** wrote down what he saw when he arrived: "When we got to the chapel, the chief and his three children were ready, **seated on the right hand of the pulpit, all neatly dressed**." 

The man is **Taufa'āhau**, and he will become **George Tupou I**, and he will make the kingdom that is still there in 2026 — the only one left in the Pacific.

Before any of that, he was known for his body.

**Basil Thomson**, a young British civil servant who came over from Fiji and worked in Tonga for ten months, set down what was said of him, in the flat admiring prose of a man who had spent those months listening to Tongans describe their king:

> "His great natural powers were enhanced by **the most careful athletic training**. As he surpassed his fellows in stature and length of limb, so was he their superior in **all sports that demanded skill**. **None was so fleet of foot, none could meet him in a wrestling or boxing match**, none could endure against him in swimming in the surf, nor handle a *tafa'anga* — a fishing canoe — laden with fish in a sea way, as he, none was his match in a fight to the death."

Hold that against the rest of this book. In England the question of who was allowed to be good at football was settled by a regulation about money. In Sydney it was settled by which school a boy's father could pay for. In Tonga, a hundred years before a ball arrived, the measure of the most powerful man in the islands was **how fast he ran, how he wrestled, and how he handled a boat in a sea**.

Nobody had to teach Tonga that the body was worth something. That is the first thing to know about this chapter, and it is the reason the last one is so hard.

There is no rugby in this generation. There is no union, no club, no ball, no fixture. What there is instead is every institution that will carry the game when it comes: a church, a school, a chosen man maintained by the people who chose him, and a kingdom that nobody ever annexed.

Go back five years, to a beach at Hihifo.

---

### The people who were already there

The Wesleyan Methodists did not arrive first, and they did not arrive easily.

The **London Missionary Society** had tried in **1796** and failed. Lawry, the Wesleyans' pioneer, landed in **1822** and left after **fourteen months** when his wife's health broke. It was **1826** before a second Wesleyan party — **John Thomas** and **John Hutchinson** — established themselves at **Hihifo**, on the western end of Tongatapu, and the work began to grow, in the words of the Tongan church historian **Finau Pila 'Ahio**, "in spite of strong resistance from the Tongans."

But when the Wesleyan **Nathaniel Turner** came ashore at Hihifo on **2 November 1827** and moved along the coast to Nuku'alofa, he found something he had not put there.

Nuku'alofa was already Christian. Not because a European had made it so, but because of **four Tahitians** — **Hape**, **Tafeta**, **Borabora** and **Longi**. Hape and Tafeta had "**called at Nuku'alofa on their way to Fiji from Tahiti**," decided the place needed a mission, and stayed. The other two joined them later. Two chiefs, **Aleamotu'a Tupou** and **Ulakai**, backed them.

And what the four Tahitians built, before any Wesleyan reached the town, was **a chapel and a school**.

About three hundred people were meeting regularly for worship. Turner went down the day after he landed, sat in the chapel the Tahitians had put up, counted **nearly two hundred and forty** in the congregation, and — the phrase is 'Ahio's — "was impressed by their success."

Sit with the shape of that, because it is the shape of everything this chapter is about. **Islanders travelling somewhere else stopped at an island that was not theirs, were kept by the people there, and did work on their behalf.** It is 1827. It is a hundred and ninety-nine years before a Tongan forward is kept by a French club and does work on its behalf, and the arrangement is recognisably the same arrangement.

### The school, 1828

In early **1828** Turner and **William Cross** took over the school the Tahitians had built and restarted it.

They began with **eighty pupils**. **Within six months the roll was a hundred and fifty.** The task, in the missionaries' own description, was to "teach both children and adults" to read their own language, and the measure of progress they recorded was that several students could now "spell out words of five to six" letters.

That school is the single most important institution in this chapter and it is already running in 1828 — thirty-eight years before Tupou College, ninety-six before the first Test match, a hundred and ninety-eight before a Tongan side plays a Nations Cup fixture in a stadium in another hemisphere. Every carrier of Tongan rugby that this chapter will meet is a descendant of it.

### The first man sent

The missionaries reached a decision in 1829 that tells you what was going to happen to Tonga for the next two centuries.

They wanted to open Ha'apai. They needed London's approval to send a European. So they sent a Tongan instead: **Pita Vi**, a native of Ha'apai and one of the first people baptised at Nuku'alofa, who is remembered as **the first Tongan preacher and teacher**.

Taufa'āhau came down to Tongatapu in **August 1829** and was, 'Ahio records, **deeply disappointed** — not that a man had been sent, but that the man was Tongan and not European.

Read that twice. **The first Tongan ever sent out to do the work was received at home as the cheaper substitute for an imported one**, and the complaint came from the man who would found the kingdom. That argument has never stopped running. It is running in 2026, in a different currency, about forwards.

### 2 January 1835

Then the direction reversed, and Tonga became the sender.

'Ahio: "Another important result of the revival was **the taking of the gospel to Fiji and Samoa. Tongan evangelists and teachers, like Joel Bulu, Sailosi Fa'one, James Havea, and Paula Vea**, and many others, went to Fiji with the gospel. **The District Meeting on January 2, 1835, decided to send William Cross and David Cargill to Fiji.**"

A meeting. A minute. A resolution to send men across open sea to work on somebody else's islands.

The Fiji chapter opens its own church section with the Wesleyans establishing themselves at Lakeba in 1835, "**arriving via Tonga**" — and here is the room they came from and the date they were dispatched. The Samoa chapter records that Samoans first met Methodism as the ***Lotu Toga***, the **Tongan church**, brought home by a chief in **1828**; and that when John Williams reached Savai'i in 1830 he had already agreed with the Wesleyans **in Tonga** that Samoa would go to the London Missionary Society and Fiji to the Methodists. That handshake is why Fiji is Methodist and Samoa is Congregational, and it was made in Tonga.

**Tonga was exporting trained men, by resolution of a committee, in 1835.** That is eighty-nine years before its first international and a hundred and sixty before the game went open. When people say the Pacific export began with professionalism, this is the sentence to put in front of them.

### Moulton's two schools

In **1866** a Wesleyan minister named **James Egan Moulton** founded **Tupou College**, under the authority of the Free Wesleyan Church of Tonga. It is, in the college's own words, "**the oldest secondary school in the Kingdom of Tonga and the Pacific Islands**," created as "a center for Christian education, academic learning, and leadership formation, part of the broader introduction of Western education and **the Tongan alphabet** to the Kingdom." It sat first at Sia'atoutai and made its final move in **1948** to **Toloa**, in the eastern district of Tongatapu — some **seven hundred and fifty acres**. **King Taufa'āhau Tupou IV**, who reigned from 1967 to 2006, was one of its boys.

Three years before he built it, Moulton had helped build another one.

**Newington College, Sydney, opened in 1863.** Newington's own account of the connection is unambiguous: "**Both educational institutions were established by James Egan Moulton**, a nineteenth century Wesleyan Methodist minister who, **after helping to establish Newington in 1863, sailed to Tonga two years later in order to set up a similar College on the Island.**"

*A similar College.* Now go and read what happened to the first one.

The Australia chapter has Newington opening in 1863 and playing, in **1870**, what is usually called **the first inter-school rugby match in Australia**. And in **1892** it joined the **Athletic Association of the Great Public Schools** — five schools met at Gunsler's Café near Circular Quay on 30 March, and Newington came in with Sydney Grammar and Cooerwull Academy a fortnight later, on **12 April**. That association is the machinery that made Australian rugby union a private-school game and kept it that way for a century, while league took the working class and never gave it back.

**The same man built a school in Sydney that became a pillar of a class system, and a school in Tonga that became a nursery of a national game with no class in it at all.** Same church, same decade, same intention, same model. Two entirely different countries came out of the other end.

The Fiji chapter makes this comparison through an idea — Governor Im Thurn importing the English public school to Nasinu as a deliberate class instrument, and the instrument failing. **Tonga makes it through a man.** And the reason for the difference is not in Moulton and never was. It is in what stood outside each school's gate: in Sydney, an old boys' club that existed to keep a man among his own kind; in Tonga, a village, a congregation and a kin group that were the same body of people.

And in **1882** the crown built its own. **Tonga College**, at 'Atele, was founded by the Tongan government — by **King George Tupou I** with **Rev. Shirley Waldemar Baker** — in the same year the government took responsibility for an education system the mission had until then run almost entirely. *(This rests on Tongan press accounts rather than on the school or the ministry, and is stated as report rather than record.)*

So by the 1880s the two schools that would supply Tongan rugby for the next century were in place, and they were **a church school and a crown school**, facing each other. Not a private school and a state school, as in Sydney. Not a mission school and a colonial school, as at Nasinu in the Fiji chapter. **A Tongan church and a Tongan king**, both building for the same boys.

### The kingdom nobody took

And then the fact that separates this chapter from the two before it.

In **1879**, and again in **1900**, Tonga made treaties with Britain. Neither was a conquest, and the first was not even British-initiated. The reason is on the record, and the place it is on the record is the finest available source for it: the **House of Commons**, winding the arrangement up ninety-one years later.

On **11 May 1970**, moving the Tonga Bill, **Evan Luard** told the House why the treaty had ever existed: "**To safeguard his people against the possibility of annexation by some other power, the King of Tonga voluntarily concluded a treaty of friendship with Britain in 1879.**" It was the treaty of **1900** by which "Tonga was put under British protection" — Britain running its foreign affairs, and not much else.

And then **Bernard Braine**, on what the Bill in front of them actually was:

> "This is an unusual Bill in the sense that it is not an independence Measure, **since Tonga has never been a British colonial dependency**."

Luard put it more flatly still: "This is not an independence Bill. **Tonga is, of course, already an independent State.**"

Understand what that does to this section of the book. The Fiji chapter is a country whose racial division was built by a colonial land policy in 1874 and inherited by a sport that had nothing to do with making it. The Samoa chapter is a country cut in half in 1899 by three imperial governments and handed two different footballs. **Tonga has neither.** No governor drew a line through it. No administration decided who its people were. There is no coloniser in this chapter to carry the argument — which means that whatever Tongan rugby became, its own institutions made it.

That is the hardest version of this book's question, and it is why Tonga is the chapter that closes the section.

### What this generation leaves behind

By the early 1920s Tonga has a church in every village and a school attached to it. It has the oldest secondary college in the Pacific, built on a Sydney model by a man who had built the Sydney one. It has a monarchy that has never been dissolved, a treaty it asked for, and a parliament in London that will one day confirm it was never owned.

It has a hundred years of practice at collectively keeping a chosen man so that he can go and do something, somewhere else, on everybody's behalf.

What it does not have is a game.

That arrives now — and in **1924**, at the far end of a boat journey, a Tongan fifteen will play its first international against a Fijian side breaking its voyage between two matches in Samoa, and will not lose the series.

## Generation 1: Three Tests and a Nought–All (1924–1938)

**Tonga, 1924. The third and last Test.**

Nobody scores.

Not a try, not a penalty, not a drop goal, in eighty minutes of the deciding match of the first international rugby series Tonga has ever played. **Nil–nil.** The series is shared, and the two countries go away from it level, which is exactly the result that guarantees they will do it again.

They did it again in 1926, and 1928, and 1932, and 1934, three matches at a time, alternately in Fiji and in Tonga.

That is Tonga's entry into international rugby: not a defeat by a touring superpower, not a colonial fixture arranged by a governor, but **a shared series against the neighbours, settled by a scoreless draw**, and then a decade of rematches.

Now the awkward part, which this book is not going to smooth over.

---

### Nobody can tell you how the ball got there

Somewhere before that match, rugby arrived in Tonga. **This book cannot tell you how.**

There is no founding scene in the record — no equivalent of the Native Constabulary at Ba in 1884, which the Fiji chapter can date and name, and no equivalent of the Marist Brothers in 1920, which the Samoa chapter can attribute to an order and a year. The accounts that circulate say "sailors and missionaries, in the 1920s." They trace back, when you follow them, to a tour operator's blog post and to summaries of summaries. There is no first match, no first club, no first ball, and no first man.

Even the union cannot help. The **Tonga Rugby Football Union** has an official website and a page headed *History*, and the entire history is a single paragraph that begins with the first Test and gives no founding date at all.

So the honest statement is the empty one: **rugby reached Tonga in the years around the First World War, by means nobody has written down, and the first thing anyone recorded about it is that Tonga was already good enough to draw a series with Fiji.**

And that absence is not a hole in the story. **It is the story.** In Fiji the game arrived in the custody of the colonial police, and you can name the province and the year. In Samoa it arrived with a teaching order, four years before a national team existed. Both of those are arrival stories with an administration in them.

**Tonga did not have an administration.** No governor imported it, no constabulary carried it, no colonial school system distributed it on a timetable, because — as the House of Commons would confirm in 1970 — there was never a British colonial dependency here to do any of that. The game seems simply to have turned up, in a kingdom that already had a hundred years of church schools waiting to catch it, and been absorbed without anybody thinking the moment worth recording.

A country that is not being administered does not generate paperwork about itself. That is the privilege, and this is the bill for it.

### The boat from Suva

What can be dated is the other side of the fixture, because Fiji wrote it down.

In August 1924 a Fijian party of **twenty players** sailed for Samoa and Tonga. The whole trip cost **£160**, raised by public subscription in Fiji, headed by the acting governor of the islands, who put in **£5**. They played Samoa at Apia at seven in the morning on **18 August** — the match around the tree that opens the Fiji chapter's and the Samoa chapter's first generations both — and they played the return in Apia on **19 September**.

Tonga sat between those two dates. The Fijians came south, played three Tests and six other matches, and went home having **won seven, drawn one and lost one** of the nine.

Read the nine and the three together and the shape appears. Against club and district sides Fiji won almost everything. **Against Tonga's Test fifteen they could not win the series.** The Tongans took the first Test — the union's own history says so, in the only sentence it spends on the subject — and the third finished scoreless.

### The decade

For the next ten years the two countries played each other and almost nobody else.

Three-match series, alternating between Fiji and Tonga, in **1926**, **1928**, **1932** and **1934**. In **1932** Tonga also beat Samoa. That is very nearly the complete international record of Tongan rugby before the Second World War: a neighbour, twice a decade, and one win over the other neighbour.

It is worth being clear about what that is and is not. It is not isolation imposed by anybody. There was no fixture list Tonga was excluded from, because in the 1920s and 1930s in that ocean there was no fixture list at all. What Fiji, Samoa and Tonga had was each other and a boat.

But it does mean that when Tongan rugby was forming its habits — how it scrums, what it thinks a forward is for, what it believes a Test match is — it was doing so almost entirely against Fijians, in front of Tongans, with no outside standard to measure against and nobody from the northern hemisphere watching or writing.

The men who did the writing later, when they finally saw it, would reach for the same word every time. **Big.**

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1938 Tonga has a Test record that begins with a win and a scoreless draw, a decade of series with Fiji, one win over Samoa, and a union whose founding nobody has bothered to date.

It has no coach anyone can name, no domestic competition this book can verify, no ground worth the word stadium, and no opponent further away than a night on a boat.

It also has something the two chapters beside it do not: **no outside authority with any say in the matter.** Whatever Tongan rugby was about to become, there was nobody to blame it on.

What happens next is that the ocean fills with warships, the boats stop, and Tonga — a kingdom that had spent a century sending its men out to work on other people's islands — discovers what a world war does to a country whose principal export is young men.

## Generation 2: The Protected State (1939–1969)

**3 September 1939. Nuku'alofa.**

On the day Britain declares war on Germany, so does Tonga.

Not as a possession being informed. Not as a colony whose foreign policy has been decided in London and communicated afterwards. Tonga has a treaty under which Britain conducts its external relations, and Tonga could reasonably have waited to be told what its position was. Instead **Queen Sālote Tupou III** declares war on her own account, goes to her people, and asks for volunteers.

They come. She creates the **Tongan Defence Force**, and makes its colonel-in-chief her own husband, **Prince Tungī Mailefihi**, who is also her Premier.

He dies suddenly in **1941**, and she runs the rest of the war a widow.

Hold on to the arithmetic of that. A kingdom of well under a hundred thousand people, that nobody had conquered, entered the largest war in history voluntarily, on the first day, and put the head of its government into uniform.

Now here is what the war made of Tongan bodies, and it is the sentence this entire chapter has been walking towards.

---

### "Like ducks to water"

In early **1941**, while the Allies were deciding where to put their Pacific bases, **Lieutenant-Colonel John McLeod, MC**, filed his assessment of the material available in Tonga:

> "Found the Tongans keen to make good soldiers, and they **took to drill and manoeuvres like ducks to water**. New Zealanders have a flair for getting the best of Polynesian races, for they know that, as with their own Maoris, **the blood of warriors and gentlemen flows in their native veins**."

The historian **Amanda SullivanLee**, a Tongan writing about the Tongan military, sets out what that praise actually was. Tongans and other Polynesians, she writes, "were touted as being desirable because they were **strong and brave but also submissive**." White officers did not see indigenous soldiers as equal partners; they viewed them "more like **one of the many weapons in the military's advancing arsenals**." Polynesians were **"dangerous and desirable but needing of white direction in order to be useful."**

Read that against the rugby writing in the two chapters beside this one.

In 1924, at Apia, a Fijian tour manager filed the first adjective ever applied to Samoan rugby by an outsider: **"the big, sturdy men of Apia."** the Samoa chapter notes that people were still writing that sentence a hundred and two years later. In 2007, at Nantes, after Fiji had knocked Wales out of a World Cup, the beaten fly half explained them like this: **"Give them space and time and they move the ball well and have an offloading game and put you under pressure."** the Fiji chapter calls it the compliment the rugby world had been paying Fiji since 1939 without ever once letting it cost them anything.

**Physically magnificent. Naturally gifted. Needing somebody else's structure to be useful.**

A British colonel in 1941 and a Welsh fly half in 2007 made the same assessment of the same bodies, sixty-six years and one sport apart. The rugby world did not invent that sentence. **It inherited it, from the people who were recruiting Pacific Islanders long before anyone was recruiting them to play.**

### Fifty men

The Tongan war was not large and it was not symbolic.

**Two platoons — fifty soldiers of the Tongan Defence Force — left Tonga** and went north into the **Solomon Islands**. They fought at **Guadalcanal**, at **New Georgia**, at **Bougainville**. The first group, led by **Lieutenant Henry Taliai** alongside the New Zealander **Lieutenant Benjamin Masefield**, landed on Guadalcanal on **19 April 1943**, and was sent to comb a mountainous, soaking island for the Japanese soldiers still on it.

They served in the **First Commando Fiji Guerillas** — a unit, in SullivanLee's description, "comprised of skilled Fijian, Solomon Islander, Tongan, and New Zealander soldiers."

Stop there for a moment. The Fiji chapter has **eight thousand Fijians** in the Solomon Islands in these same years, where they "gained a reputation for bravery," and builds its entire second half on what that trade in young men became. **The Fijians and the Tongans were in the same campaign, in the same unit, on the same islands.** The two chapters of this section that look most different at the end are, in the middle of the twentieth century, the same story with different numbers.

Malaria, typhus and dengue did as much damage as the Japanese. When the Tongan platoon took its casualties at **Munda Point**, the Speaker of the Tongan Assembly, **Nuku**, addressed them in the language of a country that had chosen to be there:

> "**Theirs was the blood which Tonga shed in the blood poured out by the Allied Nations for Right and Freedom.**"

That is a small kingdom claiming a share in somebody else's victory, by right of the men it sent. It is the oldest transaction in this chapter, in its most serious form: **the community keeps a chosen man so that he can go and act on its behalf, somewhere else** — and when he is killed, the community takes the credit and the grief together.

### Never colonised, and exploited anyway

SullivanLee states the paradox flatly, and it is the hinge of this whole chapter:

> "**Although Tonga was never formally colonized in the way many island nations continue to be, the exploitation and fetishization of Tongan male bodies in the interest of warfare is no less present.**"

This book has spent fifteen chapters showing that institutions produce a sport's character — that England's class line was drawn by a union, South Africa's colour line by a state, Fiji's ethnic line by a land policy and a mission board. The obvious inference, and the wrong one, is that a country with no coloniser escapes.

**Tonga did not escape.** There was no governor here, no administration, no annexation, no imposed education system — and Tongan bodies were assessed, recruited, praised in racial terms and spent overseas anyway. The absence of a coloniser did not prevent the trade. **It only removed the person you could blame it on.**

Everything that happens to Tongan rugby after 1970 is that sentence, with a ball.

### The blank years

And the rugby in this generation?

There is almost none to report, and this book is not going to invent any. Between the last pre-war series with Fiji in 1934 and the end of the 1960s, the verifiable international record of Tongan rugby is close to empty. The boats that had carried three-match series around the Pacific were carrying other things. The men who would have played were in the Solomons, or in the fields, or dead of dengue.

What the country did instead, throughout, was hold on to the thing that made it unusual.

The treaty of 1900 ran on and was amended more than once. Britain conducted Tonga's foreign affairs and very little else. There was no legislative council of settlers, no land alienation, no imported administration deciding who Tongans were. **The kingdom simply continued**, through a world war it had joined by choice, under a queen who reigned for close to half a century and became, by the 1950s, one of the most recognised monarchs in the Commonwealth.

When the rugby record resumes, it resumes in a country that has been running itself the whole time.

### What this generation leaves behind

By the end of the 1960s Tonga has a war record it volunteered for, fifty men who went to the Solomons in somebody else's commando unit, a queen who has outlasted her husband and most of her contemporaries, and a treaty that is beginning to look like an anachronism to everybody including the British.

It has a national game with almost no international history and no institutional record of itself.

And it has a body of opinion, already fixed in writing by 1941, about what Tongan men are physically for.

What happens next is that the treaty ends, the kingdom becomes formally responsible for itself — and three years later, on a Saturday afternoon at Ballymore, Tonga beats Australia.

## Generation 3: Ballymore (1970–1986)

**30 June 1973. Ballymore, Brisbane.**

Two minutes gone, and the Tongan captain has already scored.

He is **Sione Mafi**, a number eight, and the Australians on the field that afternoon will still be describing him half a century later. **Mark Loane**, eighteen years old and picked to play against him, remembered Mafi "running over the top of him and smiling" — and, he said, it was **a smile he'd wake up having nightmares about**.

Eighty minutes of this. The fullback **Valita Ma'ake** crash-tackling anything that comes down his channel. The wing **John Cole** bundled over the sideline more than once by what he called unmatched physicality. And at the end, with the Wallabies spilling the ball on attack, the centre **Tali Kavapalu** picking it up and going **fifty metres** to seal it.

**Tonga 16, Australia 11.** Four tries to two.

Their fly half **Geoff Richardson**, fifty years on, gave the flattest possible account of it: "I remember their scrum being very strong — they used the ball at the back well, and Sione Mafi, who I rated as a world class Number 8, was brilliant on the day. **At the end, they'd scored more points and that usually wins you the game.**"

It is, in the words of a Tongan who would later coach the country, "**the most iconic moment in our rugby history.** People still talk about it in Tonga."

Two things need saying before this becomes a fairy tale.

The first is that it was not a one-off ambush by a side that had never been anywhere. It was **the second Test of a tour**, and Tonga had already lost the first, in Sydney, and lost midweek to NSW Country. They were beaten twice and then they won the one that mattered.

The second is what happened to the men afterwards, which is the actual subject of this chapter.

---

### Three years out of the treaty

Go back to **4 June 1970**, three years and a few weeks before Ballymore.

On that day Tonga "officially withdrew from its protectorship agreement with Great Britain," in the US State Department's phrasing. The **Treaty of Friendship and Protectorship** of 1900 — the one that had "delegated sole authority of Tongan foreign relations to the British Foreign Office, **although it maintained Tongan autonomy**" — was done. Responsibility for external affairs and defence reverted to Nuku'alofa.

The House of Commons had spent an afternoon on it a few weeks earlier, and had been at pains to say that nothing was being granted. *Not an independence Measure. Tonga has never been a British colonial dependency. Tonga is, of course, already an independent State.*

So when the Ikale Tahi walked out at Ballymore in June 1973, they were representing a country that had been formally answerable to nobody for three years — and informally answerable to nobody for a hundred and fifty.

It is worth setting that beside the other two chapters of this section. Fiji reached its first World Cup quarter-final in 1987 **under military rule**, at a tournament that began eight days after a coup. Samoa reached its first in 1991 as a country that had been independent since 1962 but whose union was an affiliate of New Zealand's, playing with air fares raised by two businessmen. **Tonga beat Australia in the third year of full sovereignty, with nobody's permission and nobody's money.**

And then it exported the team.

### What Brisbane did next

Sione Mafi, the lock **Fa'aleo Tupi** and the utility back **Faitai Kefu** liked Brisbane. Some years later they took up offers to go back and play club rugby there, for the **Souths Magpies** — the Brisbane union club, not the Sydney league club this book has followed elsewhere.

Understand what that meant in the 1970s. Here is **Toutai Kefu**, Faitai's son, describing it:

> "It took some bravery for those players to come to Brisbane in the 1970s, basically, **with no education, no money and no English**. **My father lay bitumen on the roads.** There were laboring and factory jobs and Souths helped out."

Permanent residency was the obstacle, and the club had contacts: **Bill Hayden**, who led the Federal Labor Party from 1977 to 1983, was a former Souths player.

Sit with the sequence. **A man beats Australia at Ballymore, and then moves to Australia and lays bitumen on its roads**, while a rugby club works its political connections to keep him in the country. He was not trafficked and he was not deceived. He was a good footballer who took the best offer available to a Tongan in 1970s Brisbane, which was manual labour and a club jersey, and he took it with his eyes open.

That is the Pacific export, twenty years before anyone was paid to play, and it did not require professionalism to exist. It only required a rich country next door.

### The rest of the sentence

Faitai Kefu had sons.

**Toutai Kefu** was born in Tonga and played **sixty Tests for Australia**. He won a **World Cup** with them. He then spent five seasons in Japan with the **Kubota Spears**. His brother **Steve** also played for the Wallabies, and later for Wasps. In **2003**, four of the family — with Fa'aleo Tupi and Sione Mafi alongside them — played together for Souths in a first-grade game in Brisbane.

Rugby Australia's own retrospective puts it without embarrassment: **Kefu "did much to cultivate the Tongan connection that is so strong in Australian rugby today."**

And then the last turn, which is the one that makes this a Tongan story rather than an Australian one. **Toutai Kefu went back and coached Tonga.**

Follow it end to end. A Tongan beats Australia at Ballymore in 1973. He emigrates to Australia and lays bitumen on its roads. His sons play for Australia, and one of them wins a World Cup in Australian colours. That son returns and coaches Tonga, and tells reporters that the most iconic moment in Tongan rugby history is the afternoon his father beat the country he himself played sixty Tests for.

The Fiji chapter argues the Pacific export with numbers — five hundred professionals, eleven per cent of a nation's remittances, the muscle trade. The Samoa chapter argues it with an institution — the village that maintains a chosen man so that he can act on its behalf. **Tonga can argue it with one household, and every step of it is on the record.**

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1986 Tonga is sovereign, has beaten Australia, and has watched three of the men who did it settle in the country they beat.

It has no professional competition, no significant fixture list, no money, and no seat at the table where the sport is run. What it has is a reputation — the same reputation a British colonel wrote down in 1941, restated by Australian internationals in the language of the scrum and the crash tackle. **Big. Strong. Frightening. Brilliant on the day.**

Next year, for the first time, the sport will hold a World Cup, and it will send out invitations.

Tonga will get one. Samoa will not.

## Generation 4: The Invitation (1987–1998)

**1987. The first Rugby World Cup.**

There are sixteen places and no qualifying tournament. The sport does not hold a competition to decide who comes; it writes to people.

**Tonga gets a letter. Samoa does not.**

That is the whole of it. Two neighbouring Pacific states, a couple of hours apart by air, with intertwined churches and a shared century of rugby against each other and Fiji, and one of them is inside the first World Cup and the other is outside it. The Samoa chapter records what that did in Auckland — a generation of Samoan boys with nothing of their own to want, and Pat Lam's flat verdict that "**Samoa was not invited and there was nothing for young Samoans to aspire to.**" Samoa's only role in 1987 was to be put on standby in case Fiji could not travel after the coup.

Tonga was in the tournament. It is worth being honest about what it did there: not much. But it was in the room, and the room was being photographed.

Then, four years later, the invitation stopped coming.

---

### Missing 1991

Tonga did not play at the 1991 World Cup. **Samoa did** — and reached the quarter-finals, beat Wales at Cardiff, and produced the single most consequential fortnight in Samoan rugby history.

The neighbours had swapped places in four years.

There is no scandal in it. Qualification had arrived, Samoa won through and Tonga did not, and that is what qualification is for. But it is worth marking, because the two countries' whole modern relationship is in that swap: **the same ocean, the same church, the same export, and a completely different set of results** — and neither of them has ever had the institutional depth to make a good decade into a good era.

Tonga came back for **1995**, and again for **1999**, where it beat **Italy**. Its captain through both campaigns was a hooker named **Fe'ao Vunipola**, and this chapter is going to follow him out of the country.

### Japan, and a story that is only a story

Somewhere in this generation the other pipeline opens, and it does not run to Britain or France. It runs to Japan.

The story Tongan and Japanese rugby tell about it is very good. In **1976**, it goes, a visiting teacher from **Daito Bunka University**, who managed its rugby team, taught the Japanese abacus to **King Taufa'āhau Tupou IV**; the King was taken with it; a friendship formed; and Tongan boys began going to Daito Bunka on rugby scholarships to study the abacus.

**This book cannot stand that story up.** Followed to its source, it rests on a single self-published rugby blog. The article most often cited for it contains not one of its details — no university, no abacus, no teacher, no king. It may well be true. It is not documented, and it is told here as the story Tongan rugby tells about itself, in the same way the Fiji chapter tells the origin of the *cibi* — as a story rather than as a document.

What is not in dispute is the residue. **From the 1980s onward there were Tongans playing top-level rugby in Japan**, and some of them played *for* Japan. That is the second door out of Tonga, and it opened decades before the game went professional, exactly as the British Army and the peacekeeping contracts opened for Fiji.

By the 1990s the shape is set. A young Tongan forward with a good body has three futures available to him, and **all three are somewhere else**: a Japanese university, an Australian club, or a British one.

### The captain leaves

In **1998**, **Fe'ao Vunipola** — the man who had captained Tonga at a World Cup and would captain them at another — moved to **Wales**.

He signed for **Pontypool**, then for **Pontypridd** in 1999, where he made forty-four appearances in two seasons before moving on to Caerphilly. Pontypridd's own hall of fame remembers him as "a seasoned Tongan international" with "his robust style of play well suited to the club's abrasive mentality." He had already played in New Zealand, for Wellington. He won **thirty-three caps for Tonga between 1988 and 2001**.

He also brought his family.

His sons would grow up in Britain and qualify for **England by residency**. **Mako Vunipola** would win **seventy-nine England caps** and play **nine Tests for the British and Irish Lions**. **Billy Vunipola** would win **seventy-five**, the last of them in 2023. Both had other options — Billy was born in Australia and Mako in New Zealand, and both also qualified for Wales — and both chose England.

And their mother, **the Reverend Iesinga Vunipola**, is a **Methodist minister**, who has served as chaplain to the Tongan community in the United Kingdom.

Stop and look at that household, because this section of the book is called *the church and the export* and here they are in one family. **A Tongan captain exports himself to a Welsh club. His wife ministers to the Tongan diaspora that the export created. Their two sons win a hundred and fifty-four caps for England.**

Nothing about it is a scandal and nobody in it did anything wrong. Fe'ao Vunipola was a professional hooker in the first years of professionalism, taking a contract in the country that offered one. His wife did what Tongan ministers have done since the 1830s — went where her people were and kept them together. His sons grew up in England and played for the country they grew up in.

**Every individual decision is correct, and the sum of them is that Tonga lost a captain and produced two England internationals.**

That is the machine this chapter has been building since 1835, running at full speed, on a family the whole rugby world can name.

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1998 Tonga has been to two World Cups and missed one. It has a pipeline into Japanese universities that nobody can properly document, a pipeline into British clubs that has just taken its captain, and a diaspora large enough to need its own Methodist chaplain.

It has no professional competition, no money, and no mechanism by which a Tongan who becomes very good at rugby can remain a Tongan rugby player.

What arrives next is the open era proper — and with it the discovery that the country's best asset is now legally the property of whoever signs it first.

## Generation 5: Open, and Gone (1999–2011)

**1 October 2011. Wellington Regional Stadium.**

Tonga are beating France, and they have stopped trying to win the tournament.

The arithmetic that morning was cruel and public. To reach the quarter-finals Tonga did not merely need to beat France; they needed to beat them by a distance, take a bonus point for four tries, and deny France one. A win on its own was worth nothing in the table.

**Sukanaivalu Hufanga** scores on the wing. The fly half **Kurt Morath** kicks fourteen points. And somewhere in the second half the thing that had never happened to a Tongan side at a World Cup starts happening: they realise they are going to win.

Here is **Siale Piutau**, who would later captain the country, on what that did:

> "The funny thing is that as we got through that game and started believing that we could win, **we didn't worry about going for the four tries.**"

**Tonga 19, France 14.**

And then Tonga went home, because the win was not enough — and France, beaten by Tonga in the pool, went all the way to **the final**, where they lost to New Zealand by a point.

Read those two facts together, because they are the whole of this generation and very nearly the whole of this chapter. **Tonga beat the team that reached the World Cup final, and did not survive the pool.** And they did it by choosing, in the moment, to win the match rather than chase the tournament.

It is very easy to call that a failure of composure. It is more honest to call it what it is: **the behaviour of a country that has almost never been in front, taking the thing in front of it, because it has learned not to expect another.**

---

### What the open era actually was

Rugby union went professional in August 1995, and for Tonga the change was not that its players started being paid. It was that **somebody else started paying them**.

There was no Tongan professional competition to build. There was no Tongan club with a ground and a broadcast contract. There was a population of about a hundred thousand, a rugby-playing population the union itself would later put at **fewer than eight hundred senior players**, and a supply of large, quick, ferociously physical young men that the entire northern hemisphere had been describing in admiring racial terms since at least 1941.

What followed was not a scandal. It was a market clearing.

By the 2000s a good Tongan forward could be in a French club academy, a Japanese university, an English Premiership squad, a New Zealand province or an Australian one. What he could not be was a professional rugby player **in Tonga**, because no such job existed. And under the eligibility rules of the day, the moment he was capped by the country he had moved to, he was gone for good.

The Fiji chapter calls this a **one-way valve** and gives it in numbers — five hundred professionals abroad, eleven per cent of a nation's remittances. The Samoa chapter gives it as an institution — the village maintaining a chosen man. Tonga's version is the same machine, running on a smaller population, with **no domestic professional structure of any kind** and a diaspora concentrated in exactly the four countries most likely to cap its sons.

### The wins, and who was watching them

What Tonga had instead of a structure was days.

In **1999**, at the World Cup, Tonga beat **Italy 28–25**. Piutau, a boy at the time, remembered it precisely for what it did to boys: "It was massive just in terms of, for a young Tongan kid growing up, and **seeing that these things were possible**."

That is the entire function of a Tier Two national team, stated by someone it worked on. Not trophies. **Evidence that the thing is possible.** the Samoa chapter records the same mechanism from Cardiff in 1991 — Pat Lam's classroom, Kevin Senio at eleven years old realising *we can do that too*. It is the one thing a country with no money can still manufacture, and it costs a single afternoon.

The trouble is that it has to be manufactured again every few years, and Tonga's fixture list was not built to allow it.

**2007** was the closest they came to converting a day into a tournament. In Pool A — England, South Africa, Samoa, the United States and Tonga — the Ikale Tahi beat the **United States**, then beat **Samoa 19–15**, and went to the **South Africa** match second in the pool with a quarter-final genuinely in view for the first time in their history.

They lost that one **30–25**. South Africa won the tournament.

Hold that beside 2011 and the pattern is exact. **Tonga lost by five points to the side that won the 2007 World Cup, and beat the side that reached the 2011 final, and did not make a quarter-final in either year.** Meanwhile in Pool B, Fiji beat Wales at Nantes and went through — the fixture the Fiji chapter calls Fiji's finest hour. **Same ocean, same fortnight, opposite outcomes.**

### The women start

In **2006**, Tonga's women played in the inaugural **Pacific Tri-Nations**, the same tournament in which Fiji's women played their first Test match.

The Fiji chapter records the result from the other side: the Fijiana **beat Tonga 52–5**.

That is where the Tongan women's game begins in the international record — on the wrong end of a fifty-point margin, in a tournament invented because the three countries had nobody else to play. It would remain the least resourced of the three. In the world rankings of July 2026, Fiji's women stood thirteenth, Samoa's fifteenth, and **Tonga's twenty-eighth.**

The men's game in Tonga is short of money. The women's game has been short of the money the men's game does not have.

### What this generation leaves behind

Tonga comes out of 2011 with a win over a World Cup finalist, a defeat of Italy that a generation of Tongan boys remembers, a women's team that has played its first internationals and been beaten by fifty, and not one professional rugby job inside the kingdom.

Its captain of the 1990s had finished his playing career in Wales. Its best forwards are contracted in France, England and Japan. Its most famous sons are qualified for other countries by residency and birth, and the rules say that once they choose, the choice is permanent.

That last sentence is about to stop being true — and in the same decade the players will finally say out loud what the arrangement has been costing them.

## Generation 6: Twentieth (2012–2026)

**4 July 2026. Dick's Sporting Goods Park, Denver, Colorado.**

Tonga are playing Zimbabwe, six and a half thousand miles from Nuku'alofa and nine thousand from Harare, in the first round of the first **World Rugby Nations Cup** — the competition invented to give the countries below the top table somebody to play.

They lead **19–5** at half-time, the fly half **Patrick Pellegrini** kicking. Then Zimbabwe score three tries in nine minutes — **Mavesere**, **Muzanargwo**, **Sigauke** — and with fifteen minutes left Tonga are **behind, 24–19**.

**Moli** goes over on sixty-nine minutes. **Tuipulotu** on seventy-three. **Tonga 36, Zimbabwe 26.**

Now look at where that afternoon sits.

Two weeks later, on **18 July 2026**, Fiji played what its own union had advertised as a **home** fixture — at **Murrayfield**. Samoa played all three of its July "home" matches in **Chile**. And Tonga opened its campaign in **Colorado**.

**Three Pacific countries. One July window. Not one of them played at home.**

That is where the section called *the church and the export* comes out, a hundred and ninety-one years after a District Meeting in Tonga resolved to send men across the sea to work on other islands.

---

### The good days, and what they were worth

This generation opened with the best run of results in modern Tongan rugby.

In **2012**, at Aberdeen, **Tonga beat Scotland 21–15** — the first time they had ever beaten them. Siale Piutau, who was not in the side, remembered "waking up early just to stream it online," and said it was "massive coming off the back of 2011, a great win over France." In **2016** Piutau captained the side that beat **Italy 20–18**, and described what he was actually trying to do: "**to grow the sense of belief back in the team.**"

*Back in* the team. The belief is not a thing Tongan rugby accumulates. It is a thing it keeps having to reconstruct, because the structure that would hold it in place does not exist.

Here is the measure of that, and it is not a Tier 1 scoreline. On **30 August 2024**, at **Apia Park**, Tonga lost to **Samoa 43–17**. It was Samoa's centenary — a hundred years of Samoan Test rugby — and the defeat extended **Samoa's unbeaten home record against Tonga in Apia to twenty matches across forty-four years.**

Tonga can beat France in Wellington and Scotland in Aberdeen. **It has not won in Apia since about 1980.**

That is what having no domestic professional structure does. A team assembled a few times a year from men employed on four continents can produce an extraordinary day against anybody. What it cannot produce is the ordinary competence that wins a scrappy regional fixture, on a hard ground, against the neighbour who knows exactly what is coming.

### The rule, and the boat

Two things arrived in this generation that were genuinely built with Tonga in mind.

In **November 2021**, World Rugby amended **Regulation 8**, with effect from the first day of 2022: a capped player could change union **once**, after a **thirty-six-month stand-down**, if he was born in the new country or had a parent or grandparent born there. For the first time since the game went open, the valve worked both ways.

And in **2022**, **Moana Pasifika** entered **Super Rugby Pacific**, built to give Tongan- and Samoan-eligible players a professional pathway.

It is based in **South Auckland**.

Set that beside the Fiji chapter, where the Fijian Drua — after one COVID season in Australia — plays its rugby **in Fiji**, in front of Fijian crowds, and became the single most important institutional event in that country's rugby history: a professional contract a Fijian could sign **without leaving**. Tonga has no such thing. Tonga's professional pathway is a franchise in another country, shared with Samoa, in the city where the diaspora lives — which is to say that **the pathway is still the export, with a better contract attached.**

### What the union says about itself

The Tonga Rugby Football Union's official website has not been substantially updated since 2011. Its history page is a single paragraph. And in that paragraph, describing its own sport, the union wrote this:

> "Despite a total population of just 100,000 and **a rugby playing population of less than 800 seniors** Tonga is remarkably good at rugby. **Unfortunately rugby is Tonga's main export.**"

Fewer than eight hundred senior players, on the union's own 2011 reckoning. And the word *unfortunately*, chosen by the governing body of the sport, about the sport.

The Fiji chapter found a peer-reviewed journal stating this book's title as settled background. **Tonga's union states this book's section title as a complaint about itself.**

### The sons come back

And then, at the end, both of this chapter's families do the same thing.

**Toutai Kefu** — born in Tonga, sixty Tests for Australia, a World Cup winner in Australian colours, son of the man who beat Australia at Ballymore and then laid bitumen on Australian roads — went back and **coached Tonga**. Asked in 2023 what the 1973 win meant, he said it was "the most iconic moment in our rugby history. People still talk about it in Tonga."

**Mako Vunipola** — seventy-nine caps for England, nine Tests for the Lions, son of the Tongan captain who signed for Pontypool in 1998 — is in talks about joining **Tonga's coaching set-up for the 2027 World Cup**, with his brother **Billy**, seventy-five England caps, a possible addition to the playing squad. Their father **Fe'ao** spent five years as **interim chief executive of the Tonga Rugby Union**.

Two families. Two Tier 1 countries. **Both sets of sons came back.**

The eligibility rule of 2021 made some of that legally possible. But the rule is not what is bringing them. What is bringing them is the thing this chapter began with in the 1820s, when four Tahitians on their way to Fiji stopped at Nuku'alofa and stayed: **a man is kept by a community so that he can act on its behalf, and the obligation runs in both directions, and it does not expire when he gets on a plane.**

### Where things stood, in the middle of 2026

On **15 July 2026**, three days before Fiji played its home match at Murrayfield, the World Rugby men's rankings had **Tonga twentieth in the world**, down a place. **Samoa were nineteenth. Fiji were ninth.**

In the women's rankings the **Fijiana** stood thirteenth, Samoa fifteenth, and **Tonga twenty-eighth**.

Fiji had gone up, into the **Nations Championship**, to play the Six Nations and the Rugby Championship. **Samoa and Tonga had not.** They were in the second tier, in the same pool, playing each other and the Americas.

Ahead lay the November leg of the Nations Cup, and then **Rugby World Cup 2027 in Australia** — the first twenty-four-team tournament — where Tonga were drawn in **Pool F with England, Wales and Zimbabwe.**

England: the country the Vunipola brothers won a hundred and fifty-four caps for. Zimbabwe: the country Tonga had beaten in Denver in July.

### What this chapter leaves behind

Start where the chapter started, with a man's body.

Before there was a ball in these islands, the measure of **Taufa'āhau** — the chief who made the kingdom — was that none was so fleet of foot, none could meet him in a wrestling or boxing match, none could handle a canoe in a sea as he could. A British civil servant wrote that down admiringly. It is 1831, and already the most valuable thing a Tongan man has is what he can do with his body.

Then the institutions arrive, and none of them is imposed.

**Four Tahitians on their way to Fiji stop at Nuku'alofa in the 1820s and build a chapel and a school.** The Wesleyans take the school over in **1828** — eighty pupils, a hundred and fifty within six months. In **1829** the mission sends its first Tongan teacher, **Pita Vi**, to Ha'apai, and the future king complains that they sent a Tongan instead of a European. On **2 January 1835** a District Meeting resolves to send men to Fiji, and Tongan evangelists go, and the church that reaches Lakeba that year — the church at the centre of the Fiji chapter — **comes from here**. Samoans meet Methodism as the *Lotu Toga*, the **Tongan** church.

**Tonga was running an export of trained men, by committee resolution, in 1835.** Not because anybody made it. Because it was good at it.

In **1866** a Wesleyan named **James Egan Moulton** founds **Tupou College**, the oldest secondary school in the Pacific — three years after helping to found **Newington College in Sydney**, and explicitly to build "a similar College." Newington played the first inter-school rugby match in Australia and joined the **Great Public Schools**, the association the Australia chapter shows manufacturing a class-divided game and keeping it that way for a century. The Tongan school produced a nursery of a national game with **no class in it at all**.

Same man. Same church. Same decade. Same model. **Opposite countries came out of the other end** — because what stood outside the Sydney gate was an old boys' club that existed to keep a man among his own kind, and what stood outside the Tongan gate was a village, a congregation and a kin group that were the same body of people.

And nobody ever took the country. In **1879** its king went looking for a protector to prevent somebody else annexing him; in **1900** he signed a Treaty of Friendship and Protectorship; and on **4 June 1970** it ended. When the House of Commons wound the arrangement up, **Bernard Braine** put the fact on the record: *"it is not an independence Measure, since Tonga has never been a British colonial dependency."*

So here is what this chapter is for.

Fiji's ethnic line was drawn by a governor's land policy in 1874 and a mission board in 1901, and rugby lay down inside a shape it did not make. Samoa was cut in half in 1899 by three imperial governments and handed two different footballs, and neither half chose. **Both chapters have somebody to point at.**

**Tonga has nobody.** No governor, no administration, no partition, no annexation, no imposed school system. And the outcome is the same. Tongan bodies were assessed in racial terms by **Lieutenant-Colonel John McLeod** in **1941** — *took to drill and manoeuvres like ducks to water, the blood of warriors and gentlemen* — and, as the Tongan historian **Amanda SullivanLee** writes, "**although Tonga was never formally colonized in the way many island nations continue to be, the exploitation and fetishization of Tongan male bodies in the interest of warfare is no less present.**"

Change one word and you have the rugby. **Strong, brave, submissive; dangerous and desirable; needing white direction to be useful** — that is a colonel siting bases in 1941, and it is also "the big, sturdy men of Apia" in 1924, and it is also *give them space and time and they move the ball well* at Nantes in 2007. **A century of the same compliment, paid by people who never once let it cost them anything.**

So the answer to the question this book has been asking for sixteen chapters is not that colonialism produced the Pacific export. **The export does not need a coloniser.** It needs a small country that is very good at producing strong young men, a large country that wants them, and an institution at home that has been organising exactly that transaction since 1835. Tonga has all three, and never had a governor. **The absence of a coloniser did not prevent any of it. It only removed the person you could blame.**

The rest is a ledger.

Fewer than eight hundred senior players, on the union's own count. No World Cup quarter-final, ever. Twenty matches in Apia across forty-four years without a win. A professional team that plays in South Auckland. A win over France at a World Cup that was not enough to survive the pool, given away because a team that is never in front took the win in front of it. A rankings line in July 2026 that reads **twentieth**, and twenty-eighth for the women. And a national union that, describing its own sport on its own website, wrote: **"Unfortunately rugby is Tonga's main export."**

And against that, the two families.

**Faitai Kefu** beat Australia at Ballymore in 1973, emigrated to Brisbane with no money and no English, and laid bitumen on Australian roads. His son **Toutai** won a World Cup for Australia — and then went home and coached Tonga. **Fe'ao Vunipola** captained Tonga at two World Cups, signed for Pontypool in 1998, and raised two sons who won **a hundred and fifty-four caps for England**; their mother is a Methodist minister to the Tongan diaspora; **Mako** is now in talks to coach Tonga at the 2027 World Cup and **Billy** may yet play for it, and their father spent five years running the union.

**Both families sent their sons to a Tier 1 country. Both sets of sons came back.**

That is not sentiment and it is not a rule change. It is the oldest arrangement in these islands, the one this chapter opened with and the one Section 5 is named after: **a community keeps a chosen man so that he can go and act on its behalf, and the obligation runs in both directions, and it survives the plane.** It is the *faifekau* of 1830 — what the Samoa chapter meets as the *faifeau*. It is Pita Vi in 1829. It is the four Tahitians who stopped on their way to Fiji and stayed.

Rugby has no class, and in Tonga it has no coloniser either. What it has is a church, a school, a village and a kingdom — and every one of them was built by Tongans, and every one of them is a machine for sending men away.

In July 2026, Fiji played its home matches in Cardiff, Liverpool and Edinburgh. Samoa played its home matches in Chile. Tonga opened its season in Denver, Colorado.

**Three island nations, one week, and not one of them at home.**

Next come the countries that were let in late, and on somebody else's terms — and the first of them spent a century being told it did not belong at a table it had been sitting at since 1929.
