# Italy

## Generation 0: The Man Who Came Back from France (1910–1927)

**2 April 1911. The Arena, Milan.**

Fifteen men in the colours of the **Unione Sportiva Milanese** are playing a game almost nobody watching has seen before, against a club from **Voiron**, in France, who have clearly seen it a great deal.

The Italians lose **15–0**.

And then the thing that makes this worth an opening paragraph: according to the *Gazzetta dello Sport*, the spectators went home **"entusiasti dello spettacolo"** — thrilled by the spectacle. Beaten to nothing, by foreigners, at a sport with no Italian history whatsoever, and the crowd loved it.

This is the first match ever played by an Italian rugby team. There is no British club in it, no British referee, no British anything.

Now the man who arranged it, because he is the reason this chapter is not like the sixteen before it.

---

### The steward of La Scala

His name was **Stefano Bellandi**, and his job was ***economo del Teatro alla Scala*** — the steward of the opera house.

He did not learn rugby at a school, because no Italian school taught it. He did not learn it from an English engineer or a Scottish bank clerk, because there was no British colony in Milan playing football of any kind. He learned it, in the phrase used by the people who have written him up, in the only place available:

> ***"Il rugby lo aveva scoperto in Francia, dove era emigrato."***
>
> He had discovered rugby in France, **where he had emigrated.**

Bellandi had gone abroad to work. He came back with a game.

In Milan he set about making it known, created a rugby section inside the **Unione Sportiva Milanese**, and found somebody to play — which in 1911 meant writing to France, because there was nobody else.

### Every other chapter in this book begins differently

Hold that against the arrivals this book has already described, because Italy's is the odd one out and the oddity is the argument.

In **Uruguay, Argentina and Chile**, the game came ashore inside British commercial enclaves — the cricket club, the bank, the shipping office, the school got up for the sons of Englishmen abroad. In **Fiji** it arrived in **1884 in the custody of the colonial police**, and `14-fiji.md` can name the province. In **Samoa** it came in **1920 with the Marist Brothers**, a teaching order that had been on the islands since 1845. In **Georgia** and **Romania**, a state picked it up and carried it deliberately.

Every one of those is an institution arriving with a ball: an empire, a church, a company, a government.

**Italy got none of them.** No governor, no missionary, no regiment, no counting house, no public school. What Italy got was **a man who had gone to France to earn a living and came home with an enthusiasm** — and who happened to work at the opera.

And note which direction the traffic runs. `14-fiji.md`, `15-samoa.md` and `16-tonga.md` are three chapters about **countries that export men and import money**: the village keeps a chosen man so that he can go and act on its behalf somewhere else. **Italy did the same thing and got a sport back.** Bellandi is the emigrant in reverse — the man who went away to work and whose remittance was a game.

### Turin, the year before

There had been rugby on Italian soil once already, and the detail is worth having exactly, because the Italian federation's own history gives it in a single line:

> **1910.** *"Prima partita disputata su suolo italiano, a Torino, tra **i francesi del Racing Club di Parigi** e **gli svizzeri del Servette**."*
>
> The first match played on Italian soil, at **Turin**, between **the French of Racing Club de Paris** and **the Swiss of Servette**.

Read the sentence again and notice who is not in it.

The first game of rugby ever played in Italy was **French against Swiss**, watched by Italians, on a field in Turin. The first game played *by* Italians, the following spring, was against a French club. The man who organised it had learned the sport **in France**. And the trainer whose words survive from the university game of the 1930s was a Frenchman called **Julien Saby**.

There is no British club, tour, fixture or figure anywhere in the founding record of Italian rugby. **The game did not come to Italy over the sea from England. It came over the Alps from France** — and it came in the luggage of Italians who had gone the other way to find work.

That single fact will govern the next ninety years. The country that carried the game into Italy was the country the Home Unions **expelled in 1931** — the France chapter tells that story — and Italy's fortunes in this sport are going to track France's relationship with Britain far more closely than anything Italy does on a field.

*(A note on primacy, since the record does not quite agree with itself. **Rugby Club Torino** is sometimes said to have been constituted straight after the 1910 match and to have been the first Italian club; the federation's own timeline credits **US Milanese** with the first match by an Italian side. Both can be true, and this book has not been able to settle which came first.)*

### The seventeen quiet years

And then, for a long time, very little.

The Italian federation's own history of itself has **three entries** between 1910 and 1928 — the Turin match, the Milan match, and a committee. Whatever happened in between happened in club rooms nobody wrote up. The museum that now holds the photographs of Italian rugby's early decades describes the period before the First World War simply as a **"pioneer phase"**, and names nobody at all.

This book has learned to say so plainly rather than fill the space. **We do not know what Italian rugby did between 1911 and 1927.** It did not die, because something was there to be organised at the end of it. But there is no championship, no fixture list, no roll of clubs, and no account of who kept it going through a war that killed six hundred thousand Italians.

What ends the silence is an act of administration.

In **1927** a committee was formed and affiliated to **CONI**, the Italian Olympic committee — and its name is the thing to keep:

**Il Comitato Nazionale di Propaganda del Giuoco della Palla Ovale.**

The National Committee for the Propaganda of the Game of the Oval Ball. Its director was **Pietro Mariani**. Among its founders was Bellandi, sixteen years after the Arena.

Two words in that title are worth pausing on, and this book is going to be careful with both.

The first is ***palla ovale*** — *oval ball*. By 1927 the sport had an Italian name in its own institution's title, and it was not "rugby." It is often said that Fascism Italianised the word later, as part of the regime's campaign against foreign vocabulary; what can actually be shown is narrower and earlier than that. **The game was calling itself *palla ovale* in its own founding documents before the regime got anywhere near it.**

The second is ***propaganda***. In 1927, in Italy, that word is not innocent and it is not yet quite guilty either. It still meant, in ordinary usage, the promotion of a thing — a committee for the *spreading* of the oval ball. But the country it was spreading into had been governed by Mussolini for five years, the Olympic committee it had just affiliated to was being folded into the apparatus of the state, and within eighteen months the party's own sporting magazine would look at this small, foreign, violent, unfashionable game and call it **ours**.

### What this generation leaves behind

By the end of 1927 Italian rugby has almost nothing. No federation, no championship, no international, no ground, no money, and a total recorded history of three events in seventeen years.

What it has is an origin unlike any other in this book: **a sport carried home by a migrant worker, from the country that would shortly be thrown out of the British game** — and now a national committee, affiliated to the state's Olympic body, with the word *propaganda* in its name.

It is about to acquire a federation, a championship, a first Test match, and a regime that has decided the oval ball is exactly the sort of thing Italians should be doing with their bodies.

## Generation 1: Our Sport (1928–1938)

**20 May 1929. Barcelona.**

Italy play the first international match in their history, and lose to **Spain, 9–0**.

Not to England. Not to France. To Spain, in Spain, by nine points to nothing, eight months after acquiring a federation, in the same year as a domestic championship that had six clubs in it.

It is about as modest a beginning as a national team can have. And the following year, at **Milan on 29 May 1930**, Italy played Spain again and won **3–0** — the first victory in the country's history, by a single score, over the same opponent.

Two Tests, two years, one opponent, and twelve points scored between them.

Now look at what was being said about this sport, in Italy, at exactly the same time.

---

### The federation, and the claim

On **20 September 1928**, **Giorgio Vaccaro** was elected President of the newly created **Federazione Italiana Rugby**. The committee for the propaganda of the oval ball had become a federation. The following year it ran a **Campionato Italiano** with **six clubs** in it, won by **Ambrosiana Milano**.

Six clubs. One national team that had never won a match.

And in **December 1928** — three months after the federation existed at all — the magazine ***Lo sport fascista*** looked at this small, obscure, imported game and called it, citing the endorsement of the party secretary **Augusto Turati**:

> ***"il nostro sport."***
>
> **Our sport.**

Sit with the disproportion. A country with six rugby clubs and no international victory, and the official sporting magazine of the governing party has already decided the game belongs to it.

This book has watched states take an interest in rugby before. `04-georgia.md` and `05-romania.md` are both chapters about governments picking the sport up and carrying it — and in both, the state arrives *after* the game has established itself, and adopts something already there. **In Italy the regime got there almost first.** The federation was three months old.

And it had a general problem with imported games, which it was solving in more than one way at once. Two years after *Lo sport fascista* claimed rugby, the Fascist administration in Florence **revived the *calcio storico fiorentino***, the city's costumed Renaissance ball game, for the **fourth centenary of the siege of 1530**. Its regulations were written, the historian **Leonardo Nesti** records, by Florence's Fascist leader **Alessandro Pavolini** himself, and the message was explicit and aimed squarely at the English: ***"ma quali inglesi? Il calcio è nato in Italia, il calcio è nato a Firenze"*** — *what Englishmen? Football was born in Italy, football was born in Florence.* Nesti's term for the exercise is ***l'invenzione di una tradizione***.

That was about **football**, not rugby, and this book is not going to pretend otherwise. But it tells you the room Italian rugby was standing in. **A regime that would invent a Renaissance ancestry for one foreign ball game was not going to be troubled by adopting another** — and where football got a manufactured Florentine pedigree, rugby got the simpler treatment: an Italian name, *palla ovale*, and the word *ours*.

### Two games at once

What followed was not one Italian rugby but two, running side by side, and the distinction lasts for decades.

The first was a **civilian club game** — the championship, the six clubs, Ambrosiana in Milan, and in the Veneto the **Leoni di San Marco**, founded on **29 November 1927** by a *Gazzettino* journalist named **Amedeo Fusari** with **Pietro Pierobon**.

The second was **institutional**, and it ran through the universities. At the University of Padua the **Gruppi Universitari Fascisti** — the GUF, the party's student organisation — began organising regular rugby training in **autumn 1929**, with **nine members**. By **1934** there were twenty-four registered players.

And here is the part that tells you where the weight lay: **the GUF side proved more durable than the independent clubs.** It contested **ten Italian championships** before the war, including **seven consecutive campaigns from 1929 to 1936**. When the **Littoriali dello sport** — the regime's national student games — were established in **1933**, university rugby had a stage built for it.

A club could fold. A party organisation had a building, a budget and a supply of eighteen-year-olds.

The men running it did not talk about it as party work. The trainer **Julien Saby** — a Frenchman, which by now should surprise nobody — wrote that rugby was a game for ***"aristocratici di cuore e di pensiero"***: aristocrats of heart and of thought. That is the language of the sport's own self-image everywhere in this book, from Rugby School to the Melrose Border towns, and it was being used in Italy inside an institution named for a political party.

### Turin, 1933

And then the most consequential thing Italian rugby has ever done, which it did in an administrative meeting.

> **1933.** *"A **Torino** viene costituita la **FIRA**, la Federazione Europea. **Italia, Francia, Germania, Romania e Cecoslovacchia** sono le Federazioni fondatrici."*
>
> At **Turin**, FIRA is constituted, the European Federation. **Italy, France, Germany, Romania and Czechoslovakia** are the founding federations.

Count who is absent. **England, Scotland, Wales and Ireland are not in that room**, and were never going to be.

Now read it against `11-france.md`, because the timing is not a coincidence. **In 1931 the Home Unions expelled France from the Five Nations** for professionalism. Two years later, in an Italian city, France sat down with four countries it could still play and founded a European federation of its own.

The French chapter tells those years from the French side, and with a distinct curl of the lip: cut off from Britain and Ireland, the French were "left to make do with matches against **Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy**." *That is where a sport goes when the democracies stop playing it.*

From Italy's side it does not look like making do. **It looks like being a founder.** The organisation constituted at Turin in 1933 outlived the regime that hosted it, outlived the Soviet Union, and is the body now called **Rugby Europe** — the structure through which Romanian and Georgian rugby would later reach the world, as the Romania chapter and the Georgia chapter both record without ever explaining where it came from.

**It came from here.** Two countries locked out of the British game, and three more who were never going to be let in, built the alternative — and they built it in Turin.

### What being France's fixture actually meant

It would be pleasant to leave the alliance there. The record does not allow it.

Italy played France twice in that decade, and the Italian federation's own ledger gives both:

- **Rome, 22 April 1935: Italy 6, France 44.**
- **Paris, 17 October 1937: France 43, Italy 5.**

Thirty-eight points, and then thirty-eight again.

That is what the partnership looked like on a field. France, shut out of Britain, came to Rome and won by thirty-eight points; Italy went to Paris two years later and lost by exactly the same margin. **The alternative federation's two biggest members were not peers, and the fixture that was supposed to be France's consolation was, for Italy, a hiding.**

Both things are true at once, and the chapter is not going to choose between them. **Italy was a founder of European rugby and could not get within forty points of the only founder that mattered.** Being inside the room is not the same as being able to play.

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1938 Italy has a federation ten years old, a championship, one victory over Spain and one defeat by them, a university system quietly supplying most of its players, a seat at the founding table of European rugby, and two forty-point defeats by France.

It also has a governing party that has been calling the game **ours** since the federation's third month.

What that meant in practice — what the regime actually wanted from a sport it had claimed — is the subject of the next generation. The answer is in a single phrase, written by the man who ran Italian sport, and it is not about spectacle or health or national prestige.

He called it a **combat sport**.

## Generation 2: Sport da Combattimento (1939–1948)

**Padua, the autumn of 1945.**

The **Associazione Rugby Padova** reorganises itself, a few months after the Liberation, and goes looking for players among the men who had been at the university before the war.

And the writing about the game changes.

Italian sports journalism had spent the best part of two decades describing rugby in the vocabulary of the barracks. Now, in the same newspapers, in the same country, about the same sport played by many of the same men, the words are **cavalleria, speranza, felicità** — chivalry, hope, happiness.

Nothing about the game had altered. Not the laws, not the clubs, not the shape of the ball. What had gone was the regime that had spent seventeen years explaining what rugby was **for**.

Go back and look at what it had said.

---

### The man who ran Italian sport, on what the game was

**Achille Starace** was Secretary of the National Fascist Party from **1931 to 1939** and President of **CONI** from **1933 to 1939**. On rugby, he was not decorative and he was not vague:

> ***"Sport da combattimento, deve essere praticato e largamente diffuso."***
>
> **A combat sport. It must be practised, and widely spread.**

**Lando Ferretti** supplied the reasoning. Rugby, he wrote, produced ***"tanto fiato, tanto coraggio, tanto vigore"*** — so much breath, so much courage, so much vigour — which were the qualities a man needed to be ready for battle.

That is the whole of it, stated by the people in charge, without euphemism. The regime did not adopt rugby because it was beautiful, or Italian, or popular. It adopted rugby because it believed the game **manufactured soldiers**, and it said so in public.

The **Museo delle Civiltà** in Rome now holds the photographic archive that shows what that looked like in practice — the **fondo Luca Raviele**, a collection documenting rugby inside the mass organisations of the regime: the **Gioventù Italiana del Littorio**, the party's youth movement, and the **Gruppi Universitari Fascisti**, its students. Not rugby with the regime looking on. **Rugby inside it.**

### The same sentence, in two hemispheres

This book has heard that argument before, in a chapter about a country ten thousand miles away and a very different kind of power.

`16-tonga.md` records **Lieutenant-Colonel John McLeod, MC**, in early 1941, assessing the material available to the Allies in the Kingdom of Tonga. He found the Tongans "keen to make good soldiers", and reported that they "**took to drill and manoeuvres like ducks to water**", because "the blood of warriors and gentlemen flows in their native veins." The Tongan historian **Amanda SullivanLee** reads that praise for what it was: Polynesians were desirable because they were "**strong and brave but also submissive**", and were seen as "one of the many weapons in the military's advancing arsenals."

**Starace and McLeod are making the same assessment in the same decade.** A body is a military asset; the game that hardens it is therefore a national instrument.

But the difference is worth stating precisely, because it is the difference between two whole sections of this book. **McLeod was an imperial officer appraising somebody else's young men.** Tonga had not asked to be assessed and did not run the assessment. **Starace was appraising his own** — an Italian minister, in Rome, describing what Italians were to be turned into by a game the state had claimed.

One is an empire looking at a people it recruits from. The other is a country looking at itself and reaching the same conclusion. **Section 5 is about bodies being taken. This is about bodies being volunteered.**

### The bill

Then the war the sport was being used to prepare for arrived, and rugby paid for its adoption.

**At least ten Italian rugby players were killed in combat** — and, in the University of Padua's account, that was **the heaviest loss suffered by any Italian sporting federation.**

Ten men sounds like a small number until you remember the size of the thing they came out of. This was a sport whose first national championship, eleven years earlier, had contained **six clubs**. The regime had called it *our sport*, run it through the youth movement and the university groups, and spread it exactly as widely as Starace had instructed — and of all the games Italians played, **the oval ball buried the most.**

There is no irony to be extracted from that and this chapter is not going to try. The men who died were not the men who wrote the slogans.

### The years with no fixtures

Of Italian rugby between 1939 and 1948 there is almost no international record at all.

The federation's own ledgers show the shape of it plainly. **Italy played France in Paris in October 1937 and did not play them again until 1952.** It played Spain in Milan in 1930 and did not play Spain again until **1951**. Between the last pre-war fixture and the first post-war one there is a gap of roughly **fourteen years** in which the national team barely existed.

The clubs went on where they could, and then could not. The university sides that had been the most reliable competitors in the country lost their competitions, their grounds and their students to the war.

What survived it was the thing that had been there before the regime ever noticed the sport: **a few hundred men in a few northern cities who liked playing.** Padova reorganising in the autumn of 1945 with former university players is the whole recovery in one sentence — the institution the party had built, emptied of the party, still full of people who wanted a game.

### What this generation leaves behind

Italy comes out of the war with a federation that has survived the regime that adopted it, a set of clubs in the north and the Veneto, a university game that had been the most consistent thing in Italian rugby and was now looking for a new name for itself, and ten dead.

It also comes out with a problem it will not solve for fifty years. **It has no opponents.**

Spain twice before the war and twice after. France, when France felt like it. A European federation founded in Turin whose most powerful member had beaten Italy by thirty-eight points twice.

The next generation is the one where Italy gets close enough to France to frighten them — twice, by two points and by three — and where the answer to that is a scoreline so large that the fixture stops.

## Generation 3: The Downgraded Fixture (1949–1972)

**26 March 1967. Toulon.**

**France 60, Italy 13.**

It is the heaviest defeat Italy has ever suffered against them, and it is the last time for twenty-eight years that the team Italy plays will be France's actual team.

The reflex is to read that scoreline as the end of a long humiliation — the point at which a small country was finally found out. **It is very nearly the opposite.** Four years before Toulon, Italy had lost to France by two points. Five years before, by three.

The rout did not come after a decline. **It came after the closest Italy had ever been**, and it ended the relationship.

Go back to the beginning of it.

---

### Every spring, for sixteen years

From **1952 to 1967**, Italy played France **every single year**, and the Italian federation's ledger of it reads like a liturgy: one fixture, late March or April, the venue alternating between the two countries. Milan, then Lyon. Rome, then Grenoble. Padua, Agen, Naples, Nantes, Treviso, Chambéry, Brescia, Grenoble again, Parma, Pau, Naples, and finally Toulon.

**Sixteen consecutive years. Italy lost all sixteen.**

Six of those afternoons Italy did not score at all — **1955, 1959, 1960, 1961, 1965, 1966**. The heaviest defeat before the end came at **Agen in 1957**, where France won **38–6**.

Understand what that fixture was, though, because "lost sixteen" is a misleading way to describe it. **This was not an occasional humiliation. It was the Italian rugby calendar.** There was no Five Nations to be excluded from in any meaningful weekly sense; there was one great annual match, against the best team on the continent, and every Italian who played the game grew up knowing when it was.

And then, in the middle of all that, two afternoons that changed what was thinkable.

### Brescia, and Grenoble

**22 April 1962. Brescia. Italy 3, France 6.**

**14 April 1963. Grenoble. France 14, Italy 12.**

A three-point defeat at home, and then, in France, a two-point defeat away.

Nothing in the record before or after those two springs looks anything like them. The year before Brescia, Italy had lost 17–0 in Chambéry. The year after Grenoble, they lost 12–3 in Parma. For two seasons, in the early 1960s, an Italian side that had never beaten France was within a converted try of them, home and away.

**That is the high-water mark of the first sixty years of Italian rugby, and almost nobody outside Italy has ever heard of it.**

Then 1964, 1965, 1966 — 12–3, 21–0, 21–0 — and then Toulon.

### What sixty points bought

After **France 60, Italy 13**, the fixture did not continue in its old form. It effectively stopped.

In ESPN's flat summary of what followed: "**From then on the Italians faced 'A' teams or occasional Espoirs**" — France's second team, or its under-age side — and that arrangement lasted until **1995**, when France finally fielded a full XV against Italy again, in the Latin Cup, in **Argentina**.

Twenty-eight years.

This book has spent sixteen chapters on the many ways a rugby institution can keep somebody out. England did it with a regulation about broken time. South Africa did it with a state. The Home Unions did it to France in 1931 with an expulsion, and to Fiji, Samoa and Tonga for most of a century by simply not scheduling them.

**France did it to Italy in the gentlest way available: by continuing to play, and sending somebody else.**

There is no vote to point at, no letter, no ban. The fixture survived; the opponent was quietly substituted. And an Italian who ran out in 1975 or 1983 against a French side in French colours was playing a match that in Paris did not fully count.

### Where the two countries stop agreeing

Which produces a detail this book finds more eloquent than any of the scorelines.

The Italian federation's own all-time record against France reads **thirty-one matches played**. ESPN, describing Italy's first win over a French side at **Treviso in 1993**, called it the first time Italy had beaten "**any team calling itself France**" in **forty-five meetings**.

Thirty-one, or forty-five. The gap is fourteen, and the fourteen are the **'A' sides and the Espoirs**.

**Italy and France do not agree on how many times they have played each other.** France sent teams it declined to recognise as itself; the Italian federation, reasonably enough, declined to count them. The disagreement is not a clerical error — it is the whole relationship, preserved in two sets of books.

### And then Naples

If Toulon was the ceiling giving way, the floor went four years later, at home.

**21 February 1971. Naples. Italy 6, Morocco 8.**

The federation's own ledger carries it without comment, in a competition it calls the **Coppa Europa**. Morocco would beat Italy once more, in **Casablanca in 1977, 10–9**; those are the only two Moroccan wins in eight meetings, and both were by a single score.

It is reported that the Naples defeat cost Italy its place in the competition's first division. This book has not been able to confirm that from a source it trusts, and states only what the record shows: **a two-point home defeat by Morocco, in February, in Naples, four years after conceding sixty in Toulon.**

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1972 Italian rugby has lost the only fixture that gave its calendar a shape, and has not replaced it. It has been within two points of France and beaten at home by Morocco inside a decade. It has a federation, a championship and no standing whatsoever.

What it is about to acquire is the strangest and least remembered thing in this chapter: **a rival.**

Not France. Not Spain. **Every autumn, for fourteen years, in Rovigo and Moscow and Kiev and L'Aquila, Italy is going to play the Soviet Union — and lose most of them by a single score.**

## Generation 4: Every November, Moscow (1973–1992)

**2 November 1980. Rovigo. Italy 3, Soviet Union 4.**

That is the entire surviving account of the match.

Not a summary of it — **all of it.** A date, a town in the Veneto, and a one-point defeat, recorded in a column on the Italian federation's website. There is no match report this book could find, no photograph it could name, no player's recollection, no line in any history. Somebody kicked a penalty and somebody else did something worth a point more, and two thousand kilometres of Cold War sat between the two teams, and what is left of it is **3–4**.

This chapter is not going to invent the rest. It will not describe the light at Rovigo in November or what the Soviet forwards looked like coming off the plane, because it does not know, and neither does anyone else who has written about Italian rugby in English.

What it can do is put the column on the page, because the column is extraordinary.

---

### The fixture

It begins in **Rome in November 1978**, and Italy lose by two. The following October, in **Moscow**, they lose by nine without scoring. Then **Rovigo, November 1980**, and they lose by one — **three points to four.** Then **Moscow again, 1981**, and they draw, twelve apiece.

And so it goes, almost every autumn for the next decade. **Catania**, and Italy win by two. **Kiev**, and they lose by nine. **L'Aquila in 1984**, and they win by one — **thirteen to twelve.** **Moscow**, lose by two. **Genoa**, lose by two. **Kishinev**, lose by three. **Treviso**, lose by six. **Moscow** once more, in November 1989, and lose by three.

**Twelve matches across twelve years, and not one of them decided by more than nine points.** Most were decided by one, two or three. A one-point game at Rovigo. A one-point game at L'Aquila. **A draw in Moscow.**

Italy played the Soviet Union **fourteen times and lost nine.** For a decade and a half this was the closest, most persistent and most evenly matched fixture Italian rugby had — closer than France had ever been, more regular than anything the Five Nations offered anyone, and played almost every autumn — with one May afternoon in Catania and one blank year — in Rovigo, L'Aquila, Treviso and Genoa, and in Moscow and Kiev and Kishinev.

**The Five Nations did not know it was happening.** Neither, now, does anyone else.

### What "excluded" actually meant

It is worth being blunt about what this generation is for, because the received account of these years is wrong.

The received account is that Italy spent the second half of the twentieth century **waiting** — outside the door, unplayed, hoping to be noticed, a country with a federation and nothing to do. That is what the phrase "the late admissions" implies, and it is what most English-language rugby writing assumes.

**Italy was not waiting. Italy had a full international career.** In the competition the Italian federation calls the **Coppa Europa**, it played, across these decades:

- **Romania — forty-one times**, of which Romania won sixteen;
- **the Soviet Union — fourteen times**, of which the Soviet Union won nine;
- **Spain — twenty-seven times**; **Czechoslovakia — twelve**; **Poland — seven**; **Morocco — eight.**

That is a calendar. It is not the calendar anybody in London was reading, but it was full, it was competitive, and against its two strongest members **Italy lost more than it won**.

⭐ And it connects two chapters of this book that do not know they are connected. `05-romania.md` is a chapter about a communist state that picked rugby up and carried it deliberately, and about the golden age that produced it. **Italy is the opponent Romania played more than any other — forty-one times.** The fixture list Romanian rugby actually lived on, week by week and year by year, is in the Italian federation's ledgers.

### 22 May 1987, Auckland

Into the middle of that comes an afternoon that belongs to a different world entirely.

**The first Rugby World Cup begins, and the first match ever played in it is New Zealand against Italy.**

`12-new-zealand.md` opens its sixth generation on that whistle. **New Zealand 70, Italy 6.**

Sixty-four points, in the first fixture in the tournament's history, in front of the sport's assembled establishment. If any single scoreline fixed the outside world's idea of what Italian rugby was, it is that one — and it was recorded two and a half years after Italy had beaten the Soviet Union by a point at L'Aquila, and six months before losing to them by three in Kishinev.

The tournament did not end there, and the rest of it is more interesting than the opening. **Italy, Fiji and Argentina all finished Pool 3 level on two points**, each with one win and two defeats. Fiji went through on **tries scored — six, against Italy's five and Argentina's four** — as the Fiji chapter describes from the Fijian side, with some feeling.

**Italy beat Fiji in that pool and went out anyway**, on a tiebreaker, in the first World Cup ever held.

Four years later, at **RWC 1991**, Italy were drawn with **England, New Zealand and the United States**. This book has not been able to establish Italy's own three results in that pool and will not guess at them.

### The opponent stops existing

And then the fixture that had defined the generation ended, in a way no rugby administrator planned.

**24 December 1990, Rovigo: Italy 34, USSR 12.** **3 November 1991, Moscow: USSR 3, Italy 21.**

After twelve years of one-score matches, Italy won the last two by twenty-two points and by eighteen.

**The match in Moscow on 3 November 1991 was played seven weeks before the Soviet Union was dissolved.**

There was no farewell fixture and no acknowledgement. Italy's closest rival for a decade and a half — the team it had drawn with in Moscow, lost to by one at Rovigo, beaten by one at L'Aquila — simply ceased to be a country, and the fixture went off the calendar with it.

That is how this generation of Italian rugby ends: not with a promotion or a rejection or a vote, but with **the other team's flag coming down.**

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1992 Italy has played more international rugby than most people outside Italy imagine, against opponents most of them could not name. It has forty-one meetings with Romania, fourteen with the Soviet Union, a World Cup exit on tries scored, and a sixty-four point defeat that is the only thing anyone remembers.

It has no fixture against France that France recognises. It has no Five Nations. And it has just lost, to history rather than to a scoreline, the one team that played it as an equal.

What happens next is that Italy finally beats something calling itself France — and then, in Grenoble, wins the competition its own federation helped found in Turin sixty-four years earlier.

## Generation 5: Beating France (1993–1999)

**22 March 1997. Grenoble.**

**France 32, Italy 40.**

In France, against France, Italy win the **Coppa Europa** — the championship of the federation constituted at Turin in **1933** by five countries the British game had no use for.

**Sixty-four years.** That is how long it took the organisation Italy helped found to be won by Italy, and it was won by beating the founder that had spent most of those years declining to send its first team.

It is, by a distance, the greatest afternoon in Italian rugby to that point. And **ten months later, Italy was elected to the Six Nations.**

Those two facts are not adjacent by accident, and the rest of this generation is the case for saying so.

---

### Treviso, and the two sets of books

Four years earlier, something had happened that Italy had waited eighty years for.

**16 March 1993. Treviso. Italy 16, France 9.**

ESPN's account is careful about what it was: "Given that it was the first time Italy had beaten **any team calling itself France** in **45 meetings**, they were entitled to their celebration."

*Any team calling itself France.* The phrasing is doing precise work, and this chapter has already explained why. Since Toulon in 1967, what France sent to Italy had been **'A' sides and Espoirs** — French colours, French anthem, and a squad Paris did not count as the national team.

So there are two versions of the record, and both are honest.

The **Italian federation's own all-time ledger** against France reads **thirty-one matches played, one Italian win** — and the one it lists is **Grenoble, 1997**. Treviso 1993 is not on it. **ESPN counts forty-five meetings**, because ESPN counts what took the field.

Thirty-one, or forty-five. **The difference is fourteen matches, and the fourteen matches are the twenty-eight years.** Italy and France still do not agree how often they have played, and the disagreement is not administrative pedantry — it is the exact measure of a fixture that continued in name after it had stopped in substance.

Treviso in 1993 was a real win over a real French team that France did not regard as France. **Grenoble in 1997 was a win over France.** The chapter records both and merges neither.

### 1995: the full team comes back

In between them, the arrangement quietly ended.

**14 October 1995. Buenos Aires. France 34, Italy 22.** A **Coppa Latina** fixture, played in Argentina, and the first time since Toulon that France fielded a full side against Italy.

**Twenty-eight years.** A generation of Italian internationals had come and gone entirely inside the gap — men who played their whole careers against a France that was not France, and retired without ever meeting the first team.

There was no announcement, because there had never been a policy to announce. The fixture had been downgraded quietly and it was restored quietly, in a third country, on the other side of the Atlantic.

### The case that was actually made

It matters that the sequence ran the way it did, because the story usually told about Italy's admission is a story about **lobbying** — that the Six Nations expanded for money, or for television, or because a sixth team made the fixture list tidier.

Look at the order of events instead.

**March 1997:** Italy beats France in France and wins the European championship.
**October 1997:** France beats Italy 30–19 at Auch.
**January 1998:** **Italy's election to the Six Nations is ratified.**

Whatever else was in the argument, **Italy walked into that decision holding a continental title won against the country that had spent thirty years not playing it.** The committee that admitted Italy in 1998 was not extending a favour to a country with nothing. It was admitting the champion of the other Europe — the Europe that had been constituted in Turin in 1933 precisely because the British game had shut France out.

**Italy won the competition it had built, and was then admitted to the competition that had excluded it.** FIRA was not a consolation prize. **Winning it was the qualification.**

### The last year outside

And then, because this book does not do triumphal endings, the two years between the election and the debut.

At the **1999 World Cup**, in **Leicester on 10 October**, Italy played **Tonga** and lost **28–25**.

`16-tonga.md` tells that afternoon from the other side, and it is one of the better passages in that chapter — **Siale Piutau**, a Tongan boy watching, saying what it did to him: *"It was massive just in terms of, for a young Tongan kid growing up, and seeing that **these things were possible**."*

Hold the two chapters together for a moment, because the pairing is the whole of Section 6.

**Italy, three months from its first Six Nations match, lost by three points to a country that would never be admitted to anything.** Tonga took from that afternoon the evidence that a small country could beat a big one. Italy took from it a reminder that the invitation it had just received was not a verdict on how good it was.

**Both countries were right.**

### What this generation leaves behind

Italy ends the century with a European title, one recognised win over France in sixty-two years of trying, a ratified seat at a table it had been kept from since that table was invented, and a defeat by Tonga.

It also ends it with an expectation that nobody in Rome had asked for. The hope, when the Six Nations voted, was that Grenoble was a beginning.

The next generation is sixteen years long, and by the end of it people were still calling **1997** the best thing Italy had ever done.

## Generation 6: Inside (2000–2015)

**Rome, February 2000.**

Italy play their first match as a member of the Championship, against **Scotland**, and win it **34–20**.

Ninety years after two foreign clubs played the first game of rugby ever staged on Italian soil, and seventy-two years after a party magazine called the sport *ours*, Italy walk into the oldest competition in the game and beat the reigning champions in their first fixture.

Because that is what Scotland were. `09-scotland.md` records it from the other end: **Scotland won the last Five Nations, in 1999** — and then, "since Italy joined in **2000** to make the Six Nations, **Scotland have never won it.** Not once."

The two facts are consecutive and each chapter holds one of them. Scotland's last title, and Italy's first afternoon.

It was the best possible beginning, and it was a long way from being a beginning.

---

### What the next sixteen years were actually like

Italy has finished bottom of the Six Nations **more often than any other country in it**, and **has never won the Championship**.

Those are the two sentences that describe this generation, and they should be read without flinching. The table is not a slander invented by the countries that voted Italy in; it is the record.

The France column alone tells it. Having beaten France at Grenoble in 1997, Italy then lost to them in every Championship meeting from 2000 to 2009, and several were not close:

**42–31. 19–30. 33–12. 27–53. 25–0. 13–56. 37–12. 3–39. 25–13. 8–50.**

Fifty-six points in Rome in 2005. Fifty in Rome in 2009. Nil-for-Italy in Paris in 2004.

The team that had won a European title in Grenoble was, inside the Championship, a country being beaten by forty points at home. **Both of those were true of the same decade and largely the same players**, and any account of Italian rugby that uses only one of them is selling something.

### 2007

Then, twice in one season, the thing worked.

**In 2007, Italy won its first away match in the Championship — at Murrayfield.** And in the same tournament it beat **Wales in Rome**. Two victories in a single Six Nations, for the first time, and a **fourth-place finish**.

It would take **six more years** to do it again, in **2013**. And after that, another thirteen.

That is the rhythm of these sixteen years and it is worth naming precisely, because "Italy were bad" is lazy and wrong. **Italy were not consistently anything.** They were capable, perhaps twice in a decade, of a fortnight that no other team in the competition's second half could produce — and then of losing eleven in a row.

A country that had spent seventy years playing Romania and the Soviet Union in front of nobody was now playing Ireland at Croke Park and France at the Stade de France, on television, every February. **The gap it had to close was not one of talent. It was one of depth**, and depth is the one thing a fixture list cannot buy you quickly.

### The man who carried it

There was, through almost the whole of it, one figure.

**Sergio Parisse** won **142 caps for Italy** — more than any other Italian — between **2002 and 2019**. He played at **five Rugby World Cups**, only the **third man in the history of the game** to do so, after **Brian Lima** of Samoa and his own compatriot **Mauro Bergamasco**. He broke the **Six Nations appearance record**. He became the **first Italian inducted into the World Rugby Hall of Fame**. When he first ran out against New Zealand at the **2003 World Cup**, he was the **youngest player ever to have appeared for his country** on that stage.

Two of the three men to have played five World Cups are Italian. The country admitted last produced two of the most durable international careers the sport has recorded.

Brian Lima, the first of the three, is in the chapter before this one. `15-samoa.md` has him in the blue shirt at **Cardiff in 1991**, in the side that beat Wales — and again in its closing summary, as one of the two names Samoa is said to have produced. **The man Parisse was chasing spent his career in the other half of this book, playing for a country that has never been let in at all.**

And in **2005**, aged twenty-one, Parisse left **Treviso** for **Stade Français**.

Which is where this chapter began. **Rugby arrived in Italy in 1911 in the luggage of a man who had gone to France to work.** A century later the best player Italy ever produced went to France to make his career. The traffic that brought the game in took its finest export out, in the same direction, for the same reason: **that is where the work was.**

### The clubs leave

The other structural event of this generation is easy to miss because it happened off the international calendar.

In **2010**, Italian clubs entered the **Celtic League** — the Irish, Welsh and Scottish competition that became the **Pro12**. The two chosen were **Treviso**, now **Benetton**, and **Aironi**. To do it, **Benetton left the Italian championship altogether.**

**Aironi finished bottom of the table in each of its two seasons**, and the federation revoked its licence for financial reasons. **Zebre** replaced it in **2012**. The FIR would later become a shareholder in the league.

Look at what that decision actually was. Italy's answer to professionalism was to take its two strongest clubs **out of Italy's own championship** and put them in somebody else's, because the competition worth playing in was elsewhere.

`15-samoa.md` describes a version of the same fact from the other end of the world: **Moana Pasifika**, a Pacific team playing in a New Zealand-based competition, because that is where the Pacific players already live. Samoa's pathway is abroad because its people are. **Italy's pathway went abroad because its opponents were.** The same sentence — *the league that matters is somewhere else* — arrived at from opposite directions, in the same decade, by two countries that have nothing else in common.

### What this generation leaves behind

By 2015 Italy has been in the Championship for sixteen years. It has never won it, has finished last more often than anyone, has beaten France once in ninety years, and has produced a forward who will end up in the Hall of Fame.

It has two professional clubs, both playing in another country's league, one of which has already gone bankrupt.

And it has a reputation, in the competition that admitted it, as the fixture everyone expects to win — which is a different and more corrosive thing than being excluded.

What happens next takes seven days. In **November 2016**, at home, Italy will play two Test matches. They will beat **South Africa**, and lose to **Tonga**, **by the same score**.

## Generation 7: Seven Days in November (2016–2026)

**19 November 2016. Stadio Artemio Franchi, Florence.**

**Italy 20, South Africa 18.**

**Bryan Habana** scores in the eighth minute, on his hundred and twenty-fourth cap. **Andries van Schalkwyk** goes over from a rolling maul for Italy and **Carlo Canna** converts. **Damian de Allende** puts South Africa back in front and they lead **12–10** at the break. Then Canna kicks the penalty that makes it **20–18**, and with two minutes left Italy think they have scored again through Fuser, and the television match official rules it out, and it does not matter.

It is Italy's **first win over South Africa**, and South Africa's **first defeat by Italy in thirteen meetings**. The Italian defence that afternoon was organised by **Brendan Venter** — a former Springbok.

`06-south-africa.md` carries the same match from the other side, in a paragraph about the lowest floor in Springbok history.

**Seven days later, Italy played again, at home, and lost.**

---

### 26 November 2016. Stadio Euganeo, Padua.

**Tonga 20, Italy 18.**

**Lorenzo Cittadini** scores first for Italy. **Siale Piutau** crosses for Tonga's only try. And with a minute to play and Tonga a point behind, the scrum half **Takulua** stands over a penalty from just under forty metres and kicks it.

`16-tonga.md` carries that one from the other side too.

Now put the two afternoons next to each other, because Italian rugby has never produced anything that explains it better.

**Florence, 19 November 2016: Italy 20, South Africa 18.**
**Padua, 26 November 2016: Tonga 20, Italy 18.**

**The same score. The same country. Home both times. Seven days apart.**

In one week in November, Italy beat the world champions of 2007 — who would be world champions again in 2019 — and then lost to a country whose own union would put its playing population at fewer than eight hundred senior men. Neither result was a fluke and neither cancels the other. **They are the same team, and that is the point.**

Everything this chapter has described arrives at that week. A country with real players and no depth. A country capable of the best afternoon in the competition and the worst. A country whose greatest day and most humiliating day were separated by seven days and about two hundred kilometres.

### The farewell that was never played

Three years later Italian rugby lost something it could not get back, to weather.

**Sergio Parisse** went to the **2019 World Cup** in Japan for his **fifth** tournament — the third man in history to reach five. His last match was to be against **New Zealand**, in the pool.

**Typhoon Hagibis cancelled it.**

There was no send-off, no final whistle, no last cap. The most decorated career in Italian rugby ended with a fixture that was never played, and the same storm runs through `09-scotland.md`, whose account of Scotland's pool exit that year is filed under the same weather.

**One typhoon ended a nation's tournament and a great player's career**, and neither chapter of this book knew it was sharing a storm with the other.

### Cardiff, twice

The other thing that changed in these years is who Italy beats.

In **2022**, at Cardiff, **Italy beat Wales 22–21**. In **February 2025**, at Cardiff again, Italy beat them once more — and the Wales chapter records what that one did: it was **Warren Gatland's fourteenth consecutive defeat**, and he left by mutual consent days later, "the second coming ending not in a Grand Slam but in the worst run in the history of Welsh rugby."

Italy did not merely win a Six Nations match. **Italy ended a coaching era in one of the countries that invented the Championship.**

The Welsh chapter tells those afternoons as a decline. It is entitled to. But they are also, read from Rome, the moment when the fixture everyone expected to win stopped being that fixture.

### 2026

**The final table of the 2026 Six Nations reads: France 21, Ireland 19, Scotland 16, Italy 9, England 8.**

`11-france.md` closes on the afternoon that produced it — France 48, England 46, a second successive championship settled by a kick. Its account notes in passing that England had **the worst campaign in their history**, and that Wales took a **third consecutive Wooden Spoon**.

What it does not dwell on, because it is not a chapter about Italy, is the fourth line of that table.

**Italy finished above England.**

Two wins and fourth place equals the best Six Nations campaign Italy has ever had — the same as 2007, the same as 2013. Wales's only victory of the tournament was **31–17 over Italy in Cardiff**, so the two wins came elsewhere. The country that had finished bottom more often than anyone else in the competition ended the 2026 Championship in the top half of it, above the country that invented the sport and wrote the laws that kept everyone else out.

### Where things stood, in the middle of 2026

On **15 July 2026**, the World Rugby men's rankings read: South Africa, New Zealand, Ireland, France, England, Scotland, Argentina, Australia, **Fiji**, **Italy**.

**Tenth in the world** — and, as the Fiji chapter observes from ninth, below a Pacific island nation that has never been admitted to anything.

Italy had gone **up** into the inaugural **Nations Championship**, alongside the Six Nations and the Rugby Championship and Japan and Fiji, while **Samoa and Tonga went into the second-tier Nations Cup**. `12-new-zealand.md` records the All Blacks completing the first leg of that competition with victories over France and Italy.

Ahead lay **Rugby World Cup 2027 in Australia**, where Italy were drawn in **Pool B** with **South Africa**, **Georgia** and **Romania**. `05-romania.md` carries the draw from the Romanian end, and its French coach's response to being asked about the pool: to write off South Africa in advance and concentrate on Georgia and Italy would be, he said, disrespectful to the sport.

**Romania.** Forty-one meetings. The opponent Italy has played more than any other country on earth except France, and now a pool fixture at a World Cup.

### What this chapter leaves behind

Begin where the chapter began: with a man coming home.

**Stefano Bellandi** was the steward of La Scala, and he had gone to France to work. He came back with a game nobody in Milan played, put a rugby section inside the Unione Sportiva Milanese, wrote to a French club because there was nobody else to write to, and on **2 April 1911** watched his side lose **15–0** at the Arena while the crowd went home delighted.

Every other arrival in this book is an institution: a British trading enclave, a colonial constabulary, a teaching order, a government. **Italy's is a returning migrant.** And where `14-fiji.md`, `15-samoa.md` and `16-tonga.md` are three chapters about countries that send men abroad and get money back, **Italy sent a man abroad and got a sport back.**

Then the institutions took an interest, in the order institutions do.

A **federation** in 1928, and within three months the governing party's own magazine calling this obscure, foreign, six-club game ***il nostro sport*** — *our sport*. A regime that revived a Renaissance ball game in Florence to prove football was Italian, and simply renamed rugby ***palla ovale*** and took it. **Achille Starace** explaining exactly what it was for: ***sport da combattimento***, a combat sport, to be practised and widely spread. The **GUF** and the **GIL** running it through the universities and the youth movement. And then the bill — **at least ten Italian rugby players killed in the war, the heaviest loss of any Italian sporting federation**, out of a sport whose first championship had six clubs in it.

And a **committee**, in 1933, in **Turin**: **FIRA**, founded by Italy, France, Germany, Romania and Czechoslovakia, two years after the Home Unions expelled France. The France chapter tells that period from the French side, as making do with Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy. **From here it looks like founding something.** The organisation constituted in Turin became **Rugby Europe**, the structure through which Georgian and Romanian rugby reached the world — as the Georgia chapter and the Romania chapter both record without ever saying where it came from.

Then sixty years of what exclusion actually looks like, which is not a locked door.

It looks like **sixteen consecutive springs** playing France and losing all sixteen — and getting within **two points at Grenoble in 1963** and **three at Brescia in 1962**. It looks like **Toulon, 1967, France 60 Italy 13**, after which France went on playing Italy for twenty-eight years and sent somebody else. It looks like two federations keeping **two different sets of books** — thirty-one matches on one count, forty-five on the other — because the fourteen in between were teams France declined to call France.

And it looks like **Rovigo, Catania, L'Aquila, Treviso, Genoa, Moscow, Kiev, Kishinev**: fourteen matches against the Soviet Union across fourteen years, **twelve of them decided by nine points or fewer**, one-point games at Rovigo and L'Aquila, a draw in Moscow, and Italy losing nine. **Forty-one matches against Romania.** A full international life, played in front of nobody the British game recognised, of which **not one player's account of a single afternoon survives in any source this book could find.** The scores are all that is left, and they are on the federation's own website in a column.

**That is what being outside meant. Not idleness. Obscurity.**

And then the correction, which came from inside the alternative rather than from petitioning the original. **Grenoble, 22 March 1997: France 32, Italy 40**, and the **Coppa Europa** won at last — sixty-four years after Turin, in France, against France. **Ten months later Italy's election to the Six Nations was ratified.**

**Italy won the committee it had built, and was then admitted to the committee that had excluded it.**

What followed was sixteen years of finishing last more often than anybody, one afternoon in 2007 and another in 2013, a Hall of Fame number eight who left for France at twenty-one because that is where the work was, two clubs moved out of the Italian championship into somebody else's league, and one week in November 2016 in which the same team beat South Africa and lost to Tonga by the same score.

And then, in **2026**, fourth place, and England below them.

**Rugby has no class, and in Italy it never had a British one to inherit.** What it had instead was an emigrant, a regime, a committee founded in Turin, forty years of opponents nobody was watching, and — at the end — a seat at the table it had spent a century being told it did not belong at.

The chapter after this one is about the country Italy played in its very first international, and lost to, **nine points to nil, at Barcelona, on 20 May 1929.** Spain is still outside.
