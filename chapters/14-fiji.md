# Fiji

## Generation 0: The Constabulary Ground (1874–1912)

**3 January 1907. Nasinu, five miles outside Suva.**

Thirty-two boys arrive at a school whose foundations were laid the previous year. They have come from all over the colony — roughly two from each province, selected by the Native Commissioner and the Roko Tui of each district — and they have been brought here to be turned into something. The buildings are new. The first headmaster, **J. B. Thomson**, arrived from Britain the previous July to supervise the construction of the place he would then run.

The school is called **Queen Victoria School**, and the man who authorised it had been perfectly clear, in his budget address of 1905, about what it was for. Governor **Sir Everard Im Thurn** wanted a school at Nasinu for the young generation of Fijians "in order that as far as desirable they may imbibe **European rather than Fijian habits of thought and work** and what is to my mind not the least important item — language."

He was clearer still about who it was for. According to the historical record held by the school's old boys — documents printed in 1953, quoted in *The Fiji Times* — Im Thurn "believed that there were **two classes of Fijians** and that so far as these performed two different functions — **the chiefs as thinkers and overseers, the commoners as manual labourers**. He recommended that a different education was desirable for each class."

The Council of Chiefs had asked for the school in 1902, and had asked for it to be "modelled on **English public schools or colleges**." At first the intake was mixed. Thomson did not like that. "At first, only a fraction of the students were sons of chiefs, however, under the insistence of the headmaster, this policy was soon reversed and **by 1910, only the sons of chiefs were admitted**." His reasoning is on the record too: "After three years of experience, I have come to the conclusion that as a general rule, **a chief's son is far more intelligent than the son of a commoner**."

Hold that in your hand for a moment, because this book has met it before. It is Rugby School's ethos, exported. It is the Great Public Schools of Sydney, whose own history records that Sydney Boys' High applied for membership in 1894 and was not admitted until 1906. It is the machinery that produced the amateur laws in England in 1886 and drove twenty-two northern clubs out in 1895.

Queen Victoria School would go on to win the first Deans Trophy — which the union calls the oldest competition in Fijian rugby — in 1939, and it and one other school have dominated that competition ever since. And the class Im Thurn ordered — the governing caste, separated by education from the labourers — never arrived.

That is this chapter's question. **The English public-school model was imported into Fiji deliberately, explicitly, as a class instrument, by a governor who wrote down what he was doing. In Sydney and Brisbane the identical instrument worked completely. Here it did not.** Something underneath it refused.

Now go back thirty-three years, to the beginning of the colony, because the thing that refused was already in place — and so was the division that *did* take, and that still held in 2026.

---

### Two peoples, by design

**Fiji became a British colony on 10 October 1874**, by an offer of unconditional cession — an offer made, after more than a decade of manoeuvre, by a group of chiefs of whom the most powerful was **Seru Epenisa Cakobau** of Bau. It was one of the very few colonies Britain acquired by being asked.

The first governor was **Sir Arthur Gordon**, and Gordon arrived with a conviction that would shape everything that followed. He had seen what plantation labour had done to indigenous populations in the West Indies, and he did not intend to be responsible for the same thing here. So he made a decision that was, by the standards of nineteenth-century colonial administration, protective in intent and total in consequence: **indigenous Fijians would not work the plantations.** Their land would be held communally and inalienably. Their villages would be governed through their own chiefs, under the system of indirect rule that Britain was busy inventing across its empire.

Somebody, however, still had to cut the cane.

The historian Kirstie Close-Barry, in her study of the Methodist mission in Fiji, puts the figure plainly: Gordon and his successors "oversaw the migration of **60,965 indentured labourers to Fiji between 1879 and 1916**." They came from India, under the contract system the labourers themselves called **girmit** — a Fiji Hindi rendering of the English word *agreement* — and their descendants are Indo-Fijians, who within two generations were a very large share of the country's population.

So within five years of annexation the colony had two peoples in it, kept apart by policy, doing different work, holding land on entirely different terms, and answerable to different structures of authority. **Nobody had to invent a racial division in Fijian sport later on. The state had already built one, at the moment of colonisation, and had done it out of a genuine intention to protect the people it was dividing.**

This book has watched institutions produce class. In Fiji it watched an institution produce a race line first, and the sport arrived afterwards and lay down inside it.

### The mission divides itself

Christianity got to Fiji before the flag did. The Wesleyan Methodists established themselves at **Lakeba in the Lau group in 1835**, arriving via Tonga, and by Close-Barry's reckoning they "preceded the cession of the Island group by nearly 40 years." Cakobau himself converted in **1854**. By the 1860s Fijian ministers were being trained in the islands and sent back to their own villages to preach — so indigenous authority inside the church, as Close-Barry notes, actually **pre-dated British indirect rule**, and then came to operate on exactly the same principles.

The Methodist mission was not an appendage to Fijian life. It became one of its three load-bearing walls. Fijians describe the structure themselves as **vanua** — the way of the land, the chief, the village and the ties of kinship that bind them — **lotu**, the church, and **matanitu**, the state. Take the church out and the village does not stand up.

And then, in **1901**, the mission split itself in two.

Close-Barry's book opens on the moment: the establishment of "separate '**Fijian**' and '**Indo-Fijian**' branches in 1901, which formed the foundation for **two separate, ethnicised Methodist identities in Fiji**." There had been Indo-Fijian schools and services and meeting halls before that — Hannah Dudley founded the first school exclusively for Indo-Fijian children in Suva after arriving in 1897, and an Indo-Fijian mission hall opened at Nausori on 19 December 1901 — but 1901 is when the structure was formalised. The missionaries believed, Close-Barry writes, that separation "would ensure the creation of two separate churches — Fijian and Indo-Fijian — that would hold relevance for its members."

The mission, she argues, "was organised in a way that **mirrored the systems of governance used in British colonies under indirect rule**." It was not copying the state cynically. It was drawing on the same ideas from the same imperial conversation, and reaching the same design.

Remember the date. **1901.** Rugby will do precisely the same thing, in the same colony, thirteen years later — and it will not be copying the church either. Both of them will be doing what everything in Fiji did.

### The game arrives in a police uniform

There is no cricket club in this founding, and no university, and no regiment of officers.

**In 1884, at Ba, on the western side of Viti Levu, European and Fijian members of the Native Constabulary played a game of rugby football.** That is the Fiji Rugby Union's own account, in its own history — though the union hedges its narrative version to "believed first played in Fiji **in the 1880s**", which is worth noting rather than smoothing over. The scholarly account agrees and adds the clause that matters: games "between European and Fijian policemen and soldiers of the Armed Constabulary, **developing along segregated lines based on ethnicity**."

It is a very particular origin. Everywhere else in this book the game came ashore in a commercial community — wine traders in Bordeaux, nitrate men in Iquique, railway and bank clerks in Buenos Aires and Montevideo, hotel builders and gold miners in New Zealand. The **cricket club as incubator** is the oldest thread here: a British clubhouse, a summer game, a winter to fill.

In Fiji the carrying institution was **an arm of the colonial state**. The Native Constabulary was the police force that indirect rule ran on — Fijian men, in a British uniform, under British officers, distributed across the provinces. When the sport spread, it spread along the same lines the administration did. The union's own chronology bears it out: by 1904 there was a club competition in Suva, and "two of the earliest clubs were **Civil Service and Constabulary**."

Rugby entered Fiji as a thing that the government did.

### Albert Park, and a prophecy from Wairarapa

The record for the next decade is thin, and mostly a matter of ships.

By 1900 the union records that "reports of games began to appear in the press, and although **most players were expatriates**, a club competition began in 1904." By 1911, "the crews of visiting warships provided frequent opposition and the first regular club, **Davies**, had been formed."

One incident from that period is worth keeping, for its sheer improbability. In **1907** a New Zealander named **A. C. Holms**, the Wairarapa fullback, passed through Suva with three other New Zealand players. He did not merely watch. He "not only played with Fijians on **Albert Park**, Suva, but predicted (in a Vancouver newspaper) that **Fiji would one day make their mark in rugby football**."

A provincial fullback from the lower North Island, filing a prediction to a Canadian paper, from a park in a colonial capital, about a country that would not play its first international for another seventeen years. He was right in a way he could not have imagined, and this chapter will come back to him.

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1912 Fiji has a colonial society deliberately built in two parts, a church that has just formalised the same division inside itself, an English public school at Nasinu designed to manufacture a Fijian governing class, a rugby ball that arrived in the custody of the police, and a handful of expatriate clubs in Suva playing visiting warships.

It has no union, no international, no national competition and no jersey.

All of that arrives in the next twelve months, and it arrives because a plumber from Dunedin came to Suva to work on a hotel.

## Generation 1: Two Unions, One Colony (1913–1938)

**18 August 1924. Apia Park, Western Samoa. Seven o'clock in the morning.**

Fiji are about to play the first international match in their history, and the kick-off has been set for dawn so that the Samoans can get to work afterwards.

There is **a large tree standing on the halfway line**. Nobody has removed it, and nobody appears to think it needs removing. The Fijians are wearing **all black** — the white jersey with the palm tree is still two years away. They have stopped here on their way to Tonga.

They win **6–0**.

The report of the afternoon — the morning — praises Fiji for their **speed and agility**, and describes the Samoans as "**the big, sturdy men of Apia**", a piece of characterisation that will attach itself to Samoan rugby for the next hundred years and is not finished attaching itself yet. The general opinion on all sides, the record says, was that the game "was **the cleanest and finest exhibition of football played in Samoa**."

Fiji's first Test match was against Samoa, in Samoa, at first light, around a tree. Samoa and Tonga both have chapters of their own in this section, and this morning is in both of them.

Now go back eleven years, to a building site in Suva.

---

### The plumber from Otago

In **1913** a group of New Zealanders came to Suva to build the **Grand Pacific Hotel**. One of them was a plumber named **Paddy Sheehan**, from Dunedin, and Sheehan had previously been **captain of Otago**.

He looked at the casual, occasional, ship-dependent rugby being played around the capital and concluded that it needed organising. He formed the **Pacific Club**. By the end of that club's first meeting three more had been planned, and were duly formed: **United Services, Cadets and Rewa**. Together the four of them constituted the **Fiji Rugby Football Union** — though, as the union's own history is careful to say, it "played only in Suva."

When the first officers were elected, Sheehan became chairman. The governor of the day, **Sir Ernest Bickham Sweet-Escott**, donated the **Escott Shield** for the club championship. The Pacific Club won it, and Suva's clubs were still competing for it when the union wrote up its own chronology nearly ninety years later.

There is a small and pleasing symmetry in this. In the New Zealand chapter, **Otago** is the province that walked out of the meeting that founded the New Zealand union in 1892, rather than submit to a central authority. Twenty-one years later a former captain of Otago is in Suva, on a hotel site, in overalls, founding somebody else's union and taking the chair of it.

That December the All Blacks stopped at Suva on the way home from California and played a friendly at Albert Park. Fiji's rugby union was months old and had already hosted New Zealand.

### The second union

The union Sheehan founded was the union of the colonial capital: expatriates, civil servants, the services, the clubs of Suva. Fijians had been playing since Ba in 1884, and were not in it.

So in **1914** — the year after the Fiji RFU was constituted — "a so called '**native competition**' began under the leadership of **Ratu Epeli Ganilau**." The first clubs were **Taipou, Tarirere, Hill** and **Ofisa**, which is to say the Police. In **1915** a Suva businessman, **J. J. Davies**, presented the **Davies Cup**, first won by Gaunavou; and in the same year "a **Fiji Native Union** was begun and became **affiliated to** the Fiji RFU."

Affiliated. Not merged.

Two unions, one colony, from 1915. It would stay that way for thirty years.

It is worth being exact about what this was and what it was not, because it would be easy and wrong to reach for South Africa here. Nothing in Fiji resembled the machinery that the South Africa chapter describes — no legislated separation of sport, no state that had decided to build a race out of a football code, no barbed wire. What happened in Fijian rugby in 1914 and 1915 was something duller and, in its way, more instructive. **The colony was already organised in ethnic compartments, from the land law down through the church, and when rugby got big enough to need an institution it simply took the shape of the container it was growing in.** A separate competition, then a separate union, then affiliation on unequal terms.

The church had done it in **1901**. Rugby did it in **1914–15**. And, from the other side of the same line, soccer would do it too: as the scholars of Fijian football record, the **Fiji Indian Football Association** became the de facto controlling body of that sport in **1938** — which reflected, they write, "not only the community's passion for administering, watching, and playing soccer, but also **the role they were assigned by the British during colonialism** as the merchant class, the intellectual class, the trade unionists, the schoolteachers, and the politicians."

Three institutions, three separate decisions, one shape. Not one of them was copying the others.

### The tour, and the jersey

The Fijians of the native competition were, within a decade, the national team.

The **1924** trip that produced the dawn match at Apia went on to Tonga, where Fiji played nine matches, **won seven, drew one and lost one**, and — in the union's phrase — "began the healthy relationship with the Tonga Rugby Union that continues to this day." Chapter 16 will pick that thread up from the Tongan end.

**1926** brought the first visit by an overseas side proper — **Auckland University College**, a touring team rather than a ship's crew or a party breaking a voyage home — and a return visit by Tonga. It also brought the kit: "for the first time Fiji played in their present strip of **white jersey, palm tree badge, and black shorts**."

A white jersey. It is a small thing and a very deliberate one. Every other country in the Pacific and most of the countries in this book were still working out how to look like somebody else. Fiji chose white, put a palm tree on it, and has not changed it since.

### The concrete in the middle of the field

There is one sentence in the Fiji Rugby Union's own history that explains more about the first fifty years of Fijian rugby than anything else in it.

A schools competition started in **1928**, but a national schools union did not follow until **1939** — and up to 1928, the union records, **all rugby in Fiji had taken place in the Suva–Nausori area**. Why? Here is the union's stated reason:

> "It is very difficult to establish the game outside Suva because **almost every suitable ground already has a concrete cricket pitch at the centre**."

Read that against the rest of this book.

The **cricket club as incubator** is one of the oldest motifs here. In Montevideo the Victoria Cricket Club became the Montevideo Cricket Club and hatched the first football played in Uruguay. In Buenos Aires and Valparaíso the same clubhouses hatched both codes. In Melbourne, a Rugby School old boy who was secretary of the **Melbourne Cricket Club** wrote to *Bell's Life* proposing a winter game for cricketers, and Australia got a third football code out of it and union never recovered.

In Fiji the cricket square did not incubate anything. **It sat in the middle of every playable field in the country, in concrete, and physically held rugby out of the provinces for a generation.** The same institution, in a different climate, with a different maintenance budget, produced the exact opposite effect. That is the argument of this book in one paragraph of estates management.

### Boots, and a gift from the Māori

In **1938** the first **New Zealand Māori** side to visit Fiji arrived, and in their honour "**Fijians played in boots for the first time**." The union adds, drily, that "there was still a tendency to take off boots during the match and throw them to the touch-line."

The tour tells you where Fijian rugby stood in 1938. On a five-match visit the Māori beat the **Fiji 2nd XV** and the **Fiji Europeans** — a separate European representative side still existed, twenty-four years after the native competition began — before squaring the three-Test series against Fiji proper.

And they left something behind. The **Deans Trophy**, the union records, "was **donated by the Maori All Blacks team that toured Fiji in 1938** to be competed for by the Secondary Schools of Fiji." The competition, introduced in **1939**, is described by the union as the oldest in Fijian rugby — a claim its own chronology slightly complicates, since it also records a schools competition from 1928. **Queen Victoria School won the first Deans Trophy.** Lelean Memorial won its first in 1948, **Ratu Kadavulevu School** — the other great nursery — in 1961, Marist Brothers in 1965.

Sit with the provenance a moment. The New Zealand chapter follows the New Zealand Māori side through half a century of being left at home to protect the South African fixture — a team its own union spent, repeatedly, as the price of a tour. That is the team that gave Fijian schools the trophy they have played for ever since.

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1938 Fiji has a union, a second union affiliated to it, a Test record, a white jersey with a palm tree on it, a schools trophy donated by New Zealand Māori, and a set of players who have just consented to wear boots.

What it does not have is any reputation whatsoever outside the Pacific.

That takes one tour, in 1939, and by the union's own account nobody has matched it since.

## Generation 2: Barefoot (1939–1954)

**Hamilton, 1939. The last match of the tour.**

The Fijians are playing the **New Zealand Māori**, and a good part of the crowd has come to see the barefoot men beaten at last. They have been in the country for weeks. They have not lost.

They win **14–4**.

The *Waikato Times* runs out of adjectives trying to describe what it has watched:

> "**Almost uncanny in handling the ball, lightning in the pace of their sprinting, relentless in their dive tackling** … and all the time pursuing methods of bright, open football, the Fijians gave a sparkling display and thrilled the large crowd."

It called the display "the most brilliant exhibition of football seen in Hamilton for many years," and then made a prediction of its own: "**Fiji is destined to play a big part in world rugby.**"

The paper had no way of knowing which part. Fiji would not reach a World Cup quarter-final for another forty-eight years. The thing they would come to own outright had been invented in a Scottish mill town in 1883 and would not arrive in Hong Kong until the 1970s.

**Fiji played through a full tour of New Zealand and were not beaten: seven wins and a draw.** The Fiji union's own history calls it the first time any team had gone through a full tour of New Zealand unbeaten, and says the record still stands. A good number of the men who did it played without boots.

---

### The captain, and the war dance

The side was captained by **Ratu Sir George Cakobau**, and it is worth saying who that was.

He was a great-grandson of the **Cakobau** who had offered Fiji to Queen Victoria in 1874. He would later become **Governor-General of Fiji**. In 1939 he was a young chief leading a rugby team through New Zealand, and it appears he was thinking hard about the theatre of the thing: the story Fijian rugby has always told about itself is that Cakobau decided his side needed a war dance to answer the haka, went to **Ratu Bola**, a high chief of the warrior clan of Navusaradave in Bau, and was taught the **cibi**.

That story should be told as a story rather than as a document. The Fiji Rugby Union's own history page carries a heading called "The origins of the cibi" and prints nothing underneath it. What is not in doubt is that a dance came out of 1939 and has been performed before Fijian Test matches ever since.

What is also not in doubt is the shape of the moment. **A chief of Bau, descended from the man who ceded the country, leading a team of barefoot Fijians to an unbeaten record in the country that had already made rugby its own religion.** New Zealand's own game, the New Zealand chapter argues, never acquired a class character because the institutions carrying it had no interest in sorting anybody. Fiji's had not acquired one either — and Fiji's had held out against a colonial governor who was actively trying to install one.

### The men who did not come home

They came back to a war.

The historian Brij Lal's figure, cited in the scholarship on Fijian military migration, is that **8,000 Fijians joined the Allied forces in the Solomon Islands**, where they "gained a reputation for bravery." When that was over, Fiji sent **1,600 troops** to the Commonwealth forces in Malaya.

Keep those numbers in view, because this chapter is going to return to them repeatedly and they are the other half of everything that follows. Fiji is a small country whose most reliable export, from the 1940s onwards, has been **young men who are very good at physical work under discipline, far from home, for money that comes back to the village**. In the 1940s that meant the Solomons. In the 1950s it meant Malaya. From the 1960s it would mean the British Army, from 1978 United Nations peacekeeping, from 2003 private security work in Iraq — and, running alongside all of it, professional rugby.

They are not separate stories. They are one trade with several buyers, and the sport is simply the buyer that puts its purchases on television.

### The unions become one

In **1945** — the year the war ended — the union's chronology records that "**the Native Rugby Union had ceased to exist**." Put less delicately, and as the scholarship puts it: **the two separate unions merged.**

Thirty years after the Fiji Native Union was founded as an affiliate, Fijian rugby put itself back together into a single body.

It is worth stopping on the date, because of what it sits next to. The Methodist Church, which had split itself into Fijian and Indo-Fijian branches in 1901, **did not do the same thing**. It has run divided ever since. Fijian soccer, whose governing body had been the **Fiji Indian Football Association** since 1938, did not drop "Indian" from the regulator's title until **1961**, or open its national league to all races until **1962**.

Rugby got there first, by sixteen years, and it changed nothing about who played. **The institutional division was undone and the sporting division survived it**, because the division had never been rugby's to begin with. It belonged to the land law of 1874 and the plantation of 1879, and no sporting administrator in Suva was ever going to unpick that by passing a motion.

### Rescuing Australia

Fiji spent the next decade discovering that its rugby was a commercial asset in other people's countries.

A second tour of New Zealand in **1951** was a harder trip than 1939 — played fifteen, won eight, drew two, lost five. Then came Australia, and Australia turned out to need them.

The Fiji union's own history makes a striking claim about the **1952** tour: that it "helps the **Australian Rugby Union recover from the brink of bankruptcy**." That is the FRU's telling, and it is not corroborated elsewhere in this book's sources, so take it as the claim of an interested party. What is not in dispute is what surrounded it. **The Test series was drawn 1–1, in front of record crowds.** In **1954** Fiji went back, drew the series **1–1** again, and drew record crowds again.

The Australia chapter describes those years from the other side: an Australian union founded only in 1949, seventy-five years after the first colonial one, thin, poor, and losing every promising footballer it produced to a rugby league competition that could pay. Into that walked a touring side of Fijians who had been playing barefoot thirteen years earlier, and Australians bought tickets in numbers Australian rugby union had not seen.

Note the year of that second drawn series: **1954**. Remember it. It will be sixty-nine years before Fiji beat Australia again, and when they do it will be at a World Cup.

### What this generation leaves behind

Fiji comes out of this generation with the most romantic reputation in world rugby and no institutional standing at all.

It has an unbeaten tour of New Zealand that its union says nobody has matched. It has drawn two Test series with Australia. It has one union instead of two. It has a war record. It has, in Ratu Sir George Cakobau, a captain who will end up as head of state.

What it does not have is a seat at any table where decisions get made, a fixture list it controls, or a single shilling of its own. The next generation is the one in which Fiji becomes an independent country — and discovers that independence in the world and independence in rugby are two entirely different things.

## Generation 3: The Colony Leaves (1955–1976)

**10 October 1970.** Fiji becomes an independent country — ninety-six years to the day since the chiefs offered it to Queen Victoria.

Count what the rugby looks like on that morning. The Fiji Rugby Union's own account, taking stock the following year, describes a body administering **750 clubs and 10,000 players, across 300 islands, over 100,000 square miles of the Pacific**.

Seven hundred and fifty clubs, in a country of well under a million people. (Those are the union's own figures, and the arithmetic in them is worth noticing: seven hundred and fifty clubs and ten thousand registered players is thirteen men a club, which says something about how small and how numerous the village sides were.)

Set that number against Governor Im Thurn's memorandum of 1905, in which there were two classes of Fijians, the thinkers and the labourers, and a different education was desirable for each. Sixty-five years later his colony has produced one of the densest networks of rugby clubs per head of population anywhere, and it is not organised by class at all, because it is organised by **village**. The club is the settlement. The settlement is the church congregation. The chief is not a man from a different order of society who owns the club; he is the head of the kin group that *is* the club.

That is the answer to the question this chapter opened with, and it is worth stating flatly. **In Australia the school handed the boy to an old boys' club that existed precisely to keep him among people like himself. In Fiji the school handed the boy back to his village, and a village has nobody to sort itself against.** Queen Victoria School did not fail to produce great rugby players — it produced more of them than almost any institution in the Pacific. It failed to produce a class, because the thing waiting on the other side of the school gate was not a class system. It was a kinship system with a church in the middle of it.

Now go back, because between 1955 and 1970 Fiji had been finding out exactly how far a village game could travel on its own.

---

### The good years

**1957**: Fiji toured New Zealand again and beat the Māori twice, in Dunedin and in Wellington, then beat a strong Auckland side **38–17**.

**1963**: the Fiji Rugby Football Union dropped the second F and became simply the **Fiji Rugby Union**.

**1964**: Fiji toured Europe for the first time, and lost to **Wales 28–22** in a match that was, in the union's phrase, "talked about for years." Read the Wales chapter for what Wales was in 1964 — deep in the long winter between its two golden eras, three years from Gareth Edwards's debut, a country that would shortly convince itself its salvation lay in a coach. Beating them was still a considerable thing, and Fiji got within six.

**1970**: "A rampant Fijian side **destroy the Barbarians 29–9 at Gosforth**." The Barbarians in 1970 were not a scratch side; they were an invitational assembly of the best men in Britain and Ireland, and they were the fixture through which the four home unions took the temperature of visitors. Fiji did not merely beat them.

**1974**: the All Blacks came to Suva and needed a **last-minute try to survive 14–13**.

Across two decades a country with no professional structure, no full membership of the sport's governing body, no domestic competition anyone abroad had heard of and a national team assembled from village clubs took the Barbarians apart, came within six points of Wales and within one score of New Zealand.

And it converted none of it into anything.

### The president's diagnosis

The most valuable single document in this generation is not a match report. It is a warning issued by Fiji's own union president in **1973**, and it deserves to be read whole, because it is a Fijian administrator describing Fijian rugby from the inside, without an outsider's condescension anywhere in it.

**Ratu Sir Penaia Ganilau** — later the first President of the Republic of Fiji — wrote:

> "There is room for a great deal of work in improving grounds and other facilities… **administration is a constant problem in many unions** and I would like to see more former players putting their backs into the task… the **standard of refereeing has fallen since Independence** and clearly the Union will need to pay special attention to this… coaching too is in need of this… **only a determined drive to eliminate weakness and build on strengths can confirm Fiji as a world power in rugby.**"

Grounds. Administration. Refereeing. Coaching. Every single item on that list is still on the list in 2026, and this book has watched the same list appear, in almost the same words, in Georgia, in Romania, in Uruguay and in Chile.

It is the standard condition of a country that produces players faster than it can build institutions to hold them, and Ganilau saw it coming in the third year of independence.

### The ceiling, and the doorway

Two structural facts govern this generation, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is that Fiji had **no seat at the International Rugby Board**. The Board was a closed circle of the old unions, and it took Fiji in, along with the other outsiders, only in the mid-1980s — and Fiji went in, in the account of the scholars who have written most closely about Fijian rugby, "**due to pressure from the IRB**" rather than on its own initiative. (One study dates the admission to 1986; other accounts put the tranche of new members at 1987. The year is not settled and this chapter does not settle it.) Until then, Fiji's international programme consisted of whoever chose to invite them and whoever chose to stop by. That is not a fixture list. That is hospitality.

The second is that the doorway out was already open, and it was not a rugby doorway.

**Recruitment of Fijians into the British Army began in 1961/1962.** Fijian participation in United Nations peacekeeping began in **1978**, with the Interim Force in Lebanon, and never stopped. Both of those channels were running before a single Fijian was ever paid to play rugby, and both of them were doing exactly what professional rugby would later do: taking fit young men out of Fijian villages, sending them somewhere dangerous or distant, and routing the money home.

Amateurism, in this book, has usually been a weapon — the mechanism by which the English union excluded working men in 1886, the charge on which France was expelled in 1931, the bar that made a crossing to rugby league permanent in Sydney and in Wigan alike. In Fiji it was something else. **It was simply a ceiling with no ladder under it.** Nobody in Fiji needed to be excluded from the paid game, because there was no paid game, and the men who wanted to convert their bodies into remittances had two better-established options and took them.

### What this generation leaves behind

Fiji leaves the colonial era as a rugby country of astonishing depth and no institutional weight: seven hundred and fifty clubs, a president who has correctly diagnosed every one of its administrative weaknesses, no seat at the game's top table, and a national team that beats the Barbarians and then waits to be invited somewhere.

What it needs is a format that rewards exactly what it has — speed, handling, improvisation, small squads, cheap travel — and punishes exactly what it lacks: money, depth of preparation, and structure.

That format already exists. It was invented in a Scottish mill town in 1883 to raise funds for a club that had none, and in 1976 it arrives in Hong Kong.

## Generation 4: The Short Game, and the Coup (1977–1991)

**14 May 1987. Suva.**

Soldiers walk into the Fijian parliament and remove the government. The prime minister, **Timoci Bavadra**, elected a month earlier, is deposed. The officer leading the coup is **Lieutenant Colonel Sitiveni Rabuka**, third in command of the Royal Fiji Military Forces — and a rugby player.

**Eight days later, on 22 May, the first Rugby World Cup kicks off in New Zealand and Australia, and Fiji are in it.**

The organisers had assumed they would not be. As the New Zealand chapter records from the other side of the arrangement, the coup "raised real doubts about whether the Fijians could travel, and **Western Samoa was put on standby** in case they or anyone else defaulted."

Nobody defaulted. The Fijians got on the plane.

They lost to New Zealand, who were on their way to winning the thing, and they lost one more, and they beat one — and then something happened that is worth pausing over, because it is the most Fijian result in the history of the tournament. **Three teams finished level on two points in Pool 3: Fiji, Italy and Argentina.** Argentina had the better points difference. The tiebreaker was **tries scored**, and the table read Fiji six, Italy five, Argentina four.

Fiji went through to the quarter-finals of the first Rugby World Cup by one try, from a country under military rule, with a worse points difference than one of the sides they eliminated. **They had exactly one currency and they had just enough of it.**

France beat them **31–16** in the last eight. It did not much matter. Everything about that fortnight — the coup, the plane, the tries, the exit — is the shape of the next forty years of Fijian rugby, arriving all at once.

Now go back ten years, to a stadium in a British colony on the other side of the world.

---

### What Melrose built, and who ended up with it

The **Hong Kong Sevens** began in 1976. Fiji won the second one, in **1977**, beating **Marlborough 28–18** in the final.

Go back to the Scotland chapter for what they had just won.

Sevens was invented at **Melrose on 28 April 1883**, by a Border club six years old and short of money, as a way of drawing a paying crowd to a single afternoon. It was a **mill town's improvisation**: seven men a side, short halves, quick knockouts, more matches in an afternoon, a bigger gate. Everything about the format came from poverty, and the Scottish establishment regarded it for decades as a novelty played by the wrong sort of people in the wrong sort of towns.

Ninety-four years later, the format a poor Scottish club invented to pay its bills became the property of a poor Pacific archipelago, and stayed there.

The reasons are not mystical and it is worth resisting the temptation to make them so. Sevens rewards individual evasion, handling under pressure, improvisation and raw pace, and it punishes squad depth, structured phase play, forward specialisation and preparation budgets. It is played in tournaments, so a country can arrive with twelve players instead of thirty-five and go home with a trophy. **It is the only format in rugby in which having no money is not automatically decisive**, and Fiji found it first and understood it best.

By **1982** Fiji had begun a **fifteen-match winning streak** in the format that ran through to 1984. In **1984** they destroyed **New Zealand 26–0** in the Hong Kong final. In **1991** they beat a New Zealand side captained by **Zinzan Brooke** in the final, and the union's own history records it as the occasion of "the most amazing try in the tournament's history." In **1992** they beat New Zealand again, for what the union counts as a third Hong Kong title in a row.

For most of that run Fiji had no seat at the sport's governing body at all, and could not afford to send its fifteen-a-side team anywhere.

### The soldier and the sport

It is impossible to write the Fijian 1980s without the coups, and equally important not to reduce Fijian rugby to them.

**Rabuka's intervention of 14 May 1987** was followed by a second later that year, and by a constitution in 1990 that entrenched indigenous Fijian political primacy. The country that had been built in two parts by a benevolent governor in the 1870s discovered, a century on, that a society organised in ethnic compartments produces politics organised the same way. What followed — 1987 twice, then **2000**, then **2006** — gave Fiji four coups in nineteen years and, for long stretches, a government answerable to men in uniform.

Rugby sits inside that in a way that is uncomfortable and true. The game's institutional home in Fiji has always overlapped with the disciplined services — the sport arrived in 1884 in the custody of the **Native Constabulary**; the police club was one of the four founding clubs of the native competition in 1914; the military and the police have supplied players, administrators and patrons continuously ever since. The men who play are frequently the men who serve. Ratu Sir George Cakobau captained the 1939 tourists and served with the Fiji Military Forces through the war; Ratu Sir Penaia Ganilau ran the union in 1973 and ended as President of the Republic.

This is not a scandal and it is not a conspiracy. It is what happens when the same institutions — village, chiefly authority, church, and the uniformed services that recruit from all three — carry everything a young man in Fiji might do with his body.

But it does mean that in May 1987 a Fijian rugby squad flew out of a country where the army had just taken the parliament, and a fair number of them will have known the men who did it.

### 1991, and the bill for having no structure

The union's own account of the second World Cup is not defensive. It is brutal:

> "The second World Cup is a **disaster** for Fiji as they lose all three pool games and **come home early and embarrassed**."

Four years after the quarter-final. The same country, the same clubs, an almost undiminished stock of players, and no fifteen-a-side programme in between to hold any of it together. In 1987 they had been good enough on the only day that counted. In 1991 they were not, and nothing in Fijian rugby existed to explain why or to fix it.

Fiji's sevens team, in the same twelve months, beat New Zealand in the Hong Kong Sevens final.

That gap — brilliant in the short game, unreliable in the long one — would be diagnosed for the next thirty years as a failing of Fijian temperament. It was nothing of the kind. **It was a straightforward reading of which format a country with no money, no domestic professional competition and no fixture list can and cannot sustain.**

### What this generation leaves behind

Fiji ends this generation with a World Cup quarter-final, a humiliation, back-to-back Hong Kong Sevens titles with a third arriving within months, a seat at the IRB that arrived late and by somebody else's initiative, and a country that had two coups in a single year.

And in August 1995 the game will go open, worldwide, in a hotel in Paris — and every one of those seven hundred and fifty clubs will become a supplier to somebody else's league.

## Generation 5: Open, and the Leak (1992–2006)

**21 March 1997. Hong Kong airport.**

The Fijian squad comes through arrivals for the **Rugby World Cup Sevens**. Twenty-four nations have entered. Fiji's captain is a small fly half from Suva named **Waisale Serevi**, and years later he will describe the moment of walking in:

> "In 1997 we went to the World Cup in Hong Kong and getting into the airport with **our little bags** and [seeing] all the teams with a lot of big bags and all their equipment, **we were like schoolchildren**."

Two days later Fiji beat **South Africa 24–21** in the final and are world champions. Serevi finishes the tournament as its **top scorer with 117 points** and is named **player of the tournament**.

The little bags are not a charming detail. They are the entire economic position of Fijian rugby in the year rugby union became a professional sport, expressed in luggage.

Because the other thing about 1997 is what had happened two years earlier, and it is the hinge of this whole generation. In **1995** the game went open — in Paris, in August, a hundred years almost to the day after the George Hotel, as the England chapter tells it. And in **1995 Fiji did not qualify for the World Cup at all.**

Now go back and watch what opening the game actually did to a country of seven hundred and fifty village clubs.

---

### The moment the market arrived

Professionalism did not come to Fiji. Professionalism came for Fiji.

There was no Fijian professional competition to create in 1995, no Fijian club with a stadium and a television contract, no domestic league that could be converted into a paying one. What existed was a very large population of extremely good players, distributed across villages, playing for nothing, in a country with high unemployment and a remittance economy — sitting a short hop from two of the wealthiest rugby markets on earth, and a long one from two more.

What arrived was **buyers**.

By the time researchers first tried to count them, in work published in 2013, the estimate was **around 450 Fijian athletes involved in foreign competitions**; a year later the same scholars put it at **500 Fijian players in foreign leagues** — in New Zealand, Australia, France, England and Japan. In a country whose population was well under a million, roughly half a thousand men were playing rugby professionally somewhere else.

Not one of those transfers was illegitimate. Every one of them was a young man taking the best offer available to him, usually with his family's active encouragement, usually in order to send money home. That is the point. **There was no villain in this and there did not need to be one.** A country that had spent a century producing extraordinary numbers of rugby players and had never built the institutions to hold them met an open global market. The market did what markets do.

The consequence for the national team was structural and immediate. Fiji's best fifteen were scattered across four continents, contracted to clubs whose seasons did not align, released for Test windows at their employers' discretion, and reassembled a few times a year by a union that had been described by its own president in 1973 as having an administrative problem.

### Two countries, one union

The record of the next decade reads as though two different nations were playing under the same flag.

- **1999**: Fiji lose **28–19 to France in Toulouse** at the World Cup. The union's own history is not neutral about it — it opens the entry with "abysmal refereeing from Kiwi Paddy O'Brien" — which is worth recording not as a finding but as a symptom. A union with no representation on the bodies that appoint officials will experience every marginal decision as a verdict on its standing, and Fiji's did.
- **2000**: Fiji host an IRB Sevens tournament in Suva and lose the final **31–5** to Eric Rush's New Zealand.
- **2001**: Fiji are crowned **Pacific Rim champions**, beating **Samoa 28–17** in the final — played in **Tokyo**, which is its own small comment on where Pacific rugby's money lived.
- **2003**: at the World Cup in Australia, **Rupeni Caucaunibuca** — "the Bua Bullet" — becomes the most talked-about player at the tournament, and Fiji miss the quarter-finals by losing the decider **22–20 to Scotland**. The Scotland chapter tells that one from a Scottish side deep in its two-club trap. The margin was two points.
- **2005**: Fiji win the **Rugby World Cup Sevens** again, in Hong Kong, under the New Zealander **Wayne Pivac** — beating Argentina 22–14, Australia 31–5, England 24–19 and then New Zealand **24–19** in the final. The tournament produces **William Ryder**, who had been playing for **PWD Bure in Lautoka** and whose international debut this was.
- **2006**: Fiji take the **World Sevens Series title**, ending New Zealand's six-year hold on it, finishing **22 points clear of England**.

Nine years, two Sevens World Cups and a World Series title in the short game — and, in the long one, no World Cup at all in 1995 and two failures to reach the last eight, by nine points and by two.

### 2006: two beginnings and a fourth coup

Two things happened in Fijian rugby in 2006 that have not been given equal weight since, and should be.

The first is that **Fijian women played their first ever Test match**. In the opening game of the inaugural **Pacific Tri-Nations**, the **Fijiana** lost **15–27 to Samoa**, then beat Tonga **52–5**.

It is very late. Fiji had been playing men's international rugby for eighty-two years. Women had been playing the game informally in Fiji since the late 1980s, in the account of the researchers who followed them, with "**little institutional or community support**", low registration, limited resources, and — this is their finding, not an editorial flourish — "**negative social sanction from some sections of Fijian society**." The development of the women's game, they conclude, was for years "almost entirely attributed to the determination and sacrifice of the players, coaches and supporters."

In a country where rugby is described as the epitome of indigenous masculinity, the women's game had to be built by the women, against the same institutions that made the men's game unstoppable. Hold that thought; it pays off inside fifteen years.

The second thing that happened in 2006 was on **5 December**, when **Commodore Frank Bainimarama** removed the government. It was Fiji's **fourth coup in nineteen years**, and its consequences reached rugby quickly and from an unexpected direction, which the next generation will show.

### Ratuism, religion and rugby

By the end of this generation the scholarship on Fijian rugby had settled on a formulation that is worth putting on the page, because it is the most economical statement of what this chapter is about.

Yoko Kanemasu and Gyozo Molnar open one of their papers by observing that "**today's indigenous Fijian culture is often said to be represented by ratuism, religion and rugby**," and then draw the obvious inference: "that rugby is listed alongside **two powerful institutions** (ratuism and religion) in the life of indigenous Fijians is a clear indication of the central cultural importance of the sport."

*Ratuism* — the chiefly system. *Religion* — in practice, for most iTaukei, the Methodist Church that arrived in 1835 and split itself in two in 1901. And rugby, sitting beside them as a third pillar of the same structure.

This is the answer to Im Thurn, restated in the language of social science a century later. **Fijian rugby was never going to become a class possession, because it had been absorbed into the two institutions that organise indigenous Fijian life — and neither of those institutions sorts Fijians by class.** The chief is not a bourgeois. The congregation is not a club with a membership committee.

But there is a cost buried in the same sentence, and this chapter's second half is about paying it. **If rugby is one of the three things that hold a community together, then every man who is good at it is under an obligation to the community — and the only way to discharge that obligation, after 1995, is to leave.**

### What this generation leaves behind

Fiji leaves 2006 as the best sevens nation on earth, a fifteen-a-side side that has missed one World Cup entirely and two quarter-finals narrowly, a country on its fourth coup, and an exporter of roughly five hundred professional rugby players.

The next generation is the one where somebody counts what that is worth, in currency, and discovers that rugby is not even the largest of the trades Fiji runs in young men.

## Generation 6: The Body Trade (2007–2019)

**29 September 2007. La Beaujoire, Nantes.**

Fiji and Wales are playing the last match of Pool B, and it is winner-takes-all: whoever wins joins Australia in the quarter-finals and whoever loses goes home. Wales are a Six Nations country with a professional structure, four regions and a hundred and twenty-six years of Test rugby. Fiji have not been past a World Cup pool since the tournament in which they qualified on tries scored, twenty years earlier.

Fiji's captain, the flanker **Akapusi Qera**, scores inside the first fifteen minutes. At half-time Fiji lead **25–10**.

Then Wales come back at the scrum, and keep coming, and with seven minutes left **Martyn Williams** scores what looks like the try that ends it. Fiji are behind. It is Gareth Thomas's hundredth cap for Wales.

In the seventy-sixth minute the prop **Graham Dewes** goes over, and **Nicky Little** kicks the conversion, and Fiji win **38–34**.

World Rugby's own retrospective calls it, without qualification, "**Fiji's finest hour at the Rugby World Cup**." The Wales chapter tells the Welsh side of that evening: a defeat that ended a coaching regime and sent a country into another round of its recurring search for a saviour. Wales's own fly half, Stephen Jones, gave the reason afterwards, and it is the compliment the rugby world had been paying Fiji since the *Waikato Times* in 1939, without ever once letting it cost them anything: "Give them space and time and they move the ball well and have an offloading game and put you under pressure. They did that day and scored some wonderful tries."

From Nantes it looked like something else. It looked like the day the sport's oldest joke — *the Fijians are wonderful to watch, aren't they* — stopped being funny.

Eight days later, in Marseille, Fiji played South Africa in the quarter-final. **Vilimoni Delasau** and **Sireli Bobo** scored two tries in two minutes to draw it level at **20–20**. South Africa, who would win the tournament a fortnight later, then pulled clear and won **37–20**.

Now pull back, because between that quarter-final and the end of this generation, researchers finally did to Fijian rugby what nobody had done before: they measured it.

---

### The numbers

Here is what the export looked like, once somebody counted it.

In work published by the *Journal of Sport for Development* in 2017, **Rochelle Stewart-Withers, Koli Sewabu and Sam Richardson** assembled the figures. Fijian players, they note, "can be found in all levels of rugby from the top tiers (**France, Britain and New Zealand**) to lower tiers (**USA, Romania and Japan**)" — and there is a small jolt in reading *Romania* in that list, because Romania has a chapter of its own in this book, about a country that is itself a supplier. Fiji exports players to countries that are exporting players of their own.

The counts:

- **Up to 500 Fijian athletes hold professional contracts in foreign competitions**, on Kanemasu and Molnar's estimate.
- **More than 50 Fijian players in the top two divisions of French rugby alone.**
- In France, the number of professional Pacific Island athletes rose **179 per cent over a seven-year period**.
- And the money: **in 2006, rugby-generated remittances sent to Fiji were estimated at F$18.54 million — 11 per cent of all workers' remittances sent to the country that year.**

Eleven per cent of a national economy's inbound remittances, generated by men playing a game.

Then the mechanism, described without euphemism. French clubs, the authors write, moved "to establish **academies in Fiji**" as "attempts to procure the best young talent from the country to bolster their playing stocks." The young men "are offered what are considered to be lucrative contracts but are likely to be **entry-level academy contracts**." And then the sentence that ought to be quoted every time a European club is praised for its Pacific scouting network:

> "**Contracts can be poor or non-existent, so athletes are vulnerable and at risk of exploitation.**"

Against which the home union offered, in the same authors' summary: "There is **no regulatory framework** to guide, monitor and manage the development of athletes. There is **no proper database/system** in place to track athlete's profiles/statuses. Athletes report a **lack of confidence in home systems and trust in home union**."

Ratu Sir Penaia Ganilau had written down that administration was a constant problem in **1973**. Forty-four years later it was still the finding, and by now it had a price attached.

### The sentence that names this book

And then, in the same paper, this — offered as background, as a fact so obvious it needs no argument:

> "**rugby in Fiji is not bound by class … where even the poorest can participate**"

That is the title of this book, asserted in a peer-reviewed journal, about one country, as a settled matter.

It is worth being precise about what it does and does not mean. It does **not** mean that Fiji escaped the thing this book has been tracking. It means that in Fiji the institutions that carried the game — village, chief, church, constabulary, the school that handed the boy straight back to all four — had **no interest in sorting Fijians from one another**, and so no class ever formed inside the sport, even though a colonial governor tried to build one into existence in 1905.

What Fiji got instead was a **different axis of sorting, inherited whole from the colonial state**: not class but ethnicity, drawn by a land policy in 1874, populated by an indenture system from 1879, formalised by a mission board in 1901, and reproduced by two rugby unions in 1914 and 1915 and by a football association in 1938. Rugby is the iTaukei game. Soccer is the Indo-Fijian one. Rugby's institutional division was dissolved in **1945** and soccer's in **1961–62**, and neither dissolution changed anything, because the division had never belonged to the sports.

And then Fiji got a **third** thing, which is the subject of the rest of this generation: an axis that is neither class nor race, but **nation**. The men are not sorted within Fiji. They are sorted *out of* it.

### The other trade

Here is the part of the argument that Fijian rugby writing almost never makes, and that the Fijian scholarship makes without hesitation: **rugby is not Fiji's only body trade, and it is not even its largest.**

The work of Yoko Kanemasu and her colleagues on Fijian military labour migration lays out the parallel channels, and they run through the same villages, the same age cohort and the same remittance logic as the rugby.

**Peacekeeping.** Fiji joined the **UN Interim Force in Lebanon in 1978** and has never stopped; missions in Sinai, Iraq and the Golan Heights followed. By the researchers' figures, Fiji sends **1,040 peacekeeping personnel a year**, and — the detail that stops you — "**Fiji has sent more soldiers on a per capita basis on UN peacekeeping missions than any other nation**." The trade had earned the country "well over **US$300 million since 1978**."

**The British Army.** Recruitment of Fijians began in **1961/1962**, accelerated in the late 1990s, and peaked at an annual intake of **490 in 2001/2002**. In total, **2,740 Fijians joined the British Army between 1998/99** and the mid-2010s. Then it stopped: recruitment was **suspended in 2009 because of Fiji's post-coup political condition** — Bainimarama's takeover of December 2006 had consequences that reached a recruiting office in Britain — and the Army's own downsizing followed in 2011. **The 2014/2015 intake was ten men.**

**Private military companies.** From around **2003**, coinciding with the invasion of Iraq, British and American security firms began contracting Fijians directly. Interviewees in the study had worked for Global Risk, Control Risks, G4S, SIS, SOC, Total Defence Logistics, Triple Canopy, Titan, Olive Group, Kulak, EOD Technology and Reed. They were called security guards; many did convoy security, escorts and personal protection details, armed. **Fiji's Elections Office counted 4,000 Fijians in Iraq in 2006.** The average interviewee earned **US$2,216 a month**, in a range from **US$724 to US$7,000** — pro rata, roughly **three times Fiji's average annual household income**, which the study puts at about **US$8,252**.

The authors' own term for all of this is **"muscle trade"**, conducted on the terms of an "**unequal core–periphery military labour trade**."

Now put the two lists side by side.

A young man from a Fijian village in the first decade of this century could convert his body into remittances in three ways. He could go to a French club academy on an entry-level contract with no regulatory framework behind him. He could join the British Army. Or he could take a security contract in Iraq. **Same villages. Same age group. Same economics. Same core-and-periphery terms. Different buyers.**

Rugby is not a separate story from that. **Rugby is that story with a ball in it**, and the only reason it is the one people write about is that it is the one that is televised.

### Rio

**11 August 2016. Deodoro Stadium, Rio de Janeiro.**

Rugby has returned to the Olympic Games after **ninety-two years** — in the sevens format that a Border club invented to pay its bills and a Pacific archipelago perfected. In the men's final, Fiji play Great Britain.

At half-time it is **29–0**.

Tries by the captain **Osea Kolinisau**, by **Jerry Tuwai**, by **Jasa Veremalua**, by **Leone Nakarawa**, by **Vatemo Ravouvou**. Dan Norton scores for Britain to make the final score **43–7**, which does not alter the character of the afternoon in the slightest.

It is **Fiji's first Olympic medal, of any colour, in any sport, ever**. The coach is **Ben Ryan**, an Englishman who had been let go by his own country's sevens programme and had taken the Fiji job partly on instinct; he had already delivered two World Series titles, and he would be made a **Companion of the Order of Fiji**, the country's highest honour.

Fiji declared a **national public holiday**. And the **Reserve Bank of Fiji** issued a commemorative banknote in the denomination of **seven dollars** — the only seven-denomination note in the world.

Look at what that gold medal actually was, institutionally. A country with no professional domestic competition, no money, an administration its own president had criticised forty-three years earlier, and half a thousand of its best players under contract to foreign employers, won an Olympic title — **in the one format where none of those disadvantages is decisive**, and where twelve players and a coach with an idea are enough.

The Australia chapter notes the ninety-two-year gap from the other end. The last Olympic rugby gold before Rio had been won in 1924; the one Australia won at White City in 1908 belonged to a side that had fourteen of its number expelled the following year for taking money. Fiji's was the first men's rugby gold of the modern era, and by a distance the smallest country ever to have won one.

### Kamaishi

**25 September 2019. Kamaishi Recovery Memorial Stadium, Japan.**

Fiji lose to **Uruguay, 30–27**.

The Uruguay chapter tells this from the winning side and tells it beautifully: a Uruguayan team of part-timers, produced by Uruguayan schools, beating a Fijian side of household names in a stadium built on ground the sea had taken in 2011.

From the Fijian side there is nothing to soften. It was the first upset of the tournament and it was the clearest possible statement of what the previous twenty-four years had produced: a national team drawn from five hundred professionals scattered across four continents, assembled a handful of times a year, without a domestic professional competition of its own, losing to a country with a small fraction of Fiji's playing population.

Nobody in Fiji needed the result explained. The Fiji Rugby Union had known the diagnosis since 1973 and the researchers had published the mechanism in 2017. What was missing was not knowledge. It was a **structure that kept Fijian players playing professional rugby in Fiji**.

### What this generation leaves behind

Fiji ends 2019 with an Olympic gold medal, a banknote, a World Cup quarter-final twelve years behind it, a defeat by Uruguay, and an export economy in young men that runs through French academies, British barracks and Iraqi convoy routes.

What it has never had, at any point in a hundred and thirty-five years, is a professional team of its own.

That arrives in 2022, and it has to be based in another country.

## Generation 7: The Boat (2020–2026)

**4 July 2026. Cardiff City Stadium, Wales.**

The Flying Fijians run out for a **home** match.

They are on the other side of the planet from Fiji. The stadium belongs to a Welsh football club and holds 32,500. The opposition is Wales. This is the first of Fiji's **three home fixtures** in the inaugural **Nations Championship** — the competition that finally, in the hundred and forty-second year since a game of rugby was played at Ba, put Fiji at the same table as the Six Nations and the Rugby Championship.

All three of Fiji's home matches in that first championship were played in Britain. The Fiji Rugby Union's own announcement, in February 2026, was headlined "**Flying Fijians Set to Make History with Three Blockbuster Nations Championship Home Games**", and explained that "the team's '**home**' fixtures will take fans on a **unique journey across the Northern Hemisphere**." Cardiff on 4 July, Liverpool on 11 July, Edinburgh on 18 July.

The union's board chairman, **John Sanday**, said of the third one: "For our third home game on 18 July, we step into one of rugby's most iconic arenas; Murrayfield Stadium, to face Scotland in a **rivalry that has tested us for more than four decades**."

Twelve months earlier, Fiji had beaten Scotland in Suva.

Now go back six years, because between 2020 and 2026 Fiji did more to build its own game than in the previous fifty — and still ended up playing its home matches in somebody else's country.

---

### The boat

The **Fijian Drua** — *drua* is the great double-hulled sailing canoe of Fiji — was founded in **2017** to play in Australia's National Rugby Championship, and won it in **2018**. Then the NRC was abolished at the end of 2019 and the team very nearly vanished.

It was rescued by admission to **Super Rugby Pacific for the 2022 season**, alongside **Moana Pasifika**. It was the first time a Pacific Island nation had a team at the top level of southern-hemisphere club rugby. For a country that had exported players continuously since 1995, it was the single most important institutional event in its rugby history: a professional contract that a Fijian could sign **without leaving**.

There is a detail about that first season that belongs in this chapter and nowhere else. Because of COVID travel restrictions, **the Fijian Drua spent their inaugural season based in Australia**, at Lennox Head in New South Wales.

Fiji's first professional rugby team played its first season in another country.

By the seasons that followed the team was playing in Fiji, in front of Fijian crowds, and the **Fijiana Drua** — the women's side — had done something the men's team had not: it had won its competition. They took the **Super W** title in their debut season of **2022**, beating the NSW Waratahs **32–26** in the final, and defended it in **2023**, beating the Queensland Reds **38–30**. The women's game, built for three decades against social sanction and without institutional support, arrived at professionalism and immediately won the thing.

### Tokyo, twice — and then Paris

**28 July 2021. Tokyo Stadium.** In the men's sevens final, delayed a year by the pandemic, Fiji beat **New Zealand 27–12** — tries from **Meli Derenalagi**, **Sireli Maqala**, **Jiuta Wainiqolo**, **Asaeli Tuivuaka** and **Waisea Nacuqu** — and became **back-to-back Olympic champions**.

Two Games, two golds, in a country that had won no Olympic medal at all before 2016.

And in the same week, at the same stadium, the **Fijiana** beat **Great Britain 21–12** to take the **bronze medal** in the women's tournament. **Alowesi Nakoci** crossed twice and **Reapi Uluinasau** ran half the field to seal it; the captain was **Rusila Nagasau**. On the way there they had beaten Canada, who took bronze at Rio, and knocked out **Australia**, who had taken the gold there — the Australia that had beaten Fiji **36–0** at Rio five years earlier, as the Australia chapter records. Fiji's women had finished eighth at those Games.

It was the team's first medal at any major tournament, and it made them **the first Fijian women to win an Olympic medal**.

Fifteen years earlier, the Fijiana had not played a Test match.

Three years after that they went to Paris to make it three, and did not.

**France beat Fiji 28–7 in the final.** **Antoine Dupont** — France's best fifteen-a-side player, moved across to sevens for the summer — was at the middle of it. It was **the end of Fiji's unbeaten run at the Olympic Games**: two tournaments, two golds, and then a silver. The **Fijiana** did not medal at all.

That is easier to read as a decline than it deserves to be. Fiji had owned this format for as long as they had because it was the one in which having no money was not decisive — and by 2024 a country with a great deal of money had taken it seriously enough to move its finest player into it for a season and build a summer around winning it. **The game a Border club invented because it was poor had become worth a rich country's trouble.** Fiji did not get worse. The company got better.

### The rule that changed

In **November 2021**, World Rugby amended **Regulation 8**, with effect from **1 January 2022**. A player who had represented one country could now change union — **once** — after a **36-month stand-down**, provided he or she was born in the new country or had a parent or grandparent born there, and subject to approval.

It was reported at the time as the most significant eligibility change since the game went open, and it was aimed unmistakably at the Pacific. For as long as the rules had existed they had been a one-way valve: a Fijian capped by New Zealand or Australia was gone. From 2022 the valve worked both ways.

It is worth being honest about the scale of it. It did not reverse the trade; it made a small proportion of it reversible. But it was the first rule in the history of the sport that was written with Fiji, Samoa and Tonga as its intended beneficiaries rather than its incidental casualties, and that is not nothing.

### Sixty-nine years

**17 September 2023. Stade Geoffroy-Guichard, Saint-Étienne.**

Fiji beat **Australia 22–15** at the Rugby World Cup.

It is Fiji's **first win over Australia in sixty-nine years** — the last one having come in **1954**, on the second of those tours that drew record Australian crowds and which the Fiji union has always claimed helped save Australian rugby from bankruptcy. The Wallabies of 2023 would go out in the pool stage for the first time in their history, and the Australia chapter carries this defeat as part of how it happened.

**Josua Tuisova** scored the try; **Simione Kuruvoli** kicked the points. Afterwards Tuisova said he had told the players at half-time to "**empty the tank**." The coach, **Simon Raiwalui**, said: "I'm **emotionally drained** at the moment. I'm really proud of the boys." The captain, **Waisea Nayacalevu**, said what he had told them before kick-off: "**today is our final.**"

It was not their final. They had lost narrowly to Wales earlier in the pool (**32–26**), and they would beat Georgia **17–12** and then lose to **Portugal 24–23** — and still go through, on the losing bonus point that a one-point defeat brings with it.

**15 October 2023. Stade de Marseille.** The quarter-final, against England.

England led **24–10** going into the final quarter. Then **Peni Ravai** went over on sixty-five minutes and **Vilimoni Botitu** crossed soon after, and the scores were level. **Owen Farrell** landed a drop goal, and then a fifth penalty, and England survived **30–24**. Farrell scored twenty of England's thirty points.

Sixteen years after Nantes, Fiji had come within one score of a World Cup semi-final.

### Suva, and then Murrayfield

**12 July 2025. Suva.**

Fiji beat **Scotland 29–14** — four tries, and a penalty try when Darcy Graham's second yellow card became a red. It was only Fiji's third win over Scotland in eleven attempts, and **all three had come in Suva**. Scotland had won the other eight, including **57–17** in Edinburgh eight months before.

That is the Fijian home record in a sentence. At home, on grass in Suva, in front of a Fijian crowd, they are one of the hardest fixtures in the sport. The problem has never been the team. The problem has always been how rarely anyone comes.

Which brings the chapter back to July 2026.

Fiji were admitted to the **Nations Championship** as one of its twelve teams: the six of the Six Nations, the four of the Rugby Championship, **Japan and Fiji**. After a hundred and forty-two years, Fiji had a guaranteed annual fixture list against the best countries in the world — the single thing Ratu Sir Penaia Ganilau's generation, and every generation since, had lacked.

And then the July window came, and Fiji's three home matches were played at **Cardiff City Stadium**, at the **Hill Dickinson Stadium** in Liverpool, and at **Murrayfield**.

- **4 July 2026, Cardiff:** **Wales 39, Fiji 24.**
- **11 July 2026, Liverpool:** **England 73, Fiji 8.** Henry Pollock scored a hat-trick; Fiji played much of it with fourteen men after a red card; it ended a five-Test losing run for England.
- **18 July 2026, Murrayfield:** **Scotland 33, Fiji 17.**

The competition's own design makes this stranger, not less strange. The Nations Championship divides its twelve into a European pool and a "Rest of the World" pool, and the arrangement is reciprocal: **in July the European teams travel south, and in November the southern teams travel to Europe.** That is what happened elsewhere. The Australia chapter closes on Australia beating Italy **57–10 in Perth on 18 July 2026** — the same afternoon Fiji were at Murrayfield. Australia's July fixtures were played in Australia, because Australia has stadiums that pay.

Fiji took the money and moved its half of the bargain to the other hemisphere.

The Murrayfield match is the one to sit with, because for forty minutes it was the other result. Scotland scored first through Jonny Gray. Then **Tevita Ikanivere** went over, and **Selestino Ravutaumada**, and then **Elia Canakaivata** scored a third **while Fiji had a man in the sin-bin** — and Fiji led **17–7** at half-time at Murrayfield.

Then Scotland emptied their bench. **Pierre Schoeman** came on and scored. **Jamie Dobie** put Scotland ahead. **Scott Cummings** got the bonus-point try at seventy-four minutes and Dobie scored again at the end.

Fiji had the better forty minutes. Scotland had the better squad, which is a different asset, and it is the asset that a country exporting five hundred professionals has never been able to buy.

### Where things stood, in the middle of 2026

On **Monday 15 July 2026**, three days before Murrayfield, the World Rugby men's rankings read: **South Africa, New Zealand, Ireland, France, England, Scotland, Argentina, Australia, Fiji, Italy.**

**Ninth in the world.** Above Italy, a Six Nations country. Above **Wales**, sitting twelfth — outside the top ten, and eleven days past beating Fiji in Cardiff. Fiji had lost two Nations Championship matches and stayed in the top ten anyway, which is a fair statement of where the country's rugby had got to: securely, permanently good, and structurally unable to convert that into anything on a Saturday against a squad with a bench.

Samoa were nineteenth and Tonga twentieth, both having dropped a place — the two countries whose chapters come next in this section, and whose position is the harder version of Fiji's.

In the women's rankings the **Fijiana** stood **thirteenth**, with Samoa fifteenth and Tonga twenty-eighth.

**Senirusi Seruvakula** was Fiji's acting head coach heading into the **Pacific Nations Cup**, to be played in Japan from 12 to 19 September 2026. Beyond that lay **Rugby World Cup 2027 in Australia**, the first twenty-four-team tournament, where Fiji were drawn in **Pool C with Argentina, Spain and Canada**.

### What this chapter leaves behind

Start where the chapter started: with a governor, in 1905, who believed there were two classes of Fijians and built a school to keep them apart.

He got the school. Queen Victoria School opened at Nasinu on 3 January 1907 with thirty-two boys, and by 1910 was admitting only the sons of chiefs, on the headmaster's insistence and on the headmaster's stated view that a chief's son was more intelligent than a commoner's. It was modelled, at the Council of Chiefs' own request, on the English public schools — the exact institution that in England produced the amateur laws of 1886, and in Sydney and Brisbane produced two rival football codes divided by class and kept that way for a century.

In Fiji it produced the first winner of the Deans Trophy and one of the two greatest rugby nurseries in the Pacific, **and no class whatsoever**. By 1971 the union was administering seven hundred and fifty clubs across three hundred islands, and by 2017 a peer-reviewed journal could state, as unexamined background, that rugby in Fiji "is not bound by class … where even the poorest can participate."

The instrument was identical. The outcome was the opposite. And the reason is the thing standing on the other side of the school gate: in Sydney an old boys' club that existed to keep a man among his own kind, and in Fiji a village in which the club, the congregation and the kin group are the same body of people, with a chief at the head of it who is not a member of a different class but the head of the family. **Rugby has no class — institutions do. Fiji is where an institution tried to install one and the institutions underneath it would not carry it.**

But something was sorted, and it was sorted before rugby ever arrived, and it has never been undone.

Sir Arthur Gordon protected indigenous Fijians from the plantations by importing **60,965 indentured labourers** between 1879 and 1916, and produced a colony organised in two ethnic compartments. The Methodist mission split itself into Fijian and Indo-Fijian branches in **1901**. Rugby founded a European union in **1913** and a Native Union in **1915**, affiliated, not merged. Soccer's controlling body was the **Fiji Indian Football Association** from **1938**. Rugby put itself back together in **1945**; soccer dropped "Indian" from the title in **1961** and opened its league to all races in **1962**.

Neither dissolution changed who plays what. **In 2026, rugby is still the iTaukei game and soccer is still the Indo-Fijian one** — and not one rugby administrator in Suva ever had to lift a finger to keep it that way, because the division was never rugby's. It belonged to a land policy and a mission board, and sport merely lay down inside the shape they left.

That is the correction this chapter makes to the rest of the book. **An institution does not have to be a sporting institution to give a sport its character.** England's class line was drawn by the RFU. South Africa's colour line was drawn by the state, through rugby. Fiji's was drawn by a governor with good intentions and a shortage of labour, fifty years before the first Test match, and rugby simply inherited it.

And then there is the third sorting, which is the one still running.

Fiji's men are not divided from each other. They are divided from Fiji. Five hundred professional players abroad; fifty-plus in the top two divisions of French rugby; eleven per cent of the country's remittances generated by rugby alone in a single year; French academies on Fijian soil offering entry-level contracts to boys, with a home union that has no database and no regulatory framework, in a country whose own athletes report a lack of trust in it. And running in exact parallel, out of the same villages: **1,040 peacekeepers a year, 2,740 men into the British Army, 4,000 Fijians in Iraq in 2006**, and a body of scholarship that calls the whole thing what it is — a **muscle trade**, on core-and-periphery terms.

Fiji has beaten Wales at a World Cup, beaten Australia after sixty-nine years, come within one score of a World Cup semi-final, and won consecutive Olympic gold medals in a format a Scottish mill town invented because it had no money. It has a professional team of its own at last, a women's professional team that won its competition in its first season, and a women's sevens side with an Olympic medal. In July 2026 it was ninth in the world, and admitted to the sport's top table for the first time.

And its three home matches that month were played in Cardiff, Liverpool and Edinburgh.

**The country that exports its players had, by 2026, exported its home ground.** Nobody forced it: Fiji went, because the fixtures were worth more than the venue, and because the calculation that sends a nineteen-year-old to a French academy and a thirty-year-old to a convoy route outside Baghdad is the same calculation, and Fijian rugby has been making it, correctly and at a price, since the game went open.

Samoa and Tonga are next, and they are making it too, from further down the ranking and with less to sell.
