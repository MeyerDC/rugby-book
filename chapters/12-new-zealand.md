# New Zealand

## Generation 0: The Game in Somebody's Luggage (1870–1888)

**Saturday, 14 May 1870. The Botanical Gardens, Nelson.**

Two teams take the field to play a code of football that almost nobody present has played before. On one side, the Nelson Football Club. On the other, the boys of Nelson College, who have been learning the rules for a matter of days from a young man who learned them himself in a school on the edge of London. The club's captain, a watchmaker's son named **Alfred Drew**, kicks a goal — the first points scored in a rugby match in New Zealand.

It is the founding scene of the most successful institution in the history of the sport, and it does not look like one. There is no union to sanction it, no competition it belongs to, no press to speak of, and no particular reason to think the fixture will be repeated. The club had spent the previous season playing something else entirely.

---

Go back a year and the Nelson club is not a rugby club at all.

A "Football Club" had been formed in the town in **1868**, though no record survives of it playing anybody. In **1869** it reformed as the **Nelson Football Club** and played against Nelson College — and what the two sides played, in the New Zealand Rugby Museum's own description, was **"a hybrid mix of soccer and Victorian Rules."**

Readers of this book have met that sentence before. On the other side of the world, in the same decade, the oldest club in France was playing **"la combination"** — a deliberate mixture of rugby and association football that its own statutes protected for twenty-two years, because an undivided club was stronger than a divided one. Nelson in 1869 and Le Havre in 1872 were doing the same thing for the same reason: in a small town with one pool of players, you play whatever gets a game on.

What changed it was a nineteen-year-old coming home. **Charles John Monro** had attended Nelson College from 1863 to 1865 and then been sent to London to finish his education at **Christ's College, Finchley**, where he was introduced to the rugby rules. He returned **early in 1870**, joined the Nelson club, and suggested the players try them. His father was **Sir David Monro**, a politician — the family had standing, which is presumably part of why the suggestion was taken seriously.

But he could not simply produce an opponent. To get a match at all, Monro had to go to the headmaster of Nelson College, **Frank Simmons** — himself an old boy of Rugby School — and offer **"to teach the pupils the rules and arrange a game against the club."** Two men who had been taught the game in England, teaching it to everyone else in town so that a fixture could exist.

New Zealand's founding myth, then, is a schoolmaster and a teenager coaching both sides. And the country's rugby museum is at pains to keep it that modest. **"Monro has been credited as the founder of rugby in New Zealand,"** its history reads, **"but it is more appropriate to credit him for introducing the game and as co-founder."**

That is worth pausing on, because this book has spent eleven chapters prising founding stories apart — the Webb Ellis legend certified by a committee of Old Rugbeians a month before the schism of 1895, the Iquique origin that no document supports, Le Havre's "this is false!" New Zealand is the one country in the book whose own custodians got there first. Nobody in Nelson has ever needed the story to be grander, because nothing in New Zealand rugby's later history depends on it.

### The jeweller, the bank clerk and the politician's son

The interesting question is not who introduced the game. It is how it got out of Nelson.

The answer is that it went in people's luggage. The three men at the centre of the Nelson club were **Monro**, **Drew** — the chairman and captain, a jeweller and watchmaker's son who had joined the family business — and **Robert Tennent**, the secretary-treasurer, who had joined the Bank of New South Wales as a sixteen-year-old clerk in 1865. Later in that first season Tennent wrote to Monro, who was in Wellington, asking him to arrange a fixture there. Monro **"rounded up some local footballers, taught them the rugby rules"**, and the Nelson team crossed Cook Strait to play at **Petone on 12 September 1870** — Nelson winning, Drew scoring first again, and the match standing as **the first ever played between two districts**, the ancestor of every provincial fixture in the country's history.

Then the two of them changed jobs, and the game went with them.

Drew moved to **Wanganui in 1871** to start a jewellery and watchmaking business with his brother; **the following year he co-founded rugby in the town and captained the club side**, and from Wanganui the game spread into **Manawatu in 1876**. In 1874 he moved again, to New Plymouth, and **"before the 1874 winter had passed he had established rugby in the town and captained the Taranaki club."** Within five years, one watchmaker had carried the game from Nelson to Wellington, Wanganui and Taranaki. Tennent, meanwhile, was **promoted in 1874 to manage a new Bank of New South Wales branch at Patea** — and became **a founding member of the Patea Football Club**. A bank transfer founded a rugby club.

Note what is absent. No governing body sent these men anywhere. No school system propagated the game down a hierarchy of feeder institutions, as it did in England, and no expatriate commercial enclave held it as a private amenity, as it did in Montevideo and Valparaíso. The carriers were a provincial tradesman and a bank employee moving around a young country for work, and they took football with them the way they took their tools.

There is one familiar mechanism, though. Drew **represented Nelson at cricket**; Tennent played club cricket and was **secretary-treasurer of the Nelson Cricket Club**. The same summer institution that incubated both football codes across South America — that produced the Montevideo Cricket Club, and Argentina's first clubs, and Chile's — is sitting underneath the first rugby club in New Zealand too. It is the most reliable fact in this entire book: wherever the British went, the winter game came out of the cricket club.

### "Banks and Lawyers" versus "All Corners"

By **1873** the game had reached Auckland, and by **1875** it was being played in Otago, Canterbury, Hawke's Bay, Marlborough and on the West Coast. What it looked like in those years is preserved in the newspaper reports, and it is not what the English game looked like in the same decade.

Auckland's first season, 1873, produced a "scratch" game on **3 May** and then a series of fixtures whose team-sheets tell you everything about how the colony sorted itself. When full sides could not be raised, **"Thirteen Colonials" played "Eight Outsiders"** — the *Daily Southern Cross* reporting that **"the new rules seem to have been well studied by the players."** Later that winter came **"Civilians" against "Volunteers"**, and **"Colonials" against "The World"**, and on 5 July a **"Heavy-Weights" against "Light-Weights"** match, in which **"players weighing over 10 st. 10 lbs. were classed 'Heavy-Weights.'"** The categories available to an Auckland footballer in 1873 were: how much you weighed, whether you were in the militia, and whether you were born here.

Then there is Greymouth. On the West Coast — gold and coal country, the closest thing New Zealand has to a coalfield — a notice ran in *The Grey River Argus* on **Friday 9 April 1875**: **"A meeting of those interested in the formation of a Football Club for the approaching season will be held at Gilmer's Hotel, on Saturday, at 8 p.m."** The club opened its season on **1 May**, and the *Argus* reported a day so fine **"that it was difficult to believe one's self in the Southern Hemisphere"**, sides picked by Mr Newton and Mr Ahearn, and **"the best of feeling manifested throughout, everyone taking his 'spills' in good part."**

And among the club's fixtures that season: a match against the local **Fire Brigade**, and a game, played over two days, of **"Banks and Lawyers" versus "All Corners."**

Hold that against the English chapter. In **1875**, in England, banks and lawyers versus all corners was not a fixture. It was the social order, and within eleven years the Rugby Football Union would write it into law — the 1886 amateur regulations, drafted to ensure that men who worked with their hands could not be compensated for the time it cost them to play, and the schism of 1895 that followed when twenty-two northern clubs would not accept it. The same code, the same decade, the same empire, the same rulebook posted out from Twickenham's predecessors. On the West Coast of the South Island it produced a two-day novelty match between the professions and everybody else, arranged for the amusement of both.

**Nothing in the rules did that.** The rules were identical. What differed was that there was no institution in New Zealand with an interest in keeping the two sides of that fixture apart — no ancient schools with a monopoly on the game, no metropolitan clubs defining themselves against the towns, no union that had inherited the sport as property and needed to defend it. The game arrived before the social machinery that might have sorted it, and by the time any machinery existed the game already belonged to everybody.

### The letter that had nowhere to go

Which was fine, until somebody from outside tried to write to "New Zealand."

In **1881** the **Southern Rugby Union** in Sydney — renamed the New South Wales Rugby Union a decade later — decided to invite a New Zealand side to tour Australia the following year. There was no national body to invite, so the invitation went to the province with the most convenient address: the **Wellington Rugby Union**. And there it stopped. Wellington's secretary, **Arthur Bate**, as the Rugby Museum records, **"had trouble in interesting the Canterbury and Otago unions in the proposal. Bate then wrote to Sydney advising of his lack of progress and suggested that the New South Welshmen might instead prefer to visit New Zealand."**

The first international invitation in New Zealand's rugby history failed because nobody could speak for the country.

So the traffic ran the other way. New South Wales sailed from Sydney on **Thursday 31 August 1882** — sixteen men, three having dropped out at the last moment, captained by a **player-manager, Ted Raper**, with one of the forwards, **R. Thallon**, serving as secretary. Their ship ran aground coming up Auckland harbour. On **9 September** they opened a seven-match visit against Auckland Provincial Clubs at the **Auckland Domain**, played over **four half-hour spells**, under a scoring system that gave **two points for a try, three for a conversion and four for any other goal**. They were the first overseas team to tour New Zealand, and they had come because the country they wanted to play could not organise itself into a fixture.

### Two men and a team

Two years later New Zealand answered, and the answer was arranged by private individuals, because there was still nobody else to do it.

**"With no national rugby body,"** the record states, **"Dunedin businessman Samuel Sleigh and Christchurch barrister William Millton (also a leading player) took upon themselves the arranging of the tour."** They assembled a squad with **"more or less equal representation from the four provincial unions then in existence"** — Auckland, Wellington, Canterbury and Otago — and immediately discovered the constraint that would define amateur rugby in this country for a century: **a number of the men selected withdrew, "mostly through work requirements."** Nineteen players finally made the eight-match tour. Sleigh managed it; Millton captained it.

Getting them onto the boat was its own comedy. One selected player, **Warbrick**, missed the steamer carrying the Auckland contingent to Wellington and had to sail separately, direct to Sydney. Another, **James O'Donnell of Invercargill**, was **arrested on a fugitive warrant at Clinton** on his way to join the team — the warrant taken out by **local tradespeople to whom he was indebted**, who had heard he was going to Australia and drawn the obvious conclusion. He was returned to Invercargill; none of his creditors turned up in court; he was released, and continued his journey to Wellington.

That is the first team ever assembled to represent New Zealand: short of men who could not get leave from work, and one player released by a magistrate because his creditors did not appear. Set it beside the RFU's regulations of two years later — an elaborate legal apparatus for ensuring that no working man could ever be reimbursed for the wages a match cost him — and the distance between the two rugbies is already unbridgeable, and nobody in New Zealand has yet noticed there is a distance.

The side's first outing was at home. A match against a Wellington XV set for Wednesday **21 May 1884** was postponed **"owing to the boisterous weather and greasy nature of the ground"**, and played the next day on a sloppy pitch at **Newtown Park** in two 25-minute spells. New Zealand won **9–0**: Ryan potted a goal after ten minutes, **Roberts** scored shortly before half-time when the local fullback could not gather a failed shot at goal, and Ryan converted. The press singled out **Taiaroa, Ryan, Allan and Roberts** — and two of the tourists, Ned Davy and Edward Millton, turned out for Wellington to make up the numbers. Afterwards the team **"were escorted to the wharf by members of the rugby fraternity to join the steamer Hauroto, which had been held until the completion of the match to allow the team to embark."**

A steamer held at the wharf for a football match. It is the first sign in this chapter of what the game would become to the country, and it happened before the country had a rugby union at all.

Two details from that team point forward. The fern stitched on the jersey **Harry Roberts** wore in 1884 — the museum still holds it — was **gold**, not silver, and the shirt was not black. And among the most prominent players in New Zealand's first representative match was a Māori, **Taiaroa**, four years before a largely Māori team would tour Britain and give this country the colours it has worn ever since.

### What this generation leaves behind

A game with no owner.

By 1888 rugby is played from Auckland to the West Coast; it has four provincial unions, a touring history in both directions, and a representative side that has been abroad. It has no national governing body, no national competition, and no agreed authority over who may wear the colours or arrange a fixture — because every attempt to organise it above the level of the province has so far been made by whoever happened to feel like it: a union secretary in Wellington, a businessman in Dunedin, a barrister in Christchurch.

That vacuum is about to be filled twice over, from opposite directions, by two ventures that could hardly be less alike. In **1888** a privately promoted, largely Māori team will sail for Britain on a scale nobody has attempted before, and will come home having invented the black jersey, the silver fern and the idea that a team from New Zealand is worth crossing the world to watch. And in **1892**, in a Wellington hotel, delegates will finally constitute a national union — a meeting that ends with two of the country's strongest provinces voting against it and walking out of the room.

The country that had no institution is about to acquire one. The argument about who the game actually belongs to starts the moment it does, and it has not finished yet.

## Generation 1: The Natives (1888–1902)

**Wednesday 3 October 1888. Richmond, on the edge of London.**

A team from New Zealand takes the field against Surrey, and before the kick-off the visitors line up and perform a **haka** — the first ever seen in Britain from a side representing this country. They are dressed entirely in black, with a badge on the chest carrying goal posts, a ball, a fern leaf and the motto **"Play up New Zealand."** An English reporter, reaching for a description, writes that the black **"in the case of the Maoris, with their dusky hue of face and hands, gives them a rather sombre aspect."**

The crowd is enormous, or perhaps it isn't; contemporary estimates range from five thousand to fifty thousand, which tells you how unprepared everybody was for the interest. Nearly the entire Australian cricket team turns up to watch. The Rugby Football Union's grandees, who had failed to meet the tourists at Fenchurch Street, attend in numbers to make up for it. **Goldsmith** scores the first try by a New Zealander in Britain; **Ellison** adds a second; **McCausland** kicks the conversion.

The referee is **George Rowland Hill**, secretary of the Rugby Football Union. Relations between him and the tourists are recorded as amicable throughout. Four months later, in the most famous match of the tour, they will not be.

---

Everything New Zealand rugby is recognised by in the world — the black jersey, the silver fern, the haka, the idea that a team from this country is worth crossing the planet to see — arrived in a single fourteen-month venture that no national body sanctioned, organised or paid for, **because no national body existed.**

The New Zealand Native team played **over a hundred rugby matches, and eight games of Victorian Rules**, across Great Britain, Australia and New Zealand between **June 1888 and August 1889**. Its own museum calls it, without much hedging, **"the most remarkable tour in rugby history."** Fifty-two matches were arranged in England, Ireland and Wales alone. The team was raised by **Joe Warbrick** — the man who, four years earlier, had missed the steamer to the 1884 tour — and it was overwhelmingly Māori.

It began at **Napier on 23 June 1888**, against Hawke's Bay, before the party had even fully assembled. The *Daily Telegraph* devoted two issues to the match, and buried in its report is one of the most consequential sentences in the history of the sport:

> **"The local men were resplendent in black and white bars, while the Maoris wore black with a silver fern tree on their breasts."**

Hold that date. **June 1888.** The New Zealand Rugby Football Union did not yet exist; it would be founded in April 1892, and would vote to adopt black jerseys with a silver fern in April 1893. The colours that the world now reads as the property of a national institution were on the chests of a privately promoted Māori touring side **four years and ten months before that institution voted to take them up.**

They travelled by sea, which meant they played wherever the ship stopped. At **Suez**, while it waited, they got up a scratch game — **"very likely the first rugby played in that country."** They also lost a forward there: **Teo Rene** injured his foot bathing in the canal and missed the first eleven matches in England. They reached England on **27 September**, having already played nine matches at home and two in Melbourne, and were taken to the Greyhound Hotel at Richmond to prepare.

The itinerary tells you something about the state of the game they had come to play. **Only one match was arranged in Scotland** — at Hawick on 22 November — **because the English and Scottish unions were not speaking to each other.** Later Scottish invitations had to be declined for lack of room. The tourists were sailing into a sport whose governing bodies were in the middle of a cold war, and it would shape everything that happened to them.

### Lansdowne Road

On **1 December 1888** they crossed to Dublin, having played **thirteen matches in four weeks**, several leading men carrying serious injuries.

Ireland led 3–0 at half-time and scored again after the interval, and the crowd settled into what looked like a routine home win. Then the tourists remembered what they were. **Keogh** scored twice, one of them from deep inside his own half; **Williams and Ellison** combined for a movement that swept the length of the field; **Elliot** added a fourth and **Maynard** a fifth from long range; **McCausland** kicked four conversions. **Ireland 4, New Zealand Natives 13.**

The Irish press declined to be impressed, preferring to explain the result as a home failure — **"seldom has an Irish international combination shown to less advantage."** It is worth noting, as the tour's historian Greg Ryan does, that **the same Irish side beat Wales that season.** A privately promoted team of New Zealanders had gone to Lansdowne Road and beaten an international side fifteen years before their country played its first official Test, and the beaten party's newspapers treated it as an embarrassment rather than an event.

### Blackheath

On **16 February 1889**, at Blackheath, the Natives played England. It is the match everything else in this generation points at, and the reason is not the score.

Start with why the fixture existed at all. England was at that moment **boycotted by the other three home unions.** After a disputed try against Scotland in 1884, the Scots had wanted an international board to arbitrate the laws; England was willing to join **only if it held a majority of the votes**, on the grounds that it had more clubs than anybody else, and refused to attend the new board's meetings in 1886 and 1887. So Ireland, Scotland and Wales refused to play it. **"This was to be the only international match played by England in 1888 and 1889,"** Ryan records. **"Indeed, some felt that the loss of their traditional opponents was the only reason England agreed to play against the Native team."** England joined the board only in 1890, after the president of the Football Association was brought in to arbitrate, and took **half the seats on a body that required a three-quarter majority** to decide anything.

That is the same institution, in the same decade, that this book watched write the amateur regulations of 1886 and drive twenty-two northern clubs out in 1895. The Natives arrived in the middle of it, and were given a fixture because the Rugby Football Union had nobody else to play.

**The referee was Rowland Hill, the Union's own secretary.**

England won by a goal and four tries — **7–0** — before some nine thousand people, and the tourists disputed three decisions. Two were tries awarded to Bedford when **Billy Warbrick** and then **Harry Lee** insisted they had touched the ball dead in goal. The third has been argued about ever since, and the best account of it is **Tom Ellison's**, in the coaching manual he later wrote, *The Art of Rugby Football*:

> **"I lured him into my arms by applying the feign dodge. By a quick wriggle, however, he escaped but left a portion of his knickers in my possession. He dashed along and the crowd roared; then suddenly discovering what was the matter he stopped, threw down the ball, and in an instant we had the vulgar gaze shut off by forming a ring around him."**

The man in Ellison's arms was **Andrew Stoddart**, England's most famous all-rounder. Believing he had called dead ball, the New Zealanders formed a circle around him to spare his modesty — and **Frank Evershed** picked up the abandoned ball and ran in at the corner. Then, while the tourists argued with Hill, Evershed picked the ball up again and **carried it round to a better position under the posts**, where Sutcliffe converted it. When Hill gave the try, **Williams, Taiaroa and Sherry Wynyard walked off the field.** Hill restarted the game without them. It took several minutes to persuade them back.

A hundred and thirty-odd years later the evidence is what it is. Ryan's verdict — that **"events do not become any clearer, although the contrast between Ellison's detailed account and the vagueness of the English press reports may offer some indication"** — is as far as anyone can honestly go, though at least one senior RFU man afterwards allowed that England might have **"given way"** over Evershed's try.

But Ellison's real complaint was not about any of the three decisions, and it is the sentence this entire chapter turns on:

> **"I may add that gross as these errors were, they were insignificant when compared with another that Mr Hill committed at the outset of the game, viz, refereeing at all in that game; he being the most important official of the English Rugby Union and the father of the team pitted against us."**

A Māori footballer from a colony with no governing body, writing in a coaching manual, put the objection more precisely than anyone in this book has yet managed: **the problem was not that the owner of the game got the decisions wrong. The problem was that the owner of the game was refereeing it.**

The Union's response settles the argument in its favour, which is what ownership means. It **demanded an apology**. McCausland, captaining in place of the injured Warbrick, sent one by telegram from Cambridge on 20 February:

> **"As captain of the New Zealand team I beg to apologise to the Rugby Union committee for the insults offered by my team to their officials on the field of play on Saturday last, and beg on behalf of my team to express their regret for their behaviour on that occasion."**

That was his **second** attempt. The first had been rejected, and the Union had made the terms explicit: another apology, **dictated by Hill himself**, or the tour was over — because clubs affiliated to the Rugby Football Union would be **forbidden to play against the tourists**.

Six years before it used that weapon on Yorkshire and Lancashire, the RFU used it on a Māori touring team five weeks' sail from home: not a fine, not a suspension, but **control of who is permitted to play whom.** The tourists had a hundred matches to fulfil and no money without them. They signed what they were given.

### The laws had no owner either

Sailing home in May and June 1889, the party paid its way in the most practical manner available: **eight matches of Victorian Rules around Melbourne**, of which they won three, alongside three rugby matches, of which they won all. Then, on **15 June at the Sydney Cricket Ground**, before "fully 10,000 persons", they beat **New South Wales 12–9**, with tries to Madigan, Elliott, Ellison and Keogh.

And the New Zealand paper that carried the report appended a note that is worth more than the result:

> **"[Note. —Under the scoring points as observed in New Zealand the New South Wales team would have won by 6 points to 4.]"**

The same eighty minutes had **two different winners depending on whose points table you consulted.** In 1889, four years after the international board was proposed and one year before England consented to join it, the laws of rugby had no more of a single owner than New Zealand's game did.

### The union, and the jersey it did not design

Back home, the vacuum finally closed.

In 1891, **E. D. Hoben**, secretary of the Hawke's Bay union, toured the country arguing for a national body, and on **16 April 1892**, at the Club Hotel in Wellington, delegates from eight unions met to create one. The chairman put the case in terms of authority rather than glory: there was **"need for a supreme football Court of Appeal in the Colony, and a body sufficiently strong to settle any disputes which might arise."**

The three strongest southern unions did not want one. Otago had opposed the idea from the start; by the second meeting **Canterbury and Southland had joined the opposition to a change of control**; Southland wrote that, given the distance, it would only ever be represented by proxy. When the motion passed, the minutes record it plainly: **"carried, Messrs. Milne and Marshall alone dissenting. These gentlemen then withdrew, and the delegates resolved themselves into the first meeting of THE NEW ZEALAND UNION."**

The delegates for Otago and Canterbury left the room, and the national governing body of New Zealand rugby was constituted in their absence. It is a very New Zealand founding: not a seizure of power but an argument about whether power should exist at all, settled by a narrow majority in a hotel.

Twelve months later the new Union held its first annual meeting, on **27 April 1893**, and one of the Wellington delegates was **T. R. Ellison** — the man who had been tackled out of Stoddart's shorts at Blackheath. The minutes are brief:

> **"It was resolved that the New Zealand Representative colours should be Black Jersey with Silver Fernleaf, Black Cap with Silver Monogram, White Knickerbockers and Black Stockings, on the motion of Mr Ellison, seconded by Mr King."**

Why Ellison chose black **"remains one of the intriguing questions in New Zealand rugby history"**, and the museum offers two explanations without deciding between them: a family account tying the fern to the proverbs *"when one warrior dies another arises"* and *"when one fern dies another arises"*, with black chosen to set the fern off; and the entirely practical possibility that a team touring Britain to play England in white, Wales in red and Ireland in green needed a colour that clashed with none of them.

Either way, the mechanism is the same one this chapter keeps finding. **The national union's first act of identity was to adopt the uniform of a private commercial venture, on the motion of one of its players.** Ellison captained the first official New Zealand side later that season.

The rest of the decade was consolidation, and it took the shape the country's geography dictated. A North Island team beat New South Wales in **1894**. On **26 June 1897** the first inter-island match was played in Wellington — **North Island 16, South Island 3** — staged as a trial to pick a New Zealand team for a ten-match tour of Australia that July. The fixture returned in 1902 and then ran annually for most of a century. Before there were Tests, the way to find out who the best players in New Zealand were was to divide the country in half.

### What this generation leaves behind

A jersey, a haka, a reputation — and an unresolved question about who they belong to.

By 1902 New Zealand has a national union with a court of appeal and a set of colours, the provinces have stopped walking out of rooms, and an inter-island trial exists to select a national side. What it has not yet had is a Test match, because nobody has been willing to give it one on equal terms.

That is about to change very fast. In **1903** New Zealand plays its first official international; in **1905** a team sails for Britain and loses once in thirty-odd matches, acquiring a name that sticks for good and losing a match in Cardiff that the country will argue about for a century. And in **1907** a Wellington postal clerk will notice that the men filling those grounds are not being paid a penny, and will do something about it — which will hand New Zealand its own version of the schism that England had in 1895, and give the Rugby Football Union's amateur weapon a second target on the far side of the world.

## Generation 2: The Originals and the All Golds (1903–1919)

**Saturday 6 August 1904. Alexandra Park, Epsom, Auckland.**

Every electric tram in the city has been put into service, and it is not enough. Horse-drawn vehicles of every description are pressed in behind them, and still a considerable proportion of the crowd has to walk out to Epsom. By two o'clock the main stand is standing room only. By twenty past, the seating — three thousand six hundred places — is gone, and thousands are packed against the rails. Men arriving late build their own stands out of whatever is to hand, including a discarded shop counter and a dray. Some have brought boxes and folding stools; and a quarter of an hour before kick-off an entrepreneur turns up with **a cartload of roughly made stools, which he sells at sixpence each — "as freely as hot-cross buns on Good Friday morning."**

The gate and stands take **£505**, a record for an interprovincial match in New Zealand. Auckland are defending the **Ranfurly Shield** in its first-ever challenge, and among their forwards is a wing-forward named **D. Gallaher**. Wellington win **6–3** and take the shield away with them.

Not one of the men on the field is paid a penny.

---

That is the arithmetic that governs this generation, and it produced two entirely different New Zealand teams within two years of each other — one that became the most famous side in the world, and one that was threatened with life bans for asking where the money went.

First, the institutional detail, because it matters more than it looks. In 1901 the **Earl of Ranfurly**, Governor of New Zealand and the union's patron, told the NZRFU he wished to present it with a trophy. The Union, at its 1902 annual meeting, decided the trophy should be a **challenge** one: it awarded it to Auckland, who had the best provincial record that year, and ruled that **challenges would be played on the home ground of the holder.**

Consider what that does. A governor hands a national body a gift, and the national body immediately converts it into an object that **lives with a province** and can only be taken by travelling to that province and beating it in front of its own crowd. The Ranfurly Shield is the most beloved trophy in New Zealand rugby, and it is, structurally, a monument to the thing the union had been founded in 1892 to overcome. The provinces that walked out of the Club Hotel lost the argument about central authority and immediately won the argument about what the game would actually feel like. A hundred and twenty years later the Shield still works exactly that way.

### The name came from the Natives

By 1903 New Zealand was playing official internationals — the first against Australia that year, and in **1904** the first ever played on New Zealand soil, against Great Britain at Wellington, watched from the stand by a fifty-three-year-old land speculator named Charles Monro.

And in 1905 a New Zealand team sailed for Britain and came back with a name.

The name's origin is one of the most repeated stories in the sport, and it is almost certainly false. The version told by one of the 1905 players at the team's fiftieth jubilee in 1955 runs like this: after New Zealand beat Hartlepool 63–0, the *Daily Mail* wrote them up as **"New Zealand team all backs"** — because, as he put it, "all our forwards could pass as good as any back" — and at the next stop a printer **"had got the l in somehow"** and made it *All Blacks*, and it stuck.

The New Zealand Rugby Museum lists this among the **"incorrect rumours"** about the name, and the reason is documentary. **"The first known published reference to All Blacks was in relation to the Natives … in mid-1889."** Four years after that, with the first official New Zealand team in Australia in 1893, an Auckland journalist wrote: **"I expect to see the all blacks come out on top with a substantial majority."** And on the morning after the Originals' first match of 1905, a British paper described **"The All Blacks, as they are styled by reason of their sable and unrelieved costume."** As the museum puts it: **"Whatever happened or did not happen in 1905, the team was known as the All Blacks when it arrived in Britain."** The accepted explanation is the boring one — they were dressed all in black, and "All" was a common prefix of the period for a fully international side, as in "All Japan."

So the name, like the jersey, like the fern, like the haka, traces back to **Joe Warbrick's privately promoted Māori tourists of 1888**, and not to the union, and not to 1905. New Zealand's entire symbolic wardrobe was assembled by a commercial venture that the national governing body did not exist to authorise.

### The Originals

What 1905 did was make the wardrobe famous.

**Dave Gallaher's** side — the **Originals** — went to Britain and won thirty-four of thirty-five matches, the sort of record that changes how a country is spoken about. This book has already watched them from the other side: from Cardiff, where Wales beat them **3–0** on **16 December 1905** with a seven-man scrum and a rehearsed move that put Teddy Morgan over in the corner; and from Paris, where the Bibliothèque nationale's account of them still reads like an eyewitness marvelling — *players of class, twisting and turning with ball in hand, endlessly counter-attacking, scoring a great many tries.*

And from Cardiff also came the grievance. Late in that match **Bob Deans** went for the corner, was hauled down, and insisted to the end of his short life that he had grounded the ball before being dragged back; the referee, arriving behind the play, ruled no try. The Welsh chapter of this book declines to adjudicate it and so does this one. What matters here is what the argument *did*: it gave New Zealand, within thirty-five years of its first inter-district match, a national grievance about a refereeing decision in a foreign country — which is to say, it gave the country a stake in a game it had previously only played.

### The postal clerk

Two years later a Wellington postal clerk did the arithmetic from Alexandra Park.

**Albert Henry Baskerville** played club rugby for Wellington and Oriental, and had written a coaching book — *Modern Rugby Football: New Zealand Methods* — before he ever read about the Northern Union in England. When he did, he formed a plan: take a team of New Zealand players to Britain to play the professional code. The great All Black wing **George Smith** helped him choose the side.

The response of his own union is the part this chapter has been building toward. The NZRFU **threatened the players with life bans** — in the New Zealand Rugby League's own summary, for the "crime" of **"being financially compensated for working time lost while playing sport, thus surrendering their amateur status."**

*Working time lost.* That is **broken time**: the precise question put to a vote at the Westminster Palace Hotel in 1893 and rejected 282–136, and the precise reason twenty-two northern clubs walked out of the George Hotel in Huddersfield in 1895. Eighteen years after the Rugby Football Union dictated an apology to a Māori touring team and threatened to make its fixtures unplayable, New Zealand's own union reached for the same weapon against its own players — and for exactly the same offence.

They went anyway. Twenty-eight players, **eight of them All Blacks**, on a tour that lasted more than nine months. They began in **August 1907** with three matches against New South Wales in Sydney, where the New South Wales Rugby League had been formed earlier that same month, and sailed for England by way of Ceylon. The *Sydney Morning Herald* jeered them as the **"All Golds"** — they played for gold — and the insult became the name history uses. They played **thirty-five matches in England and Wales between October 1907 and February 1908**, and on **New Year's Day 1908** at Aberdare, before some twenty thousand people, they lost the first rugby league international ever played, **Wales 9, New Zealand 8**, to a winning try from Dai "Tarw" Jones — a Welsh forward of the 1905 side that beat the Originals, driven into the professional code by his own union's suspension.

Baskerville himself played one match in England, so consumed was he by managing, secretarial work and journalism; he scored a try in a 21–10 win over St Helens. On the way home the party played ten more matches in Australia, and in the **first New Zealand–Australia Test**, won 11–10 in Sydney, Baskerville intercepted and ran the length of the field for a try.

He died **eleven days later**, aged **twenty-five**. A chill caught on the boat from Sydney to Brisbane turned to pneumonia after he watched a midweek match against Queensland from the grandstand. His teammates dedicated the second Test to him and won it 24–12 at the Brisbane Exhibition Ground. **The first game of rugby league ever played on New Zealand soil** — an exhibition at Athletic Park, Wellington, on **13 June 1908** — was staged to raise money for his widowed mother.

New Zealand thus acquired its own version of 1895, and the shape of it is worth marking. In England the split was regional, structural and permanent: two codes, two cultures, a wall down the middle of the country. In New Zealand it produced a smaller, quieter code that took root in particular places rather than in a class — and it left the union in possession of the national game, having discovered that it could threaten its own men and mostly get away with it.

### The war, and the two men left behind

The generation ends in uniform.

**Dave Gallaher**, captain of the Originals, was killed at Passchendaele in **1917**. The French chapter carries the Bibliothèque nationale's phrase for what became of that team — *overtaken by another duty: defending liberty.*

Then, in the spring of **1919**, a New Zealand Army side went through the inter-services tournament in Britain and won the **King's Cup**. On **5 April** at Inverleith, Edinburgh, it beat England — billed as the Mother Country — **6–3** before twenty thousand spectators, with the Prince of Wales and Prince Albert in the stand, tries to Storey and Belliss, after earlier wins over the Royal Air Force, the Canadian Expeditionary Force and the South African Forces. It was, by some distance, the best New Zealand team yet assembled.

It was then invited to tour South Africa, and South Africa asked that no "coloured" players be included.

Two men were removed: **Parekura Tureia** of Ngāti Porou, and **Nathaniel "Ranji" Wilson**, a New Zealander of West Indian descent who had played for the All Blacks before the war. **New Zealand complied.**

This book has already told that episode from Johannesburg. Told from Wellington it is a different story with the same facts. A country whose game had been built by a Māori touring side, whose jersey and fern and haka and very name came from that side, whose first representative team in 1884 had a Māori among its best players — that country, asked by a host to leave two of its own men at home, said yes. Not under duress, and not in ignorance. It said yes because it wanted the fixture.

### What this generation leaves behind

The most famous team in the world, and a bill nobody has read.

By 1919 New Zealand has the black jersey, the fern, the haka, the name, a Test record that frightens people, the Ranfurly Shield, and a governing body strong enough to threaten its own players with life bans and to drop two of them at a foreign union's request. It has also acquired a second code, a small permanent reproach living in particular towns, and a captain buried in Belgium.

What it has not acquired is any settled answer to the question the All Golds asked. Rugby in New Zealand is now a mass spectacle taking record gates, and the men producing the spectacle are paid nothing — an arrangement the union will defend for another seventy-six years, and which will hold, more or less, because the country believes in it.

The next generation is where that belief is tested twice: once by a touring side that goes through Britain without losing a single match, and once by a request from South Africa that New Zealand has already shown it will grant.

## Generation 3: The Invincibles and the Colour Bar (1920–1948)

**12 December 1924. London. The Imperial Rugby Conference.**

New Zealand has sent two men: **Stan Dean**, who is in Britain managing the touring All Blacks and who chairs the NZRFU's management committee — a job he will hold for twenty-five years — and **Wilson**, president of the Canterbury union and a former national selector. They have come with remits. Some of them propose changes to the laws that will not actually be adopted for half a century.

They are not, however, members of the International Rugby Board. **"The three Colonial Rugby Unions were in 1924 affiliated to the Rugby Football Union rather than to the IRB."** New Zealand, Australia and South Africa sit at the imperial table as dependants of one of the parties.

And in the chair is **Rowland Hill.**

The same Rowland Hill who refereed the Natives at Blackheath in 1889 while serving as secretary of the Rugby Football Union; the man Tom Ellison said should never have been on the field, being "the father of the team pitted against us"; the man who rejected McCausland's first apology and dictated the second. He is now the outstanding official figure in the world game and an ardent defender of the amateur code, and his first act at the conference is to **refuse to allow New Zealand's remit on professionalism to be discussed at all** — over New Zealand protests that when remits were invited, nobody had said some subjects were off limits.

Later, on a technical remit about the knock-on, the Welsh delegate "hoped Mr Wilson will not press this matter," and Hill closed it down — **"I do not see how you are going to alter the rule"** — and moved to the next business. The change was adopted in **1973**.

Three hundred miles north, the touring team those delegates had come with was in the middle of not losing a single match.

---

That is the shape of the whole generation. On the field, New Zealand is now unquestionably the best rugby country in the world. Off it, it has no vote, no standing, and no ability even to table the question that its own players had forced in 1907.

### The Invincibles

**Cliff Porter's 1924–25 side** went through the United Kingdom, Ireland, France and Canada **undefeated**, and has been called the **Invincibles** ever since. The fullback **George Nepia** — nineteen years old, Ngāti Porou — **played every match of the tour.**

At home the side's stature produced its own folklore. The Ranfurly Shield was held through 1925 by **Hawke's Bay**, who repelled six challenges, and in a robust 31–12 win over Southland on **8 August** a nineteen-year-old Southland forward named **Bill Hazlett** got himself tangled up with the great Invincible **Maurice Brownlie**. The Auckland referee, Bill Meredith, stopped play to admonish him:

> **"Now then, Hazlett, you leave Mr Brownlie alone."**

The remark leaked to the press, was printed the length of the country, and moved *Truth* to publish a poem about it. Hazlett went on to be an All Black himself. It is a small thing, but it tells you what had happened to the game in the fifty-five years since Nelson: a referee could stop a provincial match to protect the dignity of a national player, and the whole country would recognise the name.

### The bar

And then there is the other institution New Zealand rugby built in these years, which it did not build alone.

In **1921** the Springboks came to New Zealand for the first time, and the series was drawn — a Test each and a scoreless third in the Wellington mud. In the middle of the tour, at Napier on **7 September**, they played the New Zealand Maoris, and a South African journalist's cable home was leaked by a post office employee to the local paper. Its language is quoted in this book's South African chapter and does not need repeating here; its substance was that the spectacle of thousands of Europeans cheering on a band of coloured men against white men had disgusted the tourists. New Zealand erupted. The captain of that Māori side was **Parekura Tureia** — one of the two men New Zealand had removed from its own touring party two years earlier, at South Africa's request.

In **1926** a New Zealand Maori side toured Britain, France and Canada, playing through the winter from Cardiff to Limoges to Vancouver.

In **1928** New Zealand toured South Africa, and **left George Nepia at home.**

Nepia had played every one of the Invincibles' matches. He was, by common consent, the finest fullback in the world. He did not go, because South Africa would not have him, and because the New Zealand Rugby Football Union agreed to that condition, as it had agreed in 1919 and would agree again in 1949 and 1960.

Set the two halves of this generation side by side, because they are the same story. The union that could not get professionalism onto the agenda at an imperial conference — that had no seat of its own, and was told by an Englishman what it was permitted to discuss — was, in the same decade, exercising the one sovereign power it did possess: deciding which New Zealanders were allowed to be New Zealand. It could not choose the laws of the game. It could choose who wore the jersey. And when the price of a fixture was one of its best players, it paid.

### The one series they lost at home

The rest of the era was ordinary in a way that mattered.

The 1930 British side came and went. The 1935–36 All Blacks toured Britain and France. And in **1937** the Springboks returned and won a series in New Zealand — the only time South Africa has ever done so, then or since, and the last word in a rivalry that the two unions had by now built on a shared understanding about who could be selected.

Then the war took the game away for six years, and gave it back in an unexpected form. The **2nd New Zealand Expeditionary Force "Kiwis"** — a services team, not an All Black side — toured Britain and Europe in 1945–46 and became one of the best-loved teams the country ever sent. Only the captain, **Charlie Saxton**, had been an All Black before the war. On **24 November 1945** they played their first international, against England at Twickenham, a ground larger than anything in New Zealand, and the broadcaster Winston McCarthy left an account of it that is really an account of a style being born:

> **"This day the 'Kiwi' forwards went for the doctor. In scrums and rucks they were supreme. In fact their glorious rucking — and I've never seen a team of forwards who could heel back from the rucks like the 'Kiwis' — really began this day."**

Saxton's long stiff-armed passes travelling twenty-five yards across the field; the crowd roaring at the dive-passes; Sherratt scoring after twenty minutes. A team of soldiers, most of whom had never been near an All Black jersey, playing the fastest rucking game anyone in Britain had seen. It went home to a country that had just spent six years being told what it could not have, and it was received accordingly.

### What this generation leaves behind

The best team in the world, with no seat at the table and a colour bar it administers itself.

By 1948 New Zealand has been unbeaten through a British tour, has produced in Nepia a player the game still argues about, has invented the rucking style that will define it for fifty years, and has lost one home series in three decades. It is also a union that has twice removed Māori players from touring parties to please a foreign host, that is affiliated to another country's governing body rather than to the world one, and that was told in 1924 by the man who had dictated its apology in 1889 that it might not discuss professionalism at an imperial conference.

The bill for the second of those things comes due almost immediately. In **1949** New Zealand sends another team to South Africa without its Māori players — and this time the team is destroyed, four Tests to nil, while a second-string side is beaten at home on the same afternoons. Two defeats in one day, and a growing number of New Zealanders starting to ask aloud what exactly the country is buying with its silence.

## Generation 4: No Maoris, No Tour (1949–1969)

**Saturday 3 September 1949.**

In Johannesburg, the All Blacks lose the third Test to South Africa, **9–3**, with the small consolation of scoring the match's only try. Roughly twelve hours earlier, on the other side of the world, a second-string New Zealand side — the best of the players left at home — has lost the first Test to Australia, **11–6**.

Two Test defeats in one day. It gets worse when you count the year: **six Tests in 1949, four against South Africa and two against Australia, and New Zealand lost every one of them.**

There were nine other first-class fixtures in New Zealand that afternoon. Otago beat Canterbury at Lancaster Park; Buller held the Seddon Shield against Marlborough. The domestic game went on exactly as it always had, in front of its own crowds, in its own competitions, entirely unbothered.

And the team being beaten four-nil in South Africa had been selected on a rule that had nothing to do with rugby.

---

The New Zealand Rugby Football Union had accepted the invitation after the war, and stated the terms itself, in writing:

> **"In view of the domestic policy of South Africa, the players cannot be other than wholly European."**

It was the second of three such teams — 1928, 1949, and, as it would turn out, 1960. What is different about 1949 is that this time a substantial number of New Zealanders said so out loud.

### "To hell with them"

The objection had been running since 1921, and for nearly thirty years it had come almost entirely from Māori.

Te Rangi Hiroa — Dr Peter Buck, the physician and anthropologist — had called on the Springboks after the Napier match **"to make honourable amends"**, and had challenged the union to extend no further invitations if South Africa persisted in drawing a colour line in sport. In 1937, when the Springboks returned, **Te Arawa sought a boycott of the tour** — the first organised, nationwide protest against the rugby relationship — and Māori leaders including **Te Puea Herangi** supported the call. And when a 1940 tour was being planned, two Māori players who had played in all three Tests against the Springboks in 1937, **Everard Jackson** and **Tori Reed**, received a letter from the union.

Everard Jackson's son, the constitutional lawyer **Moana Jackson**, described it decades later:

> **"[They] received a letter from the rugby union asking them if they would make themselves unavailable for selection the following year to save the rugby union from embarrassment. Well, the Second World War arrived and saved them from embarrassment."**

That is the state of things when, in September 1948, at a Returned Services Association function in Christchurch, **Major General Sir Howard Kippenberger** went off script.

Kippenberger had commanded in Greece, Crete, Libya and Italy. At Monte Cassino, commanding the Māori Battalion, he had stood on a mine and lost both feet. He praised the men he had commanded — **"If you had the Māori Battalion on your flank, you were secure"** — and then walked straight into the argument about the tour:

> **"I am not going to acquiesce to any damned Afrikaner saying they cannot go. To hell with them."**

The Ngāti Kahungunu leader **A. T. Carroll** caught the response: **"If it is good enough for the members of our race to fight side by side with South Africans, then it is good enough for our players to oppose them on the rugby field."** **Eruera Tirikatene**, MP for Southern Māori, called on the union to do the obvious thing and decline the invitation, and asked on whose initiative Māori were being excluded. His question was met with silence.

The tour went. The team lost everything.

### The quiet fifties

Then the argument stopped, for about six years, because New Zealand won.

In **1956** the Springboks came, and New Zealand beat them in a series for the first time. The country gave itself over to it completely. As the historian of these campaigns notes, so total was the unity that **even the Communist Party supported the tour** — and the only organisation in New Zealand to oppose it was the **Māori Women's Welfare League.**

It is worth sitting with that sentence. In the year of the country's greatest rugby triumph to date, the entire political spectrum from the Communists rightward was in favour, and the objection was carried by a Māori women's organisation.

### CABTA

In the summer of 1958 the union announced that it had accepted an invitation to tour South Africa in **1960**, and said nothing whatever about the racial composition of the team. The omission was the answer: nothing had changed.

What followed was unlike 1948. The opposition ran for **more than two years**, and it built the first organisation of its kind anywhere in the world: **CABTA, the Citizens All Black Tour Association**, founded specifically to campaign against rugby contact with South Africa, with **more than twenty branches from Kaikohe to Invercargill**. Its slogan was three words long and it has outlived everything else about the period:

> **"No Maoris — No Tour."**

**George Nepia** — left at home in 1928, by then in his fifties — sent a telegram to CABTA's inaugural meeting:

> **"Best of luck. Let me know if you need a fullback."**

The petition against the tour drew **160,000 signatures**, still one of the largest in the country's history. On **18 June 1959** more than five hundred students marched through Wellington to Parliament, the first street demonstration against New Zealand's sporting contacts with South Africa; a thousand demonstrated at the team's state farewell; between two and three thousand marched in Auckland on the eve of departure. A Canterbury sociologist later called it perhaps the country's **"most vigorous controversy since the prohibition issue"** of the 1890s. Nothing on this scale had been attempted anywhere in the world to stop a sports team going to South Africa; Britain's Stop The Seventy Tour campaign, ten years later, was the first to surpass it.

The Labour government declined to intervene — the first time any government anywhere had set out a position on the question — and Prime Minister **Walter Nash** was a personal friend of the union's chairman, **Cuthbert Hogg**, whom he believed to be a real friend of the Māori. **The tour went.** New Zealand lost the series.

And CABTA's own framing deserves to be recorded exactly, because it explains what happened next. Its campaign was not, as an organisation, against apartheid. It told South African non-racial sports advocates that the protest was **not "against the racial policy of the South African government, but rather against an act of racial discrimination committed by a New Zealand sports organisation, in New Zealand, and concerning a New Zealand national sports team."**

That is a narrower objection than the one the world was starting to make, and it is an honest one: New Zealanders were angry about what was being done to New Zealanders.

### The turn

The 1960s changed the question.

South Africa was expelled from the Tokyo Olympics in 1964. When the Springboks made their fourth New Zealand tour in **1965**, the protests were small — thirty people at Whenuapai when the team landed, a couple of hundred at a rally in the Auckland Town Hall, addressed by the poet **Hone Tuwhare** and by **Whetu Tirikatene**, Eruera's daughter. What mattered was not the size but the reason. For the first time, the people protesting were protesting **apartheid**, not selection.

Then, on **5 September 1965**, with the Springboks still in the country, South Africa's prime minister announced that the All Black team touring there in 1967 would again be all-white. This time "No Maoris, No Tour" editorials ran the length of New Zealand.

On **3 February 1966**, Prime Minister **Keith Holyoake** ended fifty years of it in a paragraph:

> **"Where important moral issues are involved fundamental to our national integrity, the government has a duty to state clearly the principles which, in its view, New Zealand should observe at home and abroad… I do not think that government should seek to impose standards of conduct, but it should proclaim those standards… In this country we are one people; as such we cannot as a nation be truly represented in any sphere by a group chosen on racial lines."**

It was a non-directive directive, and the union understood it perfectly. **The 1967 tour was off.**

Holyoake deserves the credit for that, and he also deserves the rest of his record. Five years earlier, when **Frank Corner** left to take up New Zealand's seat at the United Nations, the only advice the Prime Minister gave him was to avoid using the word *abhorrent* about apartheid — **"My people don't like it."** Between 1960 and 1972 New Zealand voted against or abstained on most UN resolutions concerning South Africa. Corner's own summary of the country he represented is the fairest verdict available: **"so long as we could get away with it domestically, we could be great internationalists"** — but on race, "that was a different matter."

### What this generation leaves behind

A principle, stated by a government, and no idea at all what it will cost.

By the end of the 1960s New Zealand has established, at last, that it will not send a team chosen on racial lines. It took the objection of Te Rangi Hiroa, Te Arawa, Te Puea Herangi, the Māori Women's Welfare League, an MP named Tirikatene and his daughter, a war hero who had lost both feet at Monte Cassino, 160,000 signatures and forty-five years to get there.

In **July 1969**, fourteen people met at Auckland University and founded **Halt All Racist Tours.**

They were not formed to fight the old battle, which had been won. They were formed for the one nobody had yet noticed was coming — because South Africa, faced with losing the fixture, was about to find a way to say yes. In 1970 the All Blacks would tour South Africa with Māori and Pasifika players in the party, admitted under a status the South African state invented for the occasion. New Zealand would have to decide whether that counted as a victory.

## Generation 5: The Tour (1970–1986)

**Saturday 25 July 1981. Rugby Park, Hamilton.**

The morning has an eerie, unreal air. On a big-match day Hamilton is the most rugby town in New Zealand — the country people pour in, the Aucklanders mingle, the talk is all of the game — but today Victoria Street is drab and almost deserted. **The Mooloo parade has been cancelled**, in case it provokes friction between people who want the tour and people who do not.

The Springboks' bus arrived at a quarter past ten, **splattered with paint**. The players have been quartered in a room with mattresses and a television set. A **helicopter hovers** over the ground. Trucks have been drawn up behind the main grandstand as a bulwark, though the ends of the ground are closed by nothing but a fence. A reporter in the little press box on the bank watches the crowd fill the open seating below him, and finds he cannot concentrate on the curtain-raiser: his eyes keep flicking to the roadway on the left, then to Seddon Road on the right. *From where would the march come, and where would it end?*

Five hours later the match against Waikato has been abandoned, protesters are on the field, and New Zealanders have fought New Zealanders in the streets outside. That evening the South African Press Association's man in New Zealand, **Terry Barron** — fifteen years of covering southern Africa behind him — looks into his beer and says:

> **"Today, New Zealand turned 21."**

---

The country had spent eleven years walking toward that afternoon, and had been offered several chances to stop.

### Honorary whites

The first came in **1970**, and it arrived disguised as a victory.

Having lost the 1967 tour to Holyoake's statement that a nation cannot be represented by a team chosen on racial lines, South Africa found a way to keep the fixture: it would accept Māori and Pasifika players — reclassified, for the duration of their visit, under a status the state invented for the occasion. **Honorary whites.** **Sid Going** and **Bryan Williams** went, and Williams in particular was adored by South African crowds, including crowds who were not permitted to sit where they liked to watch him.

New Zealand had asked for its own men to be admitted, and had been granted precisely that and nothing more. The colour bar remained; the exception was personal, temporary and issued by the very government the bar belonged to. HART, founded the year before, had been formed for exactly this. From 1970 the argument in New Zealand was no longer about who could wear the jersey. It was about whether the fixture should exist at all.

### 1973

The second chance came three years later, and it was taken — though not by anybody in rugby.

South Africa was due to tour New Zealand in **1973**, and the Labour government of **Norman Kirk** cancelled it.

The reaction among rugby people was fury, and it did not fade. RNZ's own sound archive, looking back, describes the response of New Zealand's rugby supporters to the tour that never happened as having **"set the scene for the showdown of the 1981 Springbok Tour."** A grievance was banked in 1973 and spent eight years later.

But notice the pattern, because it is this chapter's subject. The union had never once stopped one of these tours itself. In 1966 a Prime Minister's statement ended the 1967 tour. In 1973 a Prime Minister's decision ended this one. In 1985 it would take a court. **Every time a South African fixture was stopped, it was stopped from outside the game.**

### 1976

The third chance came in **1976**, and this time the cost was paid by people who had never played rugby.

The All Blacks landed in South Africa on 30 June, a fortnight after the schoolchildren of **Soweto** had been shot in the streets, and toured in defiance of United Nations calls for a sporting embargo. A group of African nations demanded that the International Olympic Committee exclude New Zealand from the Montreal Games. **The IOC refused, and New Zealand's athletes competed as usual — which produced the boycott.** Nigeria's announcement, made by Major Olufemi Olutoye, was followed by others; African teams walked out of an Olympic Games because of a rugby tour.

New Zealand won gold in Montreal — John Walker in the 1500 metres, and the men's hockey team — in a stadium that a large part of a continent had left.

At home, this was the moment the argument stopped being about South Africa and started being about New Zealand. The country had been told, as plainly as international sport can tell anybody, what its rugby was costing.

Five years later, when South Africa asked to come to New Zealand, the government did not stop them.

### The fifty-six days

They arrived in July 1981 and stayed for eight weeks, and by the end of it the country had police squads with riot gear and names, barbed wire around its rugby grounds, and families that stopped speaking to each other.

The **first match, at Gisborne on 22 July**, still looked like the old world. There was a big-game atmosphere along Gladstone Road, the shops and bars busy, **"farmers down from the hills in their tweed jackets and fore-and-aft hats."** There were rumours that buses of demonstrators had been stranded because petrol stations along the route refused to serve them, and that the Waioeka Gorge road had been closed to them; it was hard to tell where fact ended and fancy began. The crowd, denied a curtain-raiser because the ground was soft, **cheered the police instead**, and cheered the Springbok reserves as they took their seats; the South African captain **Wynand Claassen** doffed his cap in thanks.

Then, from the press box, the marchers could be seen arriving — over the fence and onto the Gisborne Park golf course, police on their flanks, cameramen running ahead of them, for all the world like a television golf broadcast except that the placards read **"Boks go home"** and **"Shame."**

Three days later, at Hamilton, they got onto the field, and the match was abandoned.

The account quoted above is a rugby correspondent's, and his verdict deserves to be given as what it is — the view from the press box, honestly held:

> **"A disaster of sport in a nation that has, with a certain teen-age naivety, regarded sport as one of the purer expressions of nationalism. Worse, in those five grim hours, and afterward, New Zealander had attacked New Zealander in insensate reprisal."**

He believed the invasion had turned the protest movement from an idealism supported by people of many different persuasions into something narrower and more violent. Many New Zealanders thought so. Many others thought that a movement which had spent sixty years being polite — Te Rangi Hiroa's honourable amends, Tirikatene's unanswered question, 160,000 signatures, a telegram from George Nepia offering to play fullback — had finally found the only argument the game would hear.

The tour went on. It ended at **Eden Park on 12 September**, with a light aircraft flour-bombing the ground during the third Test and New Zealand winning **25–22** from an **Allan Hewson** penalty given by the Welsh referee **Clive Norling** — a decision South Africans have disputed ever since, which is its own small joke, given how the argument started.

### The last two tries

**1985.** The union arranged another tour of South Africa, and two Auckland lawyers — **Patrick Finnigan** and **Philip Recordon** — took it to court, arguing that the union's own constitution obliged it to promote the game's interests and that this tour would not. On **13 July** the tour was stopped by injunction.

**1986.** Virtually the entire All Black squad went anyway, privately, as the **Cavaliers** — everybody except **David Kirk** and **John Kirwan**. Their captain, **Andy Dalton**, had his jaw broken by a punch in the second match.

Read those two years together and the chapter's oldest question is asked again in modern dress. In 1907 Baskerville's men went abroad without their union's permission and were threatened with life bans. In 1986 the country's best players went abroad without their union's permission and were suspended for two Tests. **The union had never been able to stop its players going where the game took them; it had only ever been able to punish them for it.** And in 1985, for the first time, it turned out that the union could not even authorise a tour — because a court, at the request of two of its own members, said the union's constitution did not permit it.

### What this generation leaves behind

A country that has had the argument, and a game that lost it.

By the end of 1986 New Zealand has spent sixteen years discovering what its most important fixture was actually worth: the Olympic Games in 1976, its own civil peace in 1981, its captain's jaw in 1986, and something less measurable in between — the belief, held since Nelson, that this was the one thing the whole country agreed about.

And the sport has quietly changed hands in the process. Not from the union to anybody in particular, but away from the assumption that the union simply *was* New Zealand rugby, entitled to speak for it. Two lawyers stopped a tour. A court read the constitution. The players went anyway.

Which makes the timing of what happens next either the luckiest thing in the game's history or the most necessary. In **May 1987**, a year after the Cavaliers came home, New Zealand hosts the **first Rugby World Cup**, and wins it.

## Generation 6: Open (1987–2007)

**22 May 1987. Eden Park, Auckland.**

The first Rugby World Cup kicks off, and New Zealand beat Italy **70–6**. Sixteen teams have come. The All Blacks will win all six of their matches by substantial margins and take the cup by beating France by twenty points in the final; Wales will finish third.

The tournament exists because **New Zealand and Australia persuaded the International Rugby Board to sanction it, against the opposition of some northern hemisphere unions.** It is run by a committee with three joint chairmen — **Sir Nicholas Shehadie** of Australia, **Dick Littlejohn** of New Zealand, and **John Kendall-Carpenter** of the IRB. Its marketing rights have been sold to a company called **Westnally for five million US dollars**, of which one million was paid on signature and invested, the interest covering every team's travel. A Japanese communications company, **KDD**, is the major sponsor, for three and a quarter million more.

Sixty-three years earlier, at the Imperial Rugby Conference in London, New Zealand had no seat of its own, and the chairman would not let it table a motion. Now the two colonial unions have created the tournament that will define the sport, and the money has arrived in millions.

The men playing in it are not paid.

---

That gap — a global tournament, a Japanese sponsor, a marketing contract in US dollars, and fifteen amateurs on the field — is the whole of this generation. It took eight years to close, and closing it changed who owned the game more profoundly than anything since 1892.

### The last amateur cup

The 1987 tournament was nearly derailed before it began: the **military coup in Fiji** that May raised real doubts about whether the Fijians could travel, and **Western Samoa was put on standby** in case they or anyone else defaulted. Nobody did.

New Zealand's own union recorded the result of the whole enterprise in the flattest prose imaginable — "one of the most memorable seasons in the history of the game," the organisation "excellent," the matches "of a very high standard" — which is roughly how a body that had run the sport as a moral trust for ninety-five years would describe having just invented the most commercially valuable property in it.

Then came the two defeats that set up everything else. In **1991** Australia knocked New Zealand out in the semi-final. In **1995**, at Ellis Park on **24 June**, a New Zealand side built around a twenty-year-old wing who had trampled England in the semi-final lost the final to South Africa. This book has told that afternoon from Johannesburg, where it meant something no rugby match has meant before or since.

What it meant in New Zealand was different, and mostly about **Jonah Lomu**. He was, at that tournament, the most valuable athlete in the sport, and he was an amateur.

### The rugby war

What actually settled the ownership of New Zealand rugby happened in the winter of 1995, and it was not a vote of any union.

In late March a television fight in Australia spilled into the sport. **Rupert Murdoch**, building a pay-television network, needed a marquee event and set up **Super League** in direct opposition to the Australian Rugby League, whose rights **Kerry Packer** held. Murdoch's people, in the writer Peter FitzSimons's account, were "sent out among rugby league players" and **"signed them up in carparks at midnight"** — *"what's your phone number, right, put a dollar sign in front of it, sign here."*

Super League began taking union players too. Packer asked his people for a counter, and a Sydney lawyer named **Geoff Levy** supplied one: a global professional rugby competition played across Europe, America, the Pacific and Africa. Packer put up **four million dollars** of seed money; Levy brought in a former Wallaby, **Ross Turnbull**, and another lawyer, **Michael Hill**. The venture was the **World Rugby Corporation** — and its strategy is this chapter's question in a single sentence:

> **"Their plan was to sign up the players then convince the unions."**

The unions' answer was the exact mirror image. In April the three southern boards held quiet talks with Murdoch, and on **23 June 1995 — the day before the World Cup final** — announced **SANZAR** at a press conference at Ellis Park: twelve professional teams, a tri-nations series, and a television deal worth **US$555 million.** Convince the boards first, then the players.

So while South Africa and New Zealand played the final this book has already described from Johannesburg, the future of the sport was being decided by two Australian billionaires, and the asset they were bidding over was the players.

For several weeks it looked as though the World Rugby Corporation had won. **Nearly five hundred provincial and international players signed** — among them the All Black captain **Sean Fitzpatrick**, the Wallaby captain **Phil Kearns**, and **François Pienaar**, who had lifted the World Cup a fortnight before. Virtually every All Black signed. FitzSimons wrote them a column: *"Rugby is your mother. And now it happens that mum has come into a lot of money and you'll get your fair share. But if that's not enough for you, if you want mum to whore for you too, to squeeze out the last stinking dollar, and you'll trash the jersey because of it…"*

On **5 July** the New Zealand union made its counter-offer — about **$150,000 a player**, roughly a fifth of the WRC money — and got an underwhelming response, because money was not an argument it could win. What it had instead was **Jock Hobbs**, a former All Black captain turned administrator, who with **Brian Lochore** drove round the country talking to players one at a time.

It came apart in South Africa. On **3 August** the WRC held a video link-up to confirm the three big squads were committed. Ten Australians appeared, and ten New Zealanders — and only nine South Africans. Pienaar, under enormous pressure to re-sign with his own union, was not among them. The All Black lock **Ian Jones** identified that as the moment it ended: **"You can't have rugby without an All Blacks–South African rivalry. It's not international rugby… That was the end, really, just about the end of the Packer fight."**

Packer stopped the funding. On **16 August** the Australian union announced its Wallabies were back and Kearns declared the WRC finished. The same day the New Zealand union announced it had signed five provincial teams and two All Blacks, **Josh Kronfeld** and **Jeff Wilson**; the broadcaster Keith Quinn remembered sitting with Hobbs that evening, so exhausted he had not noticed he was wearing a mismatched jacket and trousers. The next day the remaining All Blacks asked to talk, and Fitzpatrick fronted for them.

Ten days after that, on **26 August 1995**, the International Rugby Board declared the game open.

Read the whole episode against 1907. Baskerville's professionals were a postal clerk and twenty-seven others, and the union answered with the threat of life bans. The World Rugby Corporation was a billionaire with four million dollars of seed money and five hundred signatures including the captain's, and the union answered by finding the money and sending two respected men to talk to every player in the country. **The weapon had changed completely. The question had not changed at all.**

### Open

For New Zealand, the declaration of an open game was not simply the end of an old rule. It was the moment the union had to decide, having just come within days of losing every player it had, what it actually owned. Its answer — and it was the most complete answer any country gave — was to **contract the players centrally**. Not the clubs, not the provinces, not the new franchises: the national union would employ the men who played for New Zealand, and would therefore control where they played, how often, and for whom.

Every argument in this chapter had been leading to it. The union that could not stop Baskerville in 1907, could not stop the Cavaliers in 1986, and could not even authorise its own tour in 1985, dealt with the professional era by simply buying the players. And it worked: for the next quarter-century New Zealand rugby was the best-run system in the sport, precisely because the question of who owned it had finally been answered on paper.

### The draft

The competition arrived first. **Super 12 kicked off on 1 March 1996 in Palmerston North**, before a big crowd, with the Auckland Blues beating the Hurricanes **36–28** — five tries to one, though the one, by **Alama Ieremia**, was the first ever scored in the competition.

Everything about that first season tells you it had been assembled in a hurry. Matches were not spread evenly across weekends, so some sides played again four days later, and the Chiefs and Crusaders had finished their programmes while two other teams still had matches to play. The Blues captain **Zinzan Brooke** remembered what the standard turned out to be: **"we had to play what seemed like Test matches every week for 13 weeks."**

And the Hurricanes' coach, **Frank Oliver**, described his squad in words that belong in the middle of this chapter's argument:

> **"We were literally the League of Nations that first year. We had players from all over the shop, eight provinces in all, and none of us really knew what we were doing because the whole thing was new to everybody… For a lot of those players, the Hurricanes had given them a lifeline. They'd come to us on the draft because they weren't wanted anywhere else."**

**On the draft.** Six months into professionalism, New Zealand rugby had a mechanism for allocating players to teams they had no connection with, and coaches who spoke of men arriving because nobody else wanted them. The provinces had spent a century as the thing the game belonged to — the Ranfurly Shield taken on its holder's own ground, the inter-island trial, Otago and Canterbury walking out of the Club Hotel rather than be governed. Now a player's employer was the national union, his team was a franchise assembled from eight provinces, and he might be sent to it because he was surplus somewhere else.

In **1996** the All Blacks won a series in South Africa for the first time — seventy-five years after the two countries first played each other. The new system's first act was to do the thing the old one never had.

### Twenty years of not winning it

And then, for two decades, New Zealand did not win the World Cup, which in New Zealand is the only statistic that counts.

Out in the semi-final in 1991. Beaten in the final in 1995. In **1999**, at Twickenham, leading France 24–10 and losing 43–31 after what the World Rugby Museum calls the most spectacular comeback in World Cup history — told, in this book, from the French side, because that is where it belongs. Out in the semi-final in 2003.

And then **Cardiff, 6 October 2007**: France again, **20–18**, in a quarter-final. The best team in the world, ranked first, unbeaten that year, gone before the semi-finals.

The reaction at home was not grief but audit. Everything was examined — the conditioning programme, the rotation policy, the referee, the reconditioning of senior players who had missed the early Super 14 season, the whole apparatus of a centrally contracted system that had been built to produce exactly one thing and had now failed to produce it five times running.

### What this generation leaves behind

A machine, and a question about what it is for.

By 2007 New Zealand has professional rugby's most complete structure: centrally contracted players, five franchises, a broadcast deal spanning three countries, and a national side that wins almost everything almost all of the time. It has resolved, in its own favour, the ownership question that ran from Warbrick's private venture through Baskerville's rebels to the Cavaliers — the union is now the employer, and the argument is over.

What it has not done since 1987 is win the tournament it invented.

The next twenty years answer that emphatically, twice, and then present the bill: because a system that owns its players can only keep them while it can outbid the rest of the world, and the rest of the world is about to get very rich indeed.

## Generation 7: The Best Team in the World (2008–2019)

**27 January 2012. Dunedin.**

The **Otago Rugby Football Union** is bailed out by the national body. New Zealand Rugby lends it **$200,000** and appoints a **change manager** — a former Bay of Plenty chief executive — to write a recovery plan; the month before, it had advanced another **$184,000** out of Otago's scheduled grants. The union has lost **close to four million dollars in five years** and expects to announce another significant loss within weeks.

The detail that went round the country was smaller than any of those figures. Earlier that month Otago had been unable to find the **$5,000 entry fee** for the national sevens tournament — **a tournament Otago was hosting.** The fee was waived.

Three months earlier, the All Blacks had won the World Cup.

---

This is the generation in which New Zealand rugby became, by some distance, the best-run and most successful operation in the sport, and in which the institutions underneath it started to go quietly broke.

### The machine

The response to Cardiff in 2007 was not to change coach. **Graham Henry** was reappointed — an unusual decision in a country where losing a World Cup quarter-final normally ends a career — and the system that had failed was audited rather than dismantled.

In **2011** New Zealand hosted the World Cup and won it, beating France in the final at Eden Park **by a single point**, with a fourth-choice first five-eighth on the field. It was twenty-four years since the last one. The tournament came in a year when Christchurch had been wrecked by earthquake, and some of what it generated went back to the city.

Then **Steve Hansen** took over, and the team got better. Over the following years the All Blacks assembled the longest winning run in the sport's history, and in **2015** at Twickenham they beat Australia to become **the first country to retain the World Cup.** Richie McCaw, Dan Carter, Ma'a Nonu and Conrad Smith left after it, together, having won everything twice.

By any measure available — win percentage, world ranking, tournaments, the sheer difficulty other countries had in beating them at all — this was the peak of the century-and-a-half this chapter covers. In September 2017 they beat South Africa **57–0**.

### What it was standing on

And the base of it was being cut back to pay for itself.

In **January 2010** the national union announced cost savings. The **Junior All Blacks** would play no matches that year. And the **women's National Provincial Championship would not be held at all** — in a World Cup year. The general manager of provincial rugby explained that women's domestic rugby was "one of many victims of the tight financial times," and that the competition would be replaced by camps and trials for the Black Ferns. The union took, in the Museum's phrase, **"a barrage of criticism."**

Two years later Otago could not pay a five-thousand-dollar entry fee.

Set those two facts against the ones in the previous section and the shape of the generation is unmistakable. The centrally contracted national team — the asset the whole structure existed to produce — was the most valuable thing in world rugby. The provincial unions that had constituted that structure in 1892, and had spent a century insisting the game belonged to them, were being lent money by it and managed by its appointees. **Otago had walked out of the founding meeting rather than accept a central authority. A hundred and twenty years later, a central authority sent Otago a change manager.**

### The Black Ferns won it anyway

The women's response to having their competition cancelled was to go and win the **World Cup in 2010**, which was their fourth in a row.

The championship was reinstated afterwards — the Museum credits the win and "the efforts of many rugby players in New Zealand" — and Auckland Storm won the restored final 38–12, a fifth consecutive title. Among those who had spoken publicly against the cancellation was **Farah Palmer**, who had captained the Black Ferns to three of those World Cups.

On **15 December 2016**, Dr Farah Palmer — by then in the World Rugby Hall of Fame — was **elected to the board of New Zealand Rugby**, nominated by Manawatu and seconded by Hawke's Bay, and elected unanimously by the union's voting members: **twenty-six provincial unions plus the New Zealand Maori Rugby Board.** She took the seat reserved for the **Māori representative**, following the retirement of Wayne Peters.

It is worth registering both halves of that. A former Black Ferns captain took a seat on the board of New Zealand Rugby in 2016, through the Māori seat, elected by the provinces — which is to say that the two constituencies this chapter has watched being governed rather than governing, from Ellison's motion in 1893 to Otago's change manager in 2012, are the ones that finally opened the room.

### Yokohama

The end came in October **2019**, in the semi-final at **Yokohama**, where England played the game of their tournament and New Zealand never led. There was no controversy and nothing to audit: they were beaten by a better side on the day, comprehensively, in front of the world.

It ended Steve Hansen's tenure, and it ended something larger. Since 2007 New Zealand had answered a failure at a World Cup by rebuilding a machine that then won two of them. That option was no longer available, because the problem this time was not the machine. It was that everybody else could now afford one too.

### What this generation leaves behind

Two World Cups, a change manager in Dunedin, and a women's team that won a World Cup in a year its domestic competition was cancelled.

By 2019 the ownership question this chapter has followed since 1870 has been answered as completely as it ever will be: New Zealand Rugby employs the players, funds the provinces, appoints managers to the unions that founded it, and owns the most valuable jersey in the sport. It is the most centralised rugby country on earth, and for a decade that made it the best.

What follows tests the only weakness such a system can have. An employer's control lasts exactly as long as it can pay more than the alternative — and in the decade after Yokohama, Japanese clubs, French clubs and private equity all arrive at once, with more money than a country of five million people has ever been able to put on the table.

## Generation 8: The Bill (2019–2026)

**A Thursday afternoon, January 2026. Auckland.**

The chairman of New Zealand Rugby faces the media to explain why the All Blacks coach has been sacked. The board took three hours and decided unanimously. **"The All Blacks are not on track the way they need to be for the 2027 World Cup,"** he says. Asked what the review found, he speaks of "a consistent theme, a consistent direction delivered to us which we then followed up on and investigated further," and declines to elaborate.

**Scott Robertson**, fifty-one years old, four straight Super Rugby titles with the Crusaders behind him and once described as the future of the All Blacks, is gone after two years.

The chairman explaining it is **David Kirk.**

The same David Kirk who, in 1986, was one of exactly two All Blacks who refused to go to South Africa with the Cavaliers. The same David Kirk who, in 1987, lifted the first World Cup at Eden Park.

And he had arrived in the chair from the **presidency of the New Zealand Rugby Players Association** — the players' own organisation — which he resigned in order to take it, the board's independence rules requiring it.

He is now the man who decides who coaches New Zealand, and the standard he applies is a tournament in Australia twenty-one months away.

---

That is where a hundred and fifty-six years of this story arrives: at a governance decision, taken unanimously, measured against a World Cup, announced by a man who was once the conscience of the team.

And the money that made it so urgent had arrived four years earlier.

### The letter

In 2021 New Zealand Rugby proposed to sell a stake in itself. The buyer was the American private equity firm **Silver Lake**; the deal on the table was worth **$465 million** for **15 percent of NZR's commercial rights.**

The players said no.

**A letter signed by senior All Blacks and Black Ferns** went to New Zealand Rugby expressing concern about the sale, and the union and the **Players' Association** went into mediation. It is worth being precise about what was being argued over, because this chapter has been circling it since 1888: NZR's commercial rights *are* the players. The jersey, the fern, the haka, the unbeaten record at Eden Park — the asset being valued at nearly half a billion dollars is the thing Joe Warbrick's private venture assembled in 1888 and Thomas Ellison persuaded the union to adopt in 1893.

In 1907 Albert Baskerville answered the question by taking a team abroad and being threatened with life bans. In 1986 the Cavaliers answered it by going anyway. In 2021 the players answered it with a signed letter and a mediator — which is either a great deal less romantic or a great deal more effective, depending on your view of how institutions are actually changed.

### The sale

The deal that eventually went through was smaller than the one proposed: **$200 million**, for a new entity called **CommercialCo** which would **control all of NZR's revenue-generating assets** while remaining under NZR's control, with up to **$100 million** more to be offered to New Zealand institutional investors later.

And the vote that cleared the final hurdle, at a special general meeting in Auckland, was taken by **the twenty-six provincial unions and the Māori Rugby Board.**

Read that against the rest of this chapter. The same federal electorate that was assembled at the Club Hotel in 1892 — the provinces, who would not be governed, who took the Ranfurly Shield onto their own grounds, who by 2012 could not pay a five-thousand-dollar entry fee — voted to sell a share of the national game to a private equity firm. They were not tricked into it and they were not overruled. **They were paid**: of the $37 million set aside for immediate investment, **$20 million went to the provincial unions — $14 million to the NPC unions and $6 million to the Heartland unions.**

Six million dollars to the Heartland: to Buller, to the West Coast, to the small unions descended from the clubs where the game was played over two days between Banks and Lawyers and All Corners.

The chairman of the day called it "a transformational moment for rugby in Aotearoa at every level of the game," and said the challenge was now to invest in the right places — "in particular at the grassroots, in teenage and women's rugby."

### 12 November 2022

Which brings us to the best afternoon in this generation, and it was not the All Blacks.

Twelve years after their domestic competition was cancelled to save money in a World Cup year, the **Black Ferns** played England in a **World Cup final at Eden Park**, in front of **a record crowd of more than 42,000** — the largest that had ever watched a women's rugby match. They won **34–31**, ending England's thirty-match winning streak.

**Ruahei Demant**, the co-captain and player of the match, afterwards:

> **"Last year we went on the northern tour and we got pumped. We sacrificed so much to get the one chance to win a World Cup at home and we did it. We hope we have made our country proud and inspired the next generation of Black Ferns."**

The team New Zealand Rugby had once replaced with "camps and trials" filled the country's largest ground and beat the best-funded side in the women's game.

### The men's decade

For the All Blacks it went the other way.

They lost the **2023 World Cup final to South Africa by a single point**, 12–11 — the second final lost to the Springboks, twenty-eight years after the first. In **2025** South Africa beat them **43–10 in Wellington**, the heaviest defeat in New Zealand's history, eight years after New Zealand had beaten South Africa 57–0. In January 2026 the coach was dismissed.

Underneath the results, the structural problem was the one Gen 7 predicted. A system that owns its players keeps them only while it can outbid everyone else, and by the 2020s it could not. Japanese and French clubs pay more than New Zealand can, and the pathway that once ran from a provincial union to a Super Rugby franchise to the All Blacks now has an exit at every stage.

### Where things stood in mid-2026

**18 July 2026. Eden Park, Auckland.**

New Zealand beat **Ireland 40–21** in front of **more than 48,000 people**, six tries to three, leading 28–7 at half-time. Patrick Tuipulotu scored the first, converted by Ruben Love; the captain **Ardie Savea** went over from the base of a scrum for the second.

It was the third win from three under the new coach, **Dave Rennie**, completing the first leg of the **inaugural Nations Championship** after victories over France and Italy. And it extended the All Blacks' unbeaten record at Eden Park to **fifty-three Tests**. They have not lost at that ground since **1994** — which is to say that no New Zealand side has been beaten there since the year before the game turned professional.

Ahead of them lay a four-Test series in South Africa, and beyond that the **2027 World Cup in Australia**, the first with twenty-four teams — the tournament New Zealand and Australia had to persuade the northern unions to allow in the first place, now grown to a size that would have been unimaginable to the men who sold its marketing rights for five million dollars in 1987.

### What this chapter leaves behind

New Zealand is the country where rugby had no class, and the reason is not in the rules — the rules were posted out from England, the same ones that were used at home to keep working men out of the game. It is in what carried it. A watchmaker's business, a bank clerk's promotion, a provincial union, a freezing works, a Māori touring side that invented the jersey before there was anyone to authorise it. A game that arrives before the machinery that might sort people, and is carried by institutions that have no interest in sorting them, ends up belonging to everybody.

But a game that belongs to everybody still has to be held by somebody, and that is the argument this chapter has actually been about. Rowland Hill refereeing the Natives and dictating their apology. Otago and Canterbury walking out of the room in 1892. Baskerville's men threatened with life bans, and the union's own colour bar administered in the same decade it was refused a seat at the imperial table. Two lawyers stopping a tour in 1985 and the whole squad going anyway in 1986. A draft, in 1996. A change manager sent to Dunedin in 2012. A letter from the players in 2021, and a vote by twenty-six provinces to sell in 2022.

New Zealand made rugby a national possession, and has spent a century and a half arguing about who is holding it. In 2026 the answer is: a commercial entity part-owned by an American investment firm, ratified by the twenty-six provinces, governed by a board on which the Māori seat is held by a former Black Ferns captain and the chair by a man who came to it from the players' union and who once refused to tour South Africa — and the game itself, still, by everybody.
