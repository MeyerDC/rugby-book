# France

## Generation 0: The British in Le Havre and Paris (1872–1892)

**20 March 1892. Bagatelle, in the Bois de Boulogne.** The first French rugby championship is decided in a single match between the two great clubs of Paris, and the referee is **Baron Pierre de Coubertin**.

Racing Club de France beat Stade Français **4–3**. It is close to the end: *le Stade Français rate de peu une pénalité égalisatrice lors de la dernière minute de jeu* — Stade Français narrowly miss an equalising penalty in the final minute.

The two sides do not agree on how to play. Racing put out two fullbacks, three three-quarters, two halves and eight forwards; Stade Français answer with **one fullback and nine**. In the Racing line is a man listed as Pujol, who is in fact **Robert Pellevé de la Motte-Ango, marquis de Flers**, a future playwright and member of the Académie française; alongside him the **Peruvian Gonzales de Candamo brothers**, a fullback named Vénot who is that year's French long-jump champion, and a Parisian of Polish descent, **Alexandre Sienkiewicz**, whose uncle is about to write *Quo Vadis*.

Two years after this afternoon, the man with the whistle will found the International Olympic Committee. He is, at this point in his life, the most articulate advocate in France of the English public-school games ethic — of athletics as moral training, played by gentlemen, for nothing.

Within forty years French rugby will have left Paris for the farms and tanneries of the south-west, will be paying its players under the table, and will be expelled from international rugby by the very unions whose amateur creed Coubertin spent his life exporting.

---

The game had come ashore twenty years before Bagatelle, and not in Paris.

**Le Havre, 1872.** British merchants — former Oxford and Cambridge men, working in the trading houses of the port — had been gathering on open ground by the sea to play. That year the crews of two Royal Navy vessels in the harbour came ashore and played them. The club those merchants belonged to became **Le Havre Athletic Club** in 1884, and is still described as the oldest sports club in France.

What they were playing was not rugby. They called it **"la combination"** — an English word, kept in English — and it was a deliberate mixture of rugby and association football: a game, as Pascal Charitas describes it, "without pre-defined conditions" as to its rules or the dimensions of its field. It lasted at Le Havre for twenty-two years.

It lasted because the club wanted it to. Charitas found the hybrid written into the statutes, which **marginalised rugby and football separately, in favour of the combined game, from 1872 to 1894**. An omnisports club in those years competed for members and prestige, and a club whose members divided into a rugby section and a football section risked becoming two smaller clubs. So the compromise on the field was a decision taken in committee. **France's oldest club played neither code cleanly for twenty-two years because an undivided club was worth more than a tidy game.**

Le Havre's claim to be the beginning is disputed by French historians, and bluntly. *« On nous dit, le rugby arrive en France au Havre en 1872. C'est faux ! Il arrive à Paris en 1877 et à Bordeaux en 1892. La matrice d'un sport qui est essentiellement provincial c'est Bordeaux ! »* — **Jean-Pierre Bodis**, in a newspaper interview in 1994: we are told rugby arrives in France at Le Havre in 1872; it is false; it arrives in Paris in 1877 and in Bordeaux in 1892, and the matrix of a sport that is essentially provincial is Bordeaux.

The argument is not pedantry, and the historians know why it is fought. As Charitas puts it, the virgin myth of origins is a struggle, with symbolic and economic consequences, and Le Havre serves as a symbol of resistance — against the hegemony of the capital on one side and the historic basin of the south-west on the other. Three regions, three claims, one sport that had not yet decided what it was.

Rugby did not arrive in France at a place on a date. It seeped in through British commercial communities in several ports and in the capital across the 1870s and 1880s, in forms not yet clearly one game or another, and the founding story was assembled afterwards — the same way the game came ashore at Batumi and Poti, at Coronel and Iquique, and left Montevideo's 1880s in archival silence.

### The pigeon-shooting ground

Bodis's Paris date has a club attached to it. In **1877** young English cloth merchants founded what is remembered as the capital's first rugby-football club, the **English Taylors**, and played on the **pigeon-shooting ground in the Bois de Boulogne**, where young Parisians came to watch and then to join. The two sides distinguished themselves by jersey: the Englishmen in blue with a red Maltese cross, the Parisians in blue with a white one.

The first Parisian club proper was not Racing. It was the **Paris Football Club**, founded in **1879**, which played both codes under two captains and which, in **1885**, took itself on tour to England. The results are their own comment on where French rugby stood: beaten by Old Millhilians 10–0, by Civil Service 22–0, by Hornsey Rise 26–0, by Gravesend 3–0. The club dissolved in **1886**.

So the sequence is English merchants, then a club of Anglophile Parisians, then the schools. And the schools are where the thing took.

### The Paris clubs

Where the game became organised, and among whom, is not in dispute.

The French game was started by schoolboys, and the date of each club is the date of a conversation.

**Racing Club de France** was created on **20 April 1882** by pupils of the **lycée Condorcet**, as a multi-sport club. They chose sky blue and white for their colours, *« en hommage à l'Université de Cambridge »* — the borrowing is not even disguised. Twenty months later, on **Wednesday 12 December 1883**, a handful of students from the **lycée Saint-Louis** sat in the **café Le Procope** on the boulevard Saint-Michel, across from the Sorbonne, and talked themselves into founding **Stade Français**.

Two lycées, two cafés, two sets of boys from the Parisian professional class, in a city where an English education was a mark of seriousness. They are the French equivalent of Dublin University and Wanderers, or of Blackheath and Richmond: metropolitan, well-connected, and comfortably placed to treat sport as a matter of principle rather than of wages. Nobody in either room was going to need paying to play.

They also built themselves an organiser, and they built it out of the same two clubs. On **20 November 1887** the leaders of Racing and Stade Français founded the body that became the **Union des Sociétés Françaises de Sports Athlétiques** — the USFSA, named as such on **31 January 1889**, after running its first national championships in April 1888. It was not a rugby union. It was a general sporting federation of the amateur, improving sort, in which rugby was one activity among many, arriving alongside hockey, swimming and the rest.

**Pierre de Coubertin** wound up his own rival committee, joined, and became the USFSA's secretary. And at the USFSA's fifth congress, in **1892**, he delivered what is remembered as the founding speech for the revival of the Olympic Games.

That is the year to hold up to the light. In the same season, in the same organisation, the same man refereed the final of France's first rugby championship and proposed to rebuild the Olympics around the amateur ideal. The creed that would expel France from international rugby in 1931 for paying its players was being drafted in Paris, in French, by a Frenchman, while French rugby was still one match long.

### What Coubertin was actually importing

He had gone to England as a young man and come back with a programme. What he found at **Rugby School** was Thomas Arnold's method, and what he admired most was not the games but the **self-government**: English boys running their own associations and their own competitions, without adults standing over them. He wrote it up in ***L'Éducation en Angleterre***, published in **1888**.

Then he set about installing it. The hygienists' campaign of 1887 gave him his opening, and he found his laboratory at the **École Monge** in Paris, a private lay school whose director believed the timetable could be cut back in favour of sport. On **1 June 1888** the school hosted the first meeting of Coubertin's **Comité pour la propagation des exercices physiques dans l'éducation**, chaired by **Jules Simon**, a former minister of public instruction and head of government. A month later the committee staged what it called the first English athletic game, played by the first two school sports associations in France, from the École Monge and the École Alsacienne.

In **1889** a pupil at the **Lycée Lakanal** in Sceaux founded the first sports association inside a state lycée. His name was **Frantz Reichel**, and thirty-one years later he would be the first leader of the French Rugby Federation. That same year Lakanal, Monge and the École Alsacienne played a tournament of *football-rugby*.

The schoolboys got their championship before the clubs did. The USFSA's football commission was constituted on **29 November 1890** — Coubertin among its five members — and the first interscholastic championship ran for four weeks between eight school associations, on the lawn at Saint-Cloud in the Bois de Boulogne, on a pitch lent by the headmaster of the Lycée Janson. **Michelet beat the École Alsacienne 5–3** in the final on **26 February**, refereed by the president of Stade Français, in weather so warm and a crowd so large that the players kept having to be given room.

That is the shape of French rugby before it had a national team: a game the English brought to a shooting range, taken up by lycée boys in the Bois de Boulogne, organised by a baron who wanted to reform French education, and watched by crowds who would not stay behind the touchline.

So the game that reached its first championship in 1892 was a Parisian, bourgeois, amateur possession, administered by men who believed in athletics as character-building, and refereed on the day by the future founder of the Olympic movement.

### What this generation leaves behind

A capital city's game, and a founding myth that will not survive inspection.

By 1892 French rugby had two strong clubs, a governing body that was not really a rugby governing body, a championship consisting of one match, and an origin story about a port on the Channel that its own historians would later reject.

What it did not yet have was any of the things it would become famous for. There were no crowds to speak of, no money, no violence worth remarking on, and no reason at all to think that this particular English import would end up belonging to the wine-growers and stockmen of the Garonne rather than to the young gentlemen of the Bois de Boulogne.

That changed fast, and by the same vehicle that carried the game to Montevideo and Valparaíso: British merchants, in a port, selling something.

In the case of France, the something was wine.

## Generation 1: The Parisian Game (1893–1920)

**1 January 1906. Parc des Princes.** France play their first international, and the opponents are the best team that has ever existed.

The New Zealanders touring Europe that winter are the **Originals** — Dave Gallaher's side, three months at sea and then unbeaten across Britain, playing a game nobody there has seen before. The Bibliothèque nationale's account of them is admiring: *ces joueurs de classe, virevoltent ballon en main, relancent inlassablement et marquent beaucoup d'essais* — players of class, twisting and turning with ball in hand, endlessly counter-attacking, scoring a great many tries. They have won **thirty-one of thirty-two** matches before they reach Paris.

The French press has no illusions. A notice in December 1905: *"Ce match, dans lequel les nôtres n'ont aucune chance de vaincre, est un évènement considérable dans tous les milieux sportifs"* — this match, in which ours have no chance of winning, is a considerable event in all sporting circles.

It is played in the wet. France lose **38–8**, ten tries to two, the French tries scored by **Cessieux** and **Dufourcq** with a conversion from **Pujol**. The captain is **Henri Armand**, the first man to lead a French XV.

The team sheet is not what anyone would expect.

Two of the fifteen are not French at all: **William Crichton**, an English full-back from Le Havre, and **Allan Muhr**, an American second row from Racing. And, in the Bibliothèque nationale's phrasing, *autre particularité de taille* — another notable peculiarity — **la France ce jour-là compte deux joueurs de couleur, André Vergès et le Guyanais Georges Jérôme**: France that day fielded two players of colour, André Vergès and Georges Jérôme, a Guyanese.

In January 1906 — while South Africa was beginning to use this same sport to build a white nation, and while the Home Unions were policing their own game to keep working men out of it. France's very first international side contained an Englishman, an American and two men of colour, and nobody appears to have thought it worth more than a line.

---

### Champions before they were a team

France's first honours came six years before their first international, and they came at home.

Rugby was on the programme of the **1900 Olympic Games in Paris**, played on the grass of the **Vincennes velodrome** in two rounds, on **14 and 28 October**. France beat **Frankfurt FC 27–17** and then **Moseley Wanderers 27–8**, and were the first Olympic champions the sport has had. Germany and England shared the silver. There was no bronze.

The French side was drawn from three Paris clubs — Stade Français, Racing and the Cosmopolitan Club — and it was captained by **Frantz Reichel**, who eleven years earlier had founded the first sports association in a French state lycée while still a pupil there, and who twenty years later would be the first man to run the French Rugby Federation. Reichel is the thread this generation hangs on: schoolboy organiser, Olympic champion, federation secretary.

### The province arrives

In Paris the championship was a private argument among a few clubs. **Stade Français**, known as *l'Académie du rugby*, reached ten finals and won seven of them; the run was broken in **1896** by **l'Olympique de Paris**, a side of wealthy young men who made a point of arriving at the ground in a carriage drawn by four horses, and whose number included **Jean Charcot**, later the polar explorer.

Then the south-west knocked the door in. **Stade Bordelais**, founded on **18 July 1889**, became good enough that the USFSA had to change the competition to accommodate it: from **1899** the championship ended with a final between **the champion of Paris and the champion of the departments** — and the Bordelais won it. They would win **seven championships between 1899 and 1911** and reach eleven finals, six of their victories coming against Stade Français.

The game the lycée boys had organised in the Bois de Boulogne was now being won, repeatedly, four hundred miles from Paris. In **1913** the ground at **Sainte-Germaine** in Bordeaux staged France's first match against **South Africa**.

### Into the Championship, and the bird on the shirt

In **1910** France were admitted to the Home Nations tournament, which by their arrival became the **Five Nations**. It was a considerable act of faith on somebody's part, because for four years France were beaten by more or less everyone.

They won one match in those first four seasons, and it was the first Test victory in the country's history. **2 January 1911, Stade Colombes**, on the opening day of the tournament: **France 16, Scotland 15**. The World Rugby Museum's account has France a man short for part of the build-up because a player got off the train at the wrong station, and the finish turned on Scotland knocking the ball on with a try at their mercy. France held out by a point.

What the captain did next is the part worth keeping. **Marcel Communeau**, a Stade Français forward from Beauvais who would win twenty-one caps and become the first Frenchman to reach ten and then twenty, wrote to *La Vie au Grand Air* on **14 January 1911** and asked for the jersey to be changed. The French wore **two interlaced rings**, the badge of the USFSA — the general athletics federation, the badge of sport in the abstract. Communeau proposed something else:

> *Je me fais ici l'interprète de nombreux sportsmen, qui en ont fait la remarque en demandant que l'on remplace sur notre maillot les deux anneaux enlacés par cet emblème bien plus français… : Le Coq Gaulois.*

I speak here for a good many sportsmen who have remarked on it, in asking that the two interlaced rings on our jersey be replaced by this far more French emblem: the Gallic cock.

The USFSA agreed on **21 January 1911**, three weeks after the win. The cockerel went onto the shirt, at first beside the rings; when the federation reorganised itself in 1920 the rings came off and the bird stayed.

A team that had won a single international in its history had just decided it was a nation rather than a branch of an athletics club. It was right, and it took the institutions another nine years to catch up.

### The referee and the crowd

**1 January 1913. Parc des Princes.** Twenty-five thousand people, a record for the ground, watch Scotland beat France **21–3**.

They do not blame the French. They blame the referee, an Englishman named **J. W. Baxter**, whose decisions they read as favouring the Scots, and at the final whistle they come over the barriers to get at him. Baxter reaches the dressing rooms because mounted police get to him first.

The Scottish Rugby Union's response was not a letter. **Scotland simply did not play France in 1914** — the fixture was taken off the card, while Ireland, Wales and England all came to Paris or received France that season — and the two countries did not meet again until **1920**.

It is the first time a Home Union disciplines French rugby for the behaviour that surrounds it rather than the rugby played in it, and it is eighteen years before the sentence of 1931. The charge sheet in London that year will read *conditions peu satisfaisantes*. It was already being drafted at the Parc des Princes on New Year's Day 1913.

Ten days later, on the same ground, **South Africa beat France 38–5**.

The organised French game of this period was, nonetheless, a Parisian and bourgeois affair, and the Bibliothèque nationale de France names its character exactly: **les cols blancs du Racing ou du Stade français** — the white collars of Racing and Stade Français.

Those two clubs and their circle ran the championship, supplied most of the national side, and understood the sport in the terms Coubertin had taught: an amateur pursuit, morally improving, played by men whose living came from elsewhere. The **USFSA**, the general athletics federation that administered rugby along with everything else, held the line on their behalf.

It was already failing.

### The scourge

The money arrived before the crowds did, and it arrived from the clubs.

The BnF's account of the period is blunt about the mechanism: *les clubs français fleurissent pour recruter les meilleurs joueurs contre une rémunération garantie* — French clubs were competing to recruit the best players **against a guaranteed payment**. The USFSA's response was administrative and revealing: it invented **a licence for foreigners**, specifically in order to prevent *le commerce de talents*, the trade in talent.

The RFU had met the same problem in the 1880s and answered it with a doctrine: an amateur creed written into the laws, which made payment a moral offence and drove twenty-two northern clubs out of the union in 1895. The French answer, twenty years later, was a licensing form for foreign players.

The difference matters, because it tells you what each body thought it was defending. The RFU was defending a definition of who ought to play. The USFSA was trying to stop clubs outbidding each other.

### The generation that did not come back

Then the thing stopped, and a great many of the people who played it did not come back.

The French federation's own reckoning is that of roughly **four hundred and twenty French sportsmen of the first rank in 1914, a hundred and twenty-one rugby players were recorded *morts pour la France***. **Twenty-one of them were internationals.** Across every rugby-playing country the figure was about **a hundred and thirty capped players**, with Scotland losing thirty-one and England twenty-eight.

The clubs took it in whole teams. **Stade Toulousain lost eighty-one of its members.** Of the **Perpignan** side that won the championship in **1914**, seven Catalans and three men from the Pyrenees were dead within months of lifting the shield; **Bayonne**, champions in 1913, lost five.

Three of them stand for the rest.

**Alfred Mayssonnié**, a Toulouse fly-half, played in France's very first Five Nations match in 1910. He was killed by a bullet through the heart in **September 1914**, in the first battle of the Marne, in the opening weeks. Toulouse raised a statue of **Herakles** in 1925 to the sportsmen it had lost, and it was Mayssonnié they had in mind.

**Aimé Giral** was eighteen years old and kicked the conversion that won Perpignan the **1914** final. He went up with the 80th Infantry Regiment in early 1915 and was mortally wounded at **Somme-Suippes**, in the Marne, on **22 July 1915**. Perpignan's ground carries his name to this day.

**Maurice Boyau** won six caps in the back row and captained France twice, at Stade Bordelais and then Racing. He became a fighter pilot, and then the **fifth-ranked French air ace of the war, with thirty-five confirmed victories**. He was shot down over Meurthe-et-Moselle on **16 September 1918**, eight weeks before the armistice.

Communeau himself went to war as a lieutenant in the 11th artillery on **2 August 1914**, transferred to aviation as an observer, survived a bad crash near Arras in 1915, and came home with the Croix de Guerre. He lived until 1971, which among the men in this section makes him an exception.

It took the other side of that 1906 afternoon too. The Bibliothèque nationale notes that the Originals would soon be *rattrapés par un autre devoir : défendre la liberté* — overtaken by another duty, defending liberty — and their captain **Dave Gallaher** was killed at Passchendaele in 1917.

### 1920

What emerged afterwards was a governing body of rugby's own. **In October 1920 the Fédération française de rugby emancipated itself from the USFSA** and became a full sporting federation in its own right; *la FFR existe officiellement depuis le 11 octobre 1920*, succeeding the USFSA's rugby committee. Its first leader was **Frantz Reichel**, a Racing man and an experienced administrator, who had left the presidency of the SCUF in 1919 to chair a provisional committee set up to deal with the USFSA's decay.

It began with nothing. *Si elle est sans le sou dans un premier temps, cette toute jeune Fédération cherche à maintenir l'idée de l'amateurisme ; elle doit pourtant lutter contre le « fléau » qui gangrène le rugby des clubs : l'argent.* Penniless at the outset, the young federation set out to maintain the idea of amateurism — and found itself fighting the **scourge** eating into club rugby: **money**.

A federation founded broke, committed to amateurism, and already losing to it. Everything that happens to French rugby in the next twenty years is in that sentence.

### What this generation leaves behind

**241 clubs**, and a centre of gravity about to move four hundred miles south.

That is the figure the BnF gives for the FFR's membership at its creation in 1920. By the 1930s it would be **close to six hundred** — and the growth would not be in Paris. *En dessous de la Loire, le rugby s'implante vigoureusement, chaque village a son club, chaque club a son derby.* **Below the Loire**, rugby took root hard: every village with its club, every club with its derby.

And with it went the ownership of the game. *Le rugby n'est plus seulement l'affaire des cols blancs du Racing ou du Stade français, c'est aussi celui des territoires et des petits entrepreneurs* — rugby was no longer only the business of the white collars of Racing and Stade Français; it belonged now to the regions, and to small businessmen.

The white collars kept the federation. They lost the sport.

## Generation 2: Up the Garonne (1921–1930)

**Easter Monday, 21 April 1930. Stade Olympique Yves du Manoir, Colombes.** France play Wales in the final fixture of the Championship, and the photograph that survives the afternoon is not of the rugby. It is of police officers treating unconscious spectators at the side of the pitch.

Wales win **11–0**. In the French pack that day, at number eight, is a twenty-five-year-old from Ille-sur-Têt named **Jean Galia**, who in four years will do more damage to the French rugby establishment than any opponent ever managed.

The ground itself has form. Six years earlier, on **18 May 1924**, it staged the **Olympic rugby final**, in which the **United States beat France 17–3** in front of fifty thousand people, in a match violent enough to end in a pitch invasion and the American team requiring police protection to leave. Romania took the bronze.

Within a year of this Easter Monday, France will be expelled from international rugby.

Now go back and see how a Parisian gentlemen's game became a sport that needed police at the touchline.

---

It went south, and it went south along a river.

The vehicle was the one that carried the game into Montevideo, Buenos Aires and Valparaíso: **British merchants in a port, selling something**. In France the something was **wine**. "The biggest sports committee outside of Paris was set up in **Bordeaux in 1888**, chiefly promoting rugby and gymnastics. Rugby took hold quickly in the city, **aided by the large number of British wine traders then in residence**."

From there it moved inland fast. The sociologist **Christian Pociello** describes the enthusiasm spreading out of Bordeaux "**like a wind whipping up and channelling rugby's spread like wildfire, towards Agen, Toulouse and Carcassonne, before the flames went on to take hold in Perpignan**."

Agen, Toulouse, Carcassonne, Perpignan: market towns and provincial cities strung along the Garonne and then down towards the Mediterranean, none of them Paris, none of them rich. That arc is where the four hundred new clubs of the following decade would appear.

### Who picked it up

The men who played it were not the men who had played it in the Bois de Boulogne.

"Countless local clubs were set up in the region's towns and villages in the early 20th century, with many of the most prized players **recruited from jobs reliant on manual labour: mines, farms, leatherworks and livestock markets**."

The historian **Philip Dine**, whose *French Rugby Football: A Cultural History* is the standard scholarly account, describes the sequence precisely — and the order matters. "After this initial period when social elites developed and played the sport, in the southwest especially rugby became the sport of **working-class neighbourhoods and then of villages**, hence its enduring and intimate association with a traditional, rural *France profonde*."

Neighbourhoods first, then villages. The game went from the Parisian bourgeoisie to the urban working class of the southern towns, and only then out into the countryside that would become its permanent image.

There was also a fight over French children's souls going on, and it cut the other way from everywhere else in this book. As Dine's reviewer summarises it: "The battle between Church and State under the early Third Republic was also waged in sport. **Secular sports clubs and federations promoted and played rugby, while their Catholic rivals of the Fédération Gymnastique et Sportive des Patronages de France** — determined to compete for the bodies (and souls) of French youth — **favoured the less violent game of soccer**."

That is the exact inverse of what happened in Uruguay, where the Christian Brothers chose rugby for their boys and the Jesuits chose football, and the choice shaped Uruguayan sport for a century. In France the Catholic sporting federation looked at rugby, decided it was too violent, and took football instead — and so rugby in France became, among other things, the **secular republic's game**.

### The jobs

And then the money, which was never quite money.

Dine's finding on how the great southern clubs actually assembled their sides is the most useful sentence in this chapter: "**teams recruited top players from other towns by promising them jobs in local businesses and industry, a particularly effective enticement in times of high unemployment like the 1930s.**"

Not a wage for playing. **A job.** A place at the tannery, the sawmill, the wine warehouse, the municipal works — arranged by men who wanted the club to win, in towns where work was scarce and a strong young man's alternative was the fields.

When the RFU wrote its amateur laws in 1886 it had banned exactly this, and in far more detail than it banned cash: it went after the job found for the player, the compensation for time lost, the "broken time" that would split English rugby in 1895. The northern English clubs had done the same thing for the same reason, and the RFU had driven them out of the union for it.

France was now doing it at scale, across an entire region, in the open, and calling itself amateur.

**Stade Toulousain** won five championships in the decade — 1922, 1923 and 1924 in succession, then 1926 and 1927 — before crowds that made rugby the *sport-king* of the south-west. The game was, in the phrase of the period, torn between amateur and professional status, with a great many nominally amateur players receiving something.

### Twenty-one years

While the region was taking the game over, the national side finally won the argument it had been losing since 1906.

France had first played **England** in March 1906 and been beaten **35–8**. They then lost to them, and lost to them, and lost to them again: by one account a run of **eighteen matches without a win** — seventeen defeats and a single drawn game, **11–11 in London in 1922**.

It ended on **2 April 1927**, on the last afternoon of the Championship, at Colombes. The wing **Edmond Vellat**, of Grenoble, scored a try in the first half. Nobody scored anything else. **France 3, England 0**, and the captain who led them, **Adolphe Jauréguy** of Stade Français, became the first Frenchman to beat the Rose.

Twenty-one years, and a single try. It is a very small scoreline for what it meant, and it arrived in the middle of the decade in which the sport changed hands — while Bourrel was assembling his team in the Aude and the Languedoc pool was turning into a running battle. The French game was becoming good and becoming ungovernable at the same time, and the two things had the same cause.

### The hat maker

The clearest case is a town of a few thousand people in the Aude, and a man who made soft hats.

**Jean Bourrel** was the most successful industrialist in his department and the first great patron French rugby had. What he wanted was a rugby team, and he bought one the way another man might buy a motor car. When **Perpignan** fell out among themselves after winning the 1925 championship, Bourrel took **half the Catalan side** — including the internationals **Eugène Ribère** and **Marcel Baillette** — and their coach **Gilbert Brutus**, and installed them at **Quillan**, at the top of the Aude valley. Over the next two seasons he added five or six more internationals from other clubs.

Quillan reached the final in **1928**, won the championship in **1929**, and reached the final again in **1930**. The club's own president, ninety years later, puts it without embarrassment: *« Il a en fait créé la première équipe professionnelle du monde »* — he actually created the first professional team in the world.

The detail is what convinces. The players trained almost every day, in a town whose whole working population was making hats. They had a physiotherapist and the beginnings of a diet. And when they travelled, they wore **Thibet hats**, one of Bourrel's own brands, which they handed out to the opposing supporters: a rugby team as a travelling advertisement for its owner's factory.

This was eleven years after the federation was founded, in a sport whose rules said that no player might be paid.

One of the men he bought is the thread that runs out of this decade and into the next two. **Eugène Ribère** was born at **Thuir**, in the Pyrénées-Orientales, in **1902**, and came up through **Perpignan** before following the money over the hills to Quillan. He was a back-row forward of eighty-two kilos who was reckoned as good going forward as he was stopping other people, and he won **thirty-four caps between 1924 and 1933**, **twelve of them as captain**.

That was the French record, and it stood for twenty years — in an era when a man might win four or five caps in a season, so that thirty-four meant a decade in the side. The player who finally passed him, in **1952**, was Jean Prat of Lourdes.

So the most-capped Frenchman of the age was a Catalan who was bought away from his home club by a hat manufacturer, in the years when his country was being thrown out of international rugby for exactly that. Nobody at the time seems to have found this remarkable.

### Lézignan-la-matraque

The competition it was played in had stopped pretending too.

To keep travel costs down, the championship began with regional pools, and the **Languedoc pool — Perpignan, Narbonne, Béziers, Carcassonne, Lézignan and Quillan** — was, in the phrase of the French journalist who reconstructed it, a powder keg. At **Lézignan** the coach **Jean Sébédio**, known as **the Sultan**, ran training with a whip in his hand, and the station master met visiting teams off the train with a cry that told them what was coming: *« Lézignan-la-matraque, tout le monde descend »* — Lézignan-the-cosh, everybody out.

The two of them met in the **1929 final**, at Ponts-Jumeaux in Toulouse. **Marcel de Laborderie**, in *Le Miroir des sports*, described the afternoon: *« Le match tout entier ne fut qu'une succession de bagarres, de batailles rangées… interrompues de temps en temps par quelques mouvements de rugby de bonne classe… Et l'on vit ce spectacle unique de joueurs, laissant le ballon de côté, combattre à coups de poing, à coups de pied, puis, séparés par l'arbitre, se débarrasser de celui-ci pour reprendre le combat à poings nus. »* The whole match was a succession of brawls and pitched battles, interrupted now and then by some good-class rugby; players abandoned the ball to fight with fists and boots, were separated by the referee, got rid of the referee, and went back to fighting bare-fisted.

An hour in, with Lézignan leading 8–0, Sébédio pulled a wad of banknotes out of his wallet and threw it towards the presidential box, shouting: *« Vous voyez, le pognon n'est pas tout en rugby. »* You see — money isn't everything in rugby.

Quillan won anyway.

The men in the stand could take a hint. Lézignan were banned by the federation, Quillan's imported players were excluded by their own regional committee, and both decisions were then reversed. Twelve of the biggest clubs in the country gave up on the federation altogether and founded a rival one, the **UFRA**, whose players were no longer selected for France. And the deaths kept happening: the Quillan hooker **Gaston Rivière** died in a collapsed scrum on **20 March 1927**, in the match where Perpignan met the men who had left them.

### What this generation leaves behind

A sport that has changed hands, and a bill about to be presented.

By 1930 French rugby was the possession of the working towns and villages below the Loire; it was adding clubs faster than the federation could count them; it recruited by finding men work; and it was played hard enough that a Championship afternoon in Colombes needed police to carry spectators off the field.

Two weeks after that Wales match, on **4 May 1930**, in an **Agen–Pau** championship semi-final, an eighteen-year-old named **Michel Pradié** was tackled, suffered a displaced vertebra, and died within hours.

In London, the four Home Unions were watching all of it, and had begun to compare notes.

## Generation 3: Expelled (1931–1946)

**2 March 1931. London.** The four Home Unions meet, and issue a sentence that removes France from international rugby for the next sixteen years.

> *Étant donné les conditions peu satisfaisantes dans lesquelles le rugby-football est dirigé et joué en France, ni nos fédérations ni les clubs dépendant de notre juridiction ne pourront organiser de match avec la France ou les clubs français.*

Given the unsatisfactory conditions in which rugby football is directed and played in France, neither our federations nor the clubs under our jurisdiction may organise a match with France or French clubs — until, the statement went on, they had *la certitude que le contrôle et la conduite du jeu sont organisés de manière satisfaisante*: certainty that the control and conduct of the game were satisfactorily organised.

Not just the national side. **The clubs too.** Every fixture, at every level, between France and Britain, stopped.

---

What the men in that room believed they were doing matters, because the easy reading is the wrong one.

Four charges lay behind the decision. **Disguised professionalism** — the jobs and payments of the previous generation, which the Welsh union's account records baldly: "in 1931, it was revealed that some French players were being paid by their clubs — in breach of the amateur ethos of the game." **Unregulated recruitment** between clubs. **Internal strife** inside the FFR, where a group of clubs had gone into open dissidence in December 1930. And **brutality**, in internationals and in the French championship alike.

The last of those was not an abstraction. Ten months before the meeting, on **4 May 1930**, in an **Agen–Pau** championship semi-final, an eighteen-year-old called **Michel Pradié** was tackled, suffered a displaced vertebra, and died within hours. Six weeks before that, at Colombes, police had carried unconscious spectators off the side of the pitch during the Wales match.

A boy was dead. The four unions that had spent sixty years insisting rugby was a moral training for gentlemen looked at France and saw a game they no longer recognised — being played for jobs, recruited for like labour, and killing people. From inside their own logic, the decision follows.

It is also true that those same unions had built the amateur creed in the first place as an instrument for deciding who was entitled to play, had used it in 1895 to expel their own northern clubs for compensating working men, and were now applying it to a foreign country whose game had passed into the hands of tanners and stockmen. Both things are true at once, and the chapter does not need to choose between them.

The sentence was passed on 2 March. The fixture list was not. On **6 April 1931**, five weeks after London had shut the door, France played England and beat them **14–13** — the last match they would play against any of the four for sixteen years, and they won it.

### The other Europe

The isolation ran, in practice, from 1931 to 1947, and France spent it building a replacement.

In **1933**, at **Turin**, five federations the International Rugby Board did not govern — **Italy, France, Germany, Romania and Czechoslovakia** — constituted the **Fédération Internationale de Rugby Amateur**. The organising meeting followed in **Paris on 2 January 1934**, on French and German initiative, and the first president elected was a Frenchman, **Roger Dantou**. Others came in behind them: the Netherlands, Portugal, Belgium, and **Catalonia**, sitting for Spain. The purpose was to promote and administer the game in Europe outside the board's authority, and the congresses met at Rome, Berlin, Paris and Bucharest until the war stopped them.

Expelled from one international system, France had helped build another and taken the head of it. That federation is the direct ancestor of the competition in which Romanian and Georgian rugby would later live their whole international lives.

What the alternative actually looked like, season by season, is best read off the fixture list.

In **1932** France played one international: **Germany**, away, won 20–4. In **1933** they played one international: **Germany**, at the Parc des Princes, won 38–17. In **1934** they played one international: **Germany**, at Hannover, won 13–9. Italy appears in **1935**, beaten 44–6 in Italy; Germany is played twice in **1936** and twice more in **1938**; Romania enters the fixture list in **1937**. Between **1932 and 1938 France played Germany nine times**, and for three consecutive years Germany was the only country France played at all.

One French account of the period puts it as *à se contenter de matchs contre l'Allemagne nazie et l'Italie fasciste* — making do with matches against Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy. The fixture list says it without needing the adjectives. That is where a sport goes when the democracies stop playing it. Not into reform. Into the only fixtures available.

It was also, briefly, a good side. In **October 1937** France won the **Coupe d'Europe**, the FIRA tournament contested by six nations — France, Italy, Germany, Romania, Belgium and the Netherlands. They beat **Romania 27–11** on the 14th and **Italy 43–5** in the final on the 17th, scoring seventy points in two matches and conceding sixteen. It is the only trophy French rugby won in the wilderness, and almost nobody in Britain noticed it had been played for.

The run ended on **27 March 1938**, when France lost **3–0** to Germany, in Germany — their only defeat of the entire isolation. It was fifteen days after the Anschluss.

At home, meanwhile, nothing stopped. The championship that had produced the violence and the paid men ran without interruption through the decade — **Toulon** in 1931, **Lyon OU**, **Aviron Bayonnais** in 1934, **Biarritz** in 1935, **Narbonne** in 1936, **CS Vienne** in 1937, **Perpignan** in 1938, **Biarritz** again in 1939. The clubs at the centre of the 1931 scandal went on winning the competition that had caused it, in front of their own crowds, for sixteen years, and the absence of Wales made no visible difference to any of them.

The reconciliation, when it came, was administrative. The FFR settled with its dissident clubs in **1932** and eventually reached an agreement with its clubs on the amateur rules, and on that basis France was **readmitted in 1939** — at which point the war removed the Championship altogether, and France did not play in it again until **1947**.

### Galia

There was one more place the game could go, and it was the one the Home Unions had not thought about.

Dine's account of the mechanism is exact: union's violent reputation "led British teams to refuse to play their French counterparts. **This created an opening** for a more recent version of the sport, rugby *league*." Playing a more attractive style and achieving some international success, professional rugby league "emerged as a **credible alternative** to rugby union in France in the 1930s."

The man who walked through the opening was **Jean Galia** — *rugbyman à XV et aventurier dans l'âme*, born at Ille-sur-Têt in 1905, capped by France from 1927, and a member of the French pack in that bruising Colombes afternoon of April 1930.

What sent him was not an idea. It was a ban. The FFR struck Galia off for taking a payment to play, which is to say for doing what the clubs of his region had been doing to everybody since the 1920s, and he went to the other code aggrieved. He had already seen it: a professional international played in France, which the French press filed under *neo rugby*. What took him was the style and the crowd — *la liberté du jeu, la vitesse des passes et **l'esprit ouvrier des tribunes*** — the freedom of the play, the speed of the passing, and **the working-class spirit of the stands**.

On **4 March 1934**, seventeen men met him at the **Hôtel du Palais d'Orsay** in Paris and were christened **les Pionniers**. None of them had played the thirteen-a-side game. They went to England to learn it, put up at the **Griffin Hotel in Leeds**, and were coached by two former league men, **Jonty Parkin** and **George Thompson**, before being sent out to play five matches in seventeen days. They lost at Wigan **30–27**, at Leeds **25–17**, at Warrington **32–16** and at Salford **35–13**. On **26 March**, at Hull, they won **26–23**.

The **Ligue Française de Rugby à XIII** was registered on **6 April 1934**, with **François Cadoret** as its president and Galia as the reason it existed. Nine days later, on **15 April**, France played England at the **Stade Buffalo** in Montrouge in front of a full ground of some twenty thousand, and lost **21–32**.

It caught immediately. *En moins de deux ans, les terrains se remplissent de curieux et de passionnés* — inside two years the grounds were filling. The new code started with a couple of dozen clubs and counted them in the hundreds within three seasons; by **1939 there were more than two hundred clubs** affiliated to the league, and France had beaten Wales to take a rugby league European championship of their own.

The shape of that is worth being exact about. A code invented in the north of England as the working man's answer to the RFU's amateur laws had arrived in the one country in Europe whose rugby had just been expelled by those same laws' authors — and it had found, in the towns of the south-west, precisely the constituency it was built for.

### 19 December 1941

The FFR's answer was not to compete. It was to have rugby league abolished.

The intention was announced long before it was executed. On **22 August 1940**, ten weeks after the armistice, Vichy's Secretary of State for Youth and the Family, **Jean Ybarnégaray**, settled the question in public: *« Le sort du rugby à XIII est clair, il a vécu… rayé purement et simplement du sport français. »* The fate of rugby league is clear — it has had its day, and it is to be struck out of French sport purely and simply. In **October 1940** the commissioner for sport ended professionalism in the code with immediate effect; that **November**, several dozen school and university league clubs were banned.

The decree merely finished it. **Décret n° 5285, signed by Maréchal Philippe Pétain on 19 December 1941**, countersigned by **Jérôme Carcopino**, the minister of national education and youth, and published in the *Journal Officiel* on the 27th, dissolved the Ligue française de rugby à XIII. **The practice of the sport was entirely prohibited.** Its property, moveable and immoveable, was **seized and transferred to the Comité national des sports** for liquidation — millions of francs of it, by the league's own reckoning. The **155 to 159 clubs** on its books as of June 1940, thirteen of them professional and the rest amateur, ceased to exist. Galia, who had built the thing, was banned from sport altogether.

The federation's own historians name who pushed for it: **Albert Ginesty**, president of the FFR, and **Paul Voivenel**, its honorary president, who appear to have submitted a report on rugby to **Jean Borotra**, Vichy's Commissioner for Sport.

And the regime had its own reason to agree, which Dine identifies: **Vichy decided that the amateur and ruralist values embodied by *le rugby à quinze* more closely fitted its ideological programme** and, in his reviewer's phrase, "**with the ready cooperation of that sport's grateful officials**, took concrete measures to reassert the primacy of rugby union."

Read that against the regime's motto. *Travail, Famille, Patrie*; the cult of the soil and the village; the return to a rural France uncorrupted by money and cities. Fifteen-a-side rugby, by 1941, had a story about itself that fitted that programme perfectly — the amateur game of the countryside — and thirteen-a-side had a story that did not, being openly professional, urban and working-class by design.

The FFR did not invent Vichy's ideology. It recognised that its own myth about itself was the one the new state wanted to hear, and it said so, at the moment when saying so would destroy a rival.

England's RFU had used bylaws in 1895 to push the northern working class out of its union. The FFR, in 1941, used a collaborationist government to abolish the working-class code outright and take its money.

### The fly-half from Port-Vendres

The man who supervised the operation had won the French championship twice.

**Joseph Pascot** — *Jep* — was born at **Port-Vendres in 1897**, schooled at the Collège de Perpignan, and enlisted in aviation three days after his eighteenth birthday. He came back from the war to play fly-half for **USP Perpignan** — the shirt of **Aimé Giral**, dead at Somme-Suippes in 1915, whose name is on the ground — won the **Bouclier de Brennus in 1921 and again in 1925**, and was capped **six times** by France. Then he went into the colonial artillery and stayed there, rising to lieutenant-colonel, a career soldier who by his own account avoided politics to the point of declining to vote.

In **August 1940** he became director of sport in **Jean Borotra**'s cabinet at Vichy, and from **April 1942 until August 1944** he ran French sport himself. He made physical education compulsory from primary school to university and put sport into the baccalauréat. He abolished professionalism. He cultivated the regional *petites patries*. And he described what he was for in a phrase that belongs entirely to its moment: sport as *« rénovateur de la race française »*, the renovator of the French race.

Set his two careers side by side. Pascot took the Brennus with Perpignan in **1925** — the championship side that **Jean Bourrel** then stripped for Quillan, buying away half the Catalan team and its coach to make what Quillan's own club now calls the first professional team in the world. Sixteen years later, the fly-half from that dismantled side was the official charged with abolishing the professional code.

At the Liberation he was tried by the **Haute Cour** and sentenced to five years of national indignity. **The sentence was annulled immediately, for acts of resistance.** He spent the rest of his life writing the history of Roussillon.

### Fifty-two years

The code came back after the Liberation, but not under its own name. Forbidden the words *rugby à XIII*, it was made to call itself *jeu à XIII* — the game of thirteen — and the FFR fought to keep it that way through the courts for decades.

It ended on **4 June 1993**, when the **Cour de cassation** *déboute définitivement la FFR*: definitively dismissed the French Rugby Federation's case.

Fifty-two years after Pétain signed the decree, a French court finally allowed the other code to call itself rugby.

### What this generation leaves behind

A game readmitted to a world that had stopped, a rival destroyed by decree, and a country about to be extremely good at this sport.

By 1946 France had been outside British rugby for fifteen years. It had built its own international federation and won its own European tournament; it had played Germany nine times and Wales not once; it had run its domestic championship straight through the quarrel without missing a season; and it had watched an entire rival federation dissolved and asset-stripped in its favour by a government it had asked to do it.

None of it made the French worse at rugby. In eight years they would beat the All Blacks; in thirteen they would win the Championship outright; and the man who did most to make that happen was already playing, in Lourdes, for a club nobody outside the Pyrenees had heard of.

## Generation 4: Monsieur Rugby (1947–1962)

**Saturday 16 August 1958. Ellis Park, Johannesburg. Kick-off 3.15 p.m., in front of a hundred thousand people.** France beat South Africa **9–5** and take the series.

The Springboks have not lost a home series in **sixty-two years**. They lose this one to a country that, twelve years earlier, had not been allowed to play British rugby at all.

The *Sunday Express* reported the afternoon in five words: **"At Ellis Park, a man cried."** The man was South African.

The French captain is **Lucien Mias**, a lock forward and a medical doctor, who on the eve of the match — overcome with nerves — is said to have prescribed himself his own medicine. Man of the match. The first Test at Newlands had been drawn **3–3**; this one settles it.

For France, almost everything about the tour was a first. The first time the team had played outside Europe. The first time they had toured a Commonwealth country at all, arriving in **Salisbury** on 8 July. The first time they were issued blazers. The first time anyone called them **les Tricolores**. And the first time they beat the Springboks.

Now go back eleven years, and watch how a pariah became the best team in Europe.

---

**1 January 1947.** France play Scotland and win **8–3**. It is their first Championship match in sixteen years, and it is played by a team most of whose members had never been eligible for one.

They took two of the four that season, beating Ireland **12–8** in Dublin and losing at home to Wales and away to England. Nobody called it a return to form, because there was no form to return to. The generation that might have had one had spent its twenties in a war.

Two years later France did something nobody in Europe had done. In **August and September 1949** they toured **Argentina** and won both Tests, **5–0** and **12–3** — the first official visit by a European national side to South America. A country that had spent the 1930s unable to get a fixture with its neighbours went and found opponents on another continent, and began a series that would run for the next thirty years.

The country that came back was not the one that had been thrown out. The clubs of the south-west had gone on playing throughout — the isolation had, if anything, concentrated the domestic game — and the man who now emerged from them had been at his club since he was a boy.

### Prat

**Jean Prat** was born in **Lourdes in 1923**, made his senior debut for **FC Lourdes** at full-back **at the age of fourteen**, and never played for anybody else. World Rugby's Hall of Fame records the rest without needing to embroider: he was "the first player in international rugby to reach **50 caps**", won all **51** of them between **1945 and 1955**, played as a flanker, and **captained France sixteen times**. He scored more than a hundred points for his country, which a back-row forward of that era was not supposed to do, because he kicked as well as he tackled.

The English gave him the name. After France won at Twickenham in **1955**, the British press started calling him **Monsieur Rugby**, and it stuck in his own country.

His club became the country. Lourdes won the championship in **1948, 1952, 1953, 1956, 1957 and 1958**, and lost the final in 1945, 1946 and 1955; it took the **Coupe de France** in 1950 and 1951 and the **Challenge Yves du Manoir** in 1953, 1954 and 1956. Between **1948 and 1960 it did not lose a match at home** — twelve years and more than a hundred games, counting the cups.

A market town in the Hautes-Pyrénées of some fifteen thousand people, known to the rest of the world as a place of pilgrimage, ran French rugby for a decade.

And it did not do it by being hard. The Lourdes of the 1950s was built on rehearsed attacking moves, drilled over and over in training and called by name on the field — *la cla*, *le Jeanjean*, *Opération Casquette*, the last of them named after the cap that the stand-off **François Labazuy** was known by. Prat's brother **Maurice** played in the centre; **Roger Martine** and **Henri Domec** were alongside them. The thing French rugby would later be loved for — the ball moving, the support runner arriving, the move nobody else had seen — was being invented in the Pyrenees, on a training pitch, by a club that treated practice as the point.

That is the south-west's arrival, stated as a fact rather than a theory. Not Bordeaux, not Toulouse, not any of the cities. A Pyrenean town.

### 27 February 1954

Of Prat's nine international tries, the Hall of Fame singles out one: "the one that saw **France beat New Zealand for the first time, 3–0**, in 1954."

Colombes, February, the same ground where the police had carried spectators off in 1930. A single try, no conversion, nothing else scored by anybody. The All Blacks of that era remembered the afternoon, in one account, as a tragedy.

Three-nil is not a scoreline that flatters anyone, and it is worth saying that this was not yet *le rugby-champagne*, the expansive attacking style for which French sides would later be celebrated. This was a French pack beating a New Zealand pack, once, by the width of a try.

But it was the first time, and it happened forty-eight years after the Originals had put thirty-eight points on France at the Parc des Princes.

And it was one afternoon in the most crowded year French rugby had ever had. In **1954** France played **nine internationals**. They beat Scotland and Ireland, lost in Wales, beat England, and finished level at the top of the Championship with England and Wales — **a first title, shared**. They beat the All Blacks in February. In April they won the **Coupe d'Europe**, the tournament their own federation had helped found in the wilderness years, putting **28–3** past West Germany and **39–12** past Italy in the final. And in August and September they went back to **Argentina** and won both Tests again, **22–8** and **30–3**.

A country that had been unable to arrange a fixture with Wales twenty years earlier now had more rugby than it could fit into a calendar.

### The titles

The results came in a rush, and then they stopped being a rush and became the normal state of affairs.

**1954**: a share of the Championship with England and Wales, the first title France had ever held. **1955**: a share again, this time with Wales, after France won **16–9 at Twickenham** in February — the match that got Prat his English nickname. **1959**: won outright for the first time, with a record that reads oddly for a champion side, two wins, a draw at Twickenham and a defeat in Dublin. **1960**: shared with England. **1961** and **1962**: outright, and nobody else close.

Five titles in nine seasons, and champions in each of the four years from 1959. Between the first of them and the last came South Africa.

### Ten matches and no tries

The tour that ended at Ellis Park nearly happened without the man who led it.

France went to southern Africa in July 1958 under **Michel Celaya**, a Biarritz forward with fifty caps who had been given the captaincy for the biggest journey the team had ever made. He hurt his knee in the opening match, against **Rhodesia**, and never played a Test on the tour at all. The armband went to the lock **Lucien Mias**.

Mias was not an obvious replacement. He had walked away from the game to study medicine in Toulouse, and had come back to it lighter by twenty kilos and considerably better than he had been before. His team-mates called him **Docteur Pack**. He thought about forward play in a way almost nobody in France did then, and the two things he is credited with introducing — the *touche en mouvement*, the moving lineout, and the *demi-tour contact* — are both about a pack behaving like a unit rather than eight men arriving separately.

France played **ten matches** in South Africa, and the two that counted were settled by the forwards and the kickers. Here is the part that says what kind of team this still was. **Across both Tests France did not score a single try.** South Africa scored two, and lost the series anyway. The nine points at Ellis Park came off the boot, behind a pack that would not go backwards.

The hooker **Robert Vigier** described what Mias did that afternoon in a sentence that has outlived the match: *« Il fut si grand cette fois-là que je m'arrêtais de jouer pour l'admirer. »* He was so great that time that I stopped playing to watch him.

Three months earlier, Mias had captained **SC Mazamet** in the final of the French championship. The team that beat them was Prat's **Lourdes**, taking its sixth title. The two men who between them define this generation of French rugby met in a club final in the spring and went to Johannesburg together in the summer.

Set the sequence beside what was happening to the other side. From 1948 the apartheid state was building this sport into an instrument of race, and the Springboks into the proof of a people's superiority; they had not lost a series at home since **1896**. In August 1958, in Johannesburg, in front of a hundred thousand of them, that machinery lost one — to a team of tanners' sons and Pyrenean shopkeepers from a country that had spent the previous decade being told it did not know how to play the game properly.

### 1961

The best of it, and the strangest, came in a single year.

On **18 February 1961**, at Colombes, France played South Africa again and the match finished **0–0**. Not a point, by anybody, in eighty minutes: two packs of forwards cancelling each other out at the Stade Yves-du-Manoir, with **François Moncla** of Pau as captain. French rugby has remembered it ever since as *le match du siècle* — the match of the century — which tells you something about what the country valued in a game at that moment, because nothing whatever happened in it.

That summer France went to **New Zealand** for the first time, and found out where they actually stood. They lost the first Test **13–6**, the second **5–3**, and the third **32–3**. Then they crossed to Australia and won **15–8**, and in November they drew **5–5** with **Romania** in Paris — a scoreline that would have been unthinkable a decade earlier, and that the Romanians would spend the next thirty years improving on.

Champions of Europe, unbeatable at home, and comprehensively taken apart in Auckland. Both of those were true in the same twelve months.

### The Bonifaces

The style everyone remembers arrived with the backs, and above all with two brothers from **Stade Montois** in the Landes.

**André Boniface** made his debut in 1954 and won 48 caps; his younger brother **Guy** arrived in 1960 and won 35. They played together in the French midfield, and they are the point at which French rugby stopped being admired for its forwards and started being loved for what happened behind them.

Beside them was a fly-half from the poorest quarter of **Dax**. **Pierre Albaladejo** kicked with both feet, won thirty caps and four Championships, and in the **1960** match against Ireland dropped **three goals** — a record for an international that stood for twenty-five years. The British press christened him **Monsieur Drop**, and, as with Prat, the French took the English name and kept it. He and Guy Boniface were both on the field for the goalless afternoon against the Springboks, which is its own comment: the two most watchable backs in Europe, in the match their country chose to remember above all others, and neither of them touched the scoreboard.

Guy Boniface did not see the golden age his generation made possible. On **1 January 1968**, aged thirty, he died of injuries from a car crash while driving home from a match.

### What this generation leaves behind

A team that has beaten everybody, and a country that has stopped apologising for how it plays.

By 1962 France had won the Championship five times in nine seasons and in each of the last four; had beaten New Zealand; had won a European trophy and a series in Argentina; and had done what no touring side had managed in sixty-two years by taking a series in South Africa — without scoring a try in it. The pariah of 1931 was the strongest team in European rugby.

What had not changed was any of the things it had been expelled for. The clubs still recruited by finding men work. The championship was still played hard enough to frighten visitors. The federation still called it all amateur.

The next twenty-five years would take both halves of that — the beauty and the violence — to their limit, in a town on the Mediterranean that won everything and terrified everyone.

## Generation 5: Flair and Castagne (1963–1987)

**13 June 1987. Concord Oval, Sydney.** A minute or so left, France and Australia level in the first World Cup semi-final ever played, and the ball goes through **eleven pairs of French hands**.

Rodriguez, Charvet, Berbizier, Lagisquet — each running at pace, each somehow finding a blue shirt as he offloads, a move that looks at every phase as though it must break down and does not. It reaches **Serge Blanco** just inside the Australian 22, with one line to run and what seems like half of Australia coming across to cover.

It is a foot race for the corner. Blanco wins it. **France 30, Australia 24.**

Seven days later, at Eden Park, New Zealand beat France **29–9** in the first World Cup final. But the semi-final is the match everyone remembers, and it is the perfect summation of what French rugby had become across the previous quarter-century: the most beautiful team in the world, and not quite the best.

---

This generation is remembered for two things that appear to contradict each other — **le beau jeu** and **la castagne**, the beautiful game and the brawl. Both are real. But the story underneath them is stranger and better than either, and it comes from a wine town on the Mediterranean.

### 1968

The generation's first triumph came twelve days after a funeral.

**Guy Boniface** died on the road on New Year's Day 1968. On **13 January**, at Murrayfield, France began the Five Nations without him and beat **Scotland 8–6**. They then beat **Ireland 16–6**, **England 14–9** at Colombes, and **Wales 14–9** in Cardiff. It was France's **first Grand Slam**, seventy-six years after the first French championship and fifty-eight after they had been let into the tournament.

The captain was **Christian Carrère**, one of only four men to play all four matches. The **Camberabero** brothers ran the half-back partnership and **Walter Spanghero** the pack. And the side that did it **had no head coach at all**: the team was chosen by a selection committee, and the players sorted out how to play on their own.

A country that had waited nearly six decades for a clean sweep got one from a team nobody was coaching. It is the first appearance of something that runs through the whole of this generation.

### Béziers

Between **1971 and 1984** the **Association Sportive Biterroise** won the French championship **ten times**, along with three Yves du Manoir challenges and other trophies. Across **1971–1978** the club was, in the academic study of it, *quasiment invincible* — very nearly unbeatable.

The obvious question is how. The answer everyone gives is that Béziers were frightening. The answer the scholarship gives is different — and it is not that Béziers invented anything. Lourdes had been drilling named moves in training since the early 1950s. What Béziers did was take that instinct and industrialise it.

David Wozniak, writing in *Études Héraultaises*, frames the puzzle exactly: how did a team from a **medium-sized town, plunged into the great wine crisis of the 1950s**, extract itself from an unfavourable economic and social context to become, for more than a decade, the world reference in its sport? *La réponse tient en deux mots : **la recherche et l'innovation***. The answer is in two words: research and innovation.

The setting rhymes with the beginning of the chapter. Béziers is in the Languedoc, and its crisis was **viticultural** — the wine trade that had carried the game into Bordeaux some sixty years earlier, now collapsing in the south and taking a town's economy with it. Rugby did not arrive in these places despite the wine. It arrived with it, and rose again out of its failure.

What the club actually did was build a laboratory.

The long midweek session ran two to three hours, physical preparation and technique together, with **Raoul Barrière** analysing every movement — passes, catches, contacts, tackles, kicking — from the touchline and restarting the exercise from the beginning when it was not right. A physiotherapist was brought in specifically to improve the players' conditioning. In December 1983 the club took its squad to **Rizoul, in the Alps, for an altitude oxygenation camp**; the same year it adopted a new strength method.

And at the end of the Friday session, the players **voted by secret ballot on the team for the weekend.**

The captain **Richard Astre** put the club's self-conception in a series of questions:

> *Qui introduit la concertation entre l'entraîneur et les joueurs ? l'AS Béziers. Qui systématise les recherches pour rationaliser l'entraînement ? l'AS Béziers. Qui fait entrer tous les joueurs qui le désirent dans tous les rouages du club ? Béziers encore.*

Who introduced consultation between coach and players? AS Béziers. Who systematised research to rationalise training? AS Béziers. Who brings every player who wants it into every working part of the club? Béziers again. And all of it, Astre said, began by questioning a notion accepted by almost all of French rugby — **that a club depends on one personality**, usually a patron.

The **BBC** sent people to investigate what they called *the Béziers Phénomène*. A former England scrum-half, by then a commentator, advised the English to copy it.

Béziers once beat the French national team **50–14**.

### What Béziers gave France

The national side then took the method wholesale.

In **1977** France won the **Grand Slam** using the **same fifteen players in every match and without conceding a single try** — playing, in Wozniak's account, Béziers' way, under Astre's influence, from the South African tour of 1975 onwards.

That is not a flair story. That is a story about a club that treated rugby as a research problem, handed its method to a national team, and produced the most controlled Five Nations campaign anyone had managed.

It also rhymes with 1968, and the uncoached side that won the first Grand Slam by working it out among themselves.

Secret ballots for selection; a Grand Slam without a coach; every player admitted into every working part of the club. Whatever else French rugby was in these years, it had a persistent instinct for **running itself from below** — a strange thing in a sport administered from above almost everywhere else it had travelled.

### The corporal

The man who took the method into the national side was five foot four.

**Jacques Fouroux** was a scrum-half from the Gers who won **twenty-seven caps between 1972 and 1977 and captained France in twenty-two of them**. He was called **le Petit Caporal** — for the way he drove men, for a famously difficult temperament, and for standing **1.63 metres**. He captained the **1977 Grand Slam** side: the one that used the same fifteen players in all four matches and conceded no tries.

In **1981** he came back as coach, and stayed until **1990**. Under him France won the Championship **six times** and the Grand Slam in **1981** and **1987**, reached the first World Cup final, and **between 1983 and 1989 never lost more than one match in a season.**

Set out as a list, the whole generation reads better than its reputation. France were champions of Europe **outright in 1967, 1968, 1977, 1981 and 1987**, and shared the title in **1970**, in the five-way tie of **1973**, in **1983** and in **1986** — eight titles in twenty-one seasons, four of them Grand Slams. The team remembered for being beautiful and losing was, in fact, the most successful side in Europe across the period, by some distance.

**Philippe Sella** of Agen is the other measure. In **1986** he scored a try in every match of the Championship, only the fourth man in its history to do it, and when he retired in 1995 he held **111 caps** and was the most-capped player the game had produced. Fouroux's line about him has outlived both of them: he had "**the strength of a bull but the touch of a piano player**".

### The castagne

And then the other half, which cannot be left out.

French club rugby in this period was violent in a way that the rest of the sport found genuinely shocking, and the most serious documented case belongs to the same Béziers side. In **1978** the second row **Michel Palmié** was **suspended for life** for striking **Armand Clerc** of Racing, leaving Clerc blind or almost blind in one eye. French accounts record that his international career was ended under pressure from the British unions. He later became a director of the FFR.

That is the ledger, and both columns are real. The club that invented player self-government and altitude training also produced the worst disciplinary case in French rugby. The country whose 1977 side conceded no tries in a Grand Slam was the country whose championship the Home Unions had once refused to be in the same competition as.

The framing that French writers use for the split is *rugby de tradition* against *rugby de terroir*: the British inheritance of discipline, fair play and the Corinthian spirit, against a southern game rooted in *impetuous emotion, instinct and liberté* that declined to accept either authority or rigid rules. It produced both columns of the ledger. It was never going to produce only one.

### The state, again

One more thread, because the state never quite let this sport alone.

After the **1960 Rome Olympics**, embarrassed by French athletes' performance and wanting to project *la France qui gagne* — the France that wins — **Charles de Gaulle** sought to associate the nation with its stylish and successful rugby side. Dine's judgement, in his reviewer's summary, is that French rugby, "long a vector of regional identity, particularly in the southwest, was **exploited in moments of perceived crisis by national governments** seeking to impose their particular vision of French values and greatness."

Nineteen years after Vichy had reached for rugby's *amateur and ruralist values*, the Fifth Republic reached for its glamour. The sport was serviceable to both.

### Casque d'Or

The player who carried the era's public face was a blond flanker from Toulouse.

**Jean-Pierre Rives** — *Casque d'Or*, the golden helmet — won **59 caps** and captained France **34 times**, then a record. He led the Grand Slams of **1977** and **1981**. And on **14 July 1979** — Bastille Day — he led France out at **Eden Park** for the second Test, a week after losing the first. In front of **fifty-seven thousand** New Zealanders France scored **four tries to two** and won **24–19**: the first French victory on New Zealand soil, on the French national day, at the ground where the All Blacks do not lose. It took another thirty-five years for anyone else to beat them there.

Behind him, **Serge Blanco** was assembling the record that would end with **93 caps**, 17 as captain, and **38 tries** from full-back — the position from which, in this era, France attacked. And **Philippe Sella**, of Agen, was beginning a career that Jacques Fouroux would summarise as having "**the strength of a bull but the touch of a piano player**".

### What this generation leaves behind

The most admired team in the world, on the eve of the tournament that would decide what admiration was worth.

**1987 was the best year French rugby had ever had, and it ended in a defeat.** France won the **Grand Slam** in the spring — the fourth of the generation — and flew south in May for the first World Cup. In a pool with Scotland, Romania and Zimbabwe they drew **20–20** with the Scots, the only drawn match of the whole tournament, and took the group on points difference. They beat **Fiji 31–16** in the quarter-final, and then came Sydney.

By the end of it France had Grand Slams in 1968, 1977, 1981 and 1987, a win in New Zealand, the most admired attacking rugby in the world, and a domestic championship still capable of ending a man's sight. It had built, at Béziers, the most sophisticated training system in the sport, and given the method away to its national team.

And in the first World Cup it had lost the final.

The tournament that beat them was about to change everything about the sport — including, within eight years, the amateur pretence that France had been quietly ignoring since the 1920s.

## Generation 6: Open (1988–2007)

**31 October 1999. Twickenham.** France are losing a World Cup semi-final to New Zealand by **24–10**, and **Jonah Lomu** has scored both of the All Black tries. There is no reason to expect what follows.

In the World Rugby Museum's account, "France, who would never have experienced such overwhelming support inside the stadium before, were inspired. **Three tries in quick succession floored New Zealand.**" The fly-half **Christophe Lamaison**, who had scored in the first half, added **two drop goals and two penalties**; inside about twenty-four minutes **Christophe Dominici**, **Richard Dourthe** and **Philippe Bernat-Salles** crossed the New Zealand line, the last of them finishing work by the flanker **Olivier Magne**. France score **thirty-three points to seven** after the interval and win **43–31**. The museum's verdict is unambiguous: **the most spectacular comeback in World Cup history**.

They lost the final to Australia. That, too, is the pattern.

But the afternoon at Twickenham is not what this generation is about. Between 1988 and 2007 the question in French rugby was not whether the team could play. It was who owned the sport, and who was going to pay for it.

### The last years of the old game

The generation opens with France as the best side in Europe and the least trusted club system in it.

**19 October 1991. Parc des Princes.** France play England in a World Cup quarter-final at home, and England arrive with a plan that has nothing to do with rugby: they go at **Serge Blanco** from the opening minutes, and they admit it afterwards. The French press called it *le traquenard*, the trap. England led 10–6 at the interval, helped by a permissive New Zealand referee; France drew level at 10–10 through the forwards and a back line marshalled by a twenty-two-year-old **Fabien Galthié**; **Jon Webb** scored the try that settled it in added time. **France 10, England 19.**

Blanco never played for France again. The most gifted attacking fullback the country had produced finished his international career being kicked off the field of his own stadium, in a tournament his federation's president had helped invent.

That federation was, at that moment, losing its grip in a more ordinary way. The championship had stopped belonging to anybody: **Agen took it in 1988, Toulouse in 1989, Racing Paris in 1990, Bègles-Bordeaux in 1991, Toulon in 1992, Castres in 1993**, before Toulouse won three in a row from **1994**. Racing's 1990 title matters for what comes later — the capital had a champion two years before a radio millionaire bought the other Parisian club, and Racing's side won it while treating the whole business as a joke, in berets and bow ties.

### Durban, 1995

France's last tournament as an amateur country was decided by weather.

**17 June 1995. King's Park, Durban.** The World Cup semi-final against South Africa is delayed an hour while water is swept off the pitch, and there is serious talk of not playing it at all. If it had been abandoned, the hosts would have gone out: South Africa's disciplinary record was worse than France's, and the tournament rules would have sent the French through to the final against New Zealand. The match was played in standing water. **South Africa won 19–15.**

Ten weeks later the International Rugby Board declared the game open.

### The end of the old man

**Albert Ferrasse** had been president of the **FFR for twenty-three years, from 1968 to 1991**. A former second row for **Agen**, he ran **SU Agen** from 1965 to 1985 before ascending to the federation, and he was president of the **International Rugby Board from 1979 to 1987**. He is credited with originating the idea of a World Cup in the 1970s, and he spent the last years of his presidency resisting the thing that idea made inevitable. His obituaries record it plainly: he was known for resisting his sport's evolution towards professionalism, a stance that made him enemies.

In **1991** he was succeeded by **Bernard Lapasset**, a customs official from Tarbes who had been his assistant secretary general and then his secretary general — "héritier d'Albert Ferrasse", as Radio France put it on his death in 2023. Lapasset would hold the presidency until **2008**, and then run world rugby until 2016. He is the man French rugby credits with professionalising itself, and the credit comes with a description of the cost: a turn taken in 1995 that he carried through *"sous les quolibets et les insultes"* — under the jeers and the insults.

The jeering is the point. France did not arrive at professionalism with relief. It arrived having spent seventy years paying players while denying it, and the denial had become a kind of constitution.

### What the open game could not afford

The International Rugby Board declared rugby open in **1995**, and the fiction ended in a sentence. What replaced it took three years to build. In **1998** the **Ligue Nationale de Rugby** was created to run the professional game by delegation from the ministry of sport and the federation: it would organise the competitions, negotiate the television contracts, and regulate the clubs.

Its first president was **Serge Blanco** — the fullback from Biarritz whose running had been the argument for French exceptionalism a decade earlier, in office from **24 July 1998 until October 2008**. And in 1999, a year into the job, the emblem of French flair said the quiet thing out loud: *"le problème du rugby, c'est qu'il n'a pas aujourd'hui les moyens d'être professionnel comme on l'entend au football."* Rugby's problem is that it does not have the means to be professional the way football is.

He was right, and the numbers say so. **Canal+ had bought the championship in 1998**, the first time it belonged to a subscription channel, and the figure barely moved for five seasons: **11.89 million euros a year from 1998–99 to 2002–03**, a total the news agency AFP put at **59.45 million** for the period. The money doubled only in **June 2003**, when the league signed a four-year deal at the Stade de France on the day of the championship final, taking the rights to **18 million in 2003–04** and **21 million by 2006–07**.

Set that against what the clubs were spending, and the professional era looks less like a windfall than an exposure. Television was **15 to 20% of a club's budget** in 2002, around **18% on average**. Four-fifths of French professional rugby was being paid for by somebody else — by gates, by sponsors, by town halls, and above all by men who wanted to own a rugby club.

### Paris buys a team

The most consequential of those men had never played the game for a living. **Max Guazzini**, a co-founder of the radio station **NRJ** and its programme director, bought **Stade Français in 1992**. What he bought was a name and a ruin: *"Lorsque je suis arrivé, ce Club, qui avait été huit fois Champion de France, était classé dans ce que l'on pourrait qualifier de cinquième division."* When he arrived, a club that had been eight times champion of France was in what you might call the fifth division.

He put money in, merged the rugby section with **CASG Paris**, and moved into **Jean Bouin**. Then he did the thing that made the difference, which was to treat a rugby match as a show and the crowd as the product. He gave tickets away: *"Moi, je préfère avoir 7 000 personnes heureuses dans notre stade que 200 qui rapportent une poignée de francs. C'est une question de philosophie."* I would rather have seven thousand happy people in our stadium than two hundred who bring in a handful of francs.

The philosophy won. Stade Français were **champions of France in 1998, 2000, 2003, 2004 and 2007**. In **2001** the players posed naked for the first **Dieux du Stade** calendar. In **2005** they ran out in **pink**, away at Perpignan, to general astonishment. A Parisian club with a radio man's instinct for publicity had done in thirteen years what the capital had not managed since the 1900s, and it had done it in a game whose heartland had spent eighty years insisting that Paris did not understand rugby.

The south-west answered on the field, and in Europe. **Toulouse won the first European Cup final in 1996**. **Brive took the second, beating Leicester 28–9 in January 1997.** Toulouse won again in **2003**, in the first all-French final, against Perpignan, and in **2005**, against Stade Français. The clubs that had been the game's aristocracy since the 1920s were still the game's aristocracy. They were simply no longer the only place the money was.

### Marcoussis

The federation's answer was concrete. In **1997**, two years after the game went open, the FFR decided to build a national centre, because — in the words of the academic history of the project — since 1995 and the arrival of professionalism the sport had been changing fast, and France had to modernise "afin de rivaliser avec les autres grandes nations rugbystiques, pour la plupart anglo-saxonnes."

The call for sites went out in December 1998, asking for at least **seventeen hectares**. Marcoussis, in the Essonne, sent its file to Lapasset in **January 1999**. Work began in **March 2001**; the **Centre National de Rugby opened on 10 September 2002**, and on **18 November 2002** the President of the Republic, **Jacques Chirac**, inaugurated it. It had taken seventeen months and **49.4 million euros**, **two-thirds of it the federation's own money**.

French rugby had built itself a headquarters worth more than four years of its television income. It did so while its own base was shrinking: in **1998 rugby was only the sixth sport in France by membership**, behind tennis, judo, equestrianism and basketball, and the federation was asking itself why — *"Nos écoles de rugby ne sont plus aussi pleines qu'avant. Pourquoi les jeunes ne vont-ils plus au rugby ?"* Our rugby schools are not as full as they used to be. Why do the young no longer come?

### The best team France had ever had

While the argument about money ran, the team won more than it ever had. **Pierre Berbizier**, appointed straight from his playing career, coached from **1991 to 1995** and took the championship in **1993**. **Jean-Claude Skrela** followed, won 34 of his 52 Tests, and took **back-to-back Five Nations Grand Slams in 1997 and 1998**; he resigned after the 1999 final.

In **February 1998**, in the opening round of the Five Nations, France played their first match at the **Stade de France**, the stadium built for football's World Cup, and beat **England 24–17**, with Bernat-Salles scoring the first rugby try on the ground. The national side had been at the **Parc des Princes since 1973**. It now played in Saint-Denis, in a bowl of eighty thousand, on the same grass as the football team.

**Bernard Laporte** took the side from late 1999 to 2007 — the most successful stretch the country has had in the professional era. **Four championships: 2002, 2004, 2006 and 2007**, with **Grand Slams in 2002 and 2004**, and semi-finals at the World Cups of 2003 and 2007. He was named the international coach of the year in 2002.

### 2007

Then France hosted a World Cup, and it went wrong in the most French way available.

**Opening night, Stade de France. Argentina beat the hosts 17–12**, a defensive performance that wrecked the party in the tournament's first match — and Argentina, at that point, had no professional league at home at all, which made the result an argument about money as much as about rugby.

France recovered. In the quarter-final, in **Cardiff**, they beat **New Zealand 20–18**, an upset ranked among the greatest the tournament has produced. In the semi-final, **England won 14–9**, steered by **Jonny Wilkinson**. Argentina beat France again in the third-place match.

A home World Cup that opened and closed with defeats by Argentina, and held in the middle one of the great victories of the modern game.

### What this generation leaves behind

A sport that had stopped lying about money, and had not yet worked out how to live on it.

By 2007 France had four Grand Slams in eleven years, the most spectacular comeback in World Cup history, a win over New Zealand in a knockout tie on home soil, and no World Cup. It had a national centre inaugurated by the President, a league run by its most famous player, a Parisian champion built on free tickets and a naked calendar, and a television contract that covered less than a fifth of what its clubs spent.

The gap between those two facts is the whole of the next twenty years. Somebody was going to close it, and the men who did were not going to be the federation.

## Generation 7: The Richest League in the World (2008–2026)

**14 March 2026. Stade de France.** The last kick of the Six Nations, and **Thomas Ramos** has a penalty to win the championship.

England have scored forty-six points in Paris and are still in it. France have scored forty-eight, four of the tries from the wing **Louis Bielle-Biarrey** and one from **Théo Attissogbe**, and Ramos has had a perfect night off the tee. The title has been in the balance all afternoon and is still in the balance now.

He kicks it. **France 48, England 46**, and a **second successive championship**.

The final table reads France 21, Ireland 19, Scotland 16, Italy 9, England 8 — the worst campaign England have ever had — and Wales with a third consecutive Wooden Spoon.

The captain is **Antoine Dupont**. The coach is **Fabien Galthié**. And the league those players go back to on Monday is the wealthiest in the history of the sport.

---

### The money

The scale of it is worth setting out plainly, because no other chapter in this book has anything to compare.

**Canal+**, which has broadcast French top-flight rugby since **1998**, agreed a deal worth **€696.8 million** for the **Top 14 and ProD2 across 2027–2032** — roughly **€139 million a season**, and a **14.7 per cent increase** on the arrangement it replaced. It is the most valuable domestic broadcast contract in world rugby, in what is by common consent the most commercially successful domestic competition the sport has.

The wages follow. Across the Top 14's **520 players** the average salary is about **€259,000 a season** — **five times** the ProD2 average, and **a third higher** than the Premiership or Japan Rugby League One. The best-paid earn in the region of **€600,000**: **Dupont**, **Owen Farrell**, **Grégory Alldritt**, **Will Skelton**, **Mathieu Jalibert**.

And then the distribution, which is the part that belongs in this book rather than in a business page.

**Four per cent** of Top 14 players earn more than €480,000 a season. **Twenty-seven per cent earn less than €60,000.**

More than a quarter of the professionals in the richest rugby league on earth are on wages that would not keep a family comfortably in Paris. The competition that pays a handful of men €600,000 also employs, in the same changing rooms, a large minority earning a fraction of it — playing in Agen and Castres and Perpignan and Brive, in the same market towns where the jobs at the tannery were being arranged a century ago.

The mechanism changed completely. The shape did not.

### The patron returns

What the money bought, first, was a team of other people's players.

**Mourad Boudjellal** had made his money in comics. He founded **Soleil Productions**, whose *Lanfeust* series sold fourteen million copies, and in **May 2006** he took over **RC Toulon**, a club that had just finished one of the worst top-flight seasons on record and was on its way down a division. He set about buying it a reputation. The first marquee signing was **Tana Umaga**, who had captained the All Blacks; the one that changed everything was **Jonny Wilkinson**, whose drop goal had beaten Australia in the 2003 World Cup final. Then he kept signing — South Africans, New Zealanders, Samoans, Englishmen, a Georgian captain — until a British commentator called them **the Galacticos** and the name stuck. In 2011 he sold the publishing house to give the club his full attention.

It worked, spectacularly and briefly. Toulon won the **European Cup in 2013, 2014 and 2015** — three in a row, which nobody had done before or has done since — and the **Top 14 in 2014**, in Wilkinson's last season. Then it stopped working. The later signings did not come off, the club went six years without a trophy, and by 2021 it was finishing eighth.

The shape of that should be familiar by now. A man who made his money outside the game buys a club, imports a squad of stars from wherever they can be found, wins everything for a few seasons, and leaves behind something that cannot sustain itself once he stops paying. **Jean Bourrel** did it at Quillan with hat money in 1929. Boudjellal did it at Toulon with comic books eighty years later. The difference is that in 1929 it was against the rules and denied, and in 2013 it was the business model.

### The decade the team lost

Here is the part that makes the money interesting rather than merely large: while the league got rich, the national team fell apart.

**Philippe Saint-André** took charge in **December 2011**. France finished fourth in his first Championship, and in **2013 they finished last** — the Wooden Spoon, and eight defeats across the year. In four attempts he never finished in the top three. His tenure ended at the **2015 World Cup**, in a quarter-final against New Zealand that France lost **62–13**, the heaviest defeat in the country's history at the tournament.

**Guy Novès** came next, in January 2016, from Toulouse, with the best club record in France. He finished fifth, then third, was whitewashed in South Africa, drew with **Japan**, and in **December 2017 became the first coach France had ever sacked**. No France coach of the modern era has a lower win rate.

Under **Jacques Brunel**, after a defeat by Ireland in the 2018 Championship, France fell to **tenth in the world rankings** — their lowest position since the rankings were created in 2003.

Eight years, three coaches, one Wooden Spoon, no titles. In the same eight years the Top 14 became the best-paid competition in the history of the sport, and a French club won Europe four times. The country's clubs had never been stronger and its team had never been worse, and the two facts were connected by a simple mechanism: the money bought the best players in the world, and the best players in the world were not French.

### The rule that says who is French

So the league legislated.

In **2010** the Ligue Nationale de Rugby introduced a **salary cap** and a quota of **JIFF** players — *joueurs issus des filières de formation*, players who came through the training pathways. The definition is the interesting part, because it is not about nationality at all. A player is JIFF if he held a French licence for **five seasons before the age of 23**, or spent **three seasons in an approved club academy between 16 and 21**. A South African raised in Perpignan qualifies. A Frenchman who learned the game abroad does not.

From the **2017–18 season** the requirement was given teeth: clubs must average at least **sixteen JIFF players per match sheet** across a season, and a club that misses it **loses six points** from the following year's standings.

A competition that had spent two decades buying finished players from other countries was now required, on pain of a points deduction, to manufacture its own. The federation did not ask the clubs to be patriotic. It made being patriotic cheaper than the alternative — which is, more or less, what the FFR has done since the 1920s, except that this time it wrote it down.

The generation that came through those academies is the one that won in 2022 and again in 2025 and 2026.

### What the money does to everybody else

This is where France stops being only its own story.

The Top 14's wealth is the single largest force acting on the rugby of countries that cannot compete with it, and this book has already watched it work.

**Claude Saurel** concluded in the 1990s that Georgian rugby could not be built in Georgia, and arranged for French clubs to sign Georgian players instead — an entire national development strategy outsourced to a foreign league. It worked: Georgia acquired a Test pack that nobody in Europe wants to scrummage against. It also means Georgia has never been able to keep its best players at home, and the point is made precisely by **Mamuka Gorgodze**, the Georgian captain, who won the **2015 European Cup with Toulon**. Romania, which used to beat France in Bucharest, lost its domestic game to the same pull. The Pacific islands supply the Top 14 and the Japanese league with players their own unions can neither pay nor keep.

France did not design this. It built a rich league in a country that likes rugby, and the gravity did the rest. But the gravity is the story of modern Tier 2 rugby, and its source is the competition described above.

### The World Cups

Twice in this generation France came close enough to touch it, and both times the margin was one point.

**2011** was the strangest run any side has made to a World Cup final. France lost twice in their pool — to New Zealand, and then, on **1 October**, to **Tonga**, **19–14**, a result that remains among the worst in French rugby history. They went through second anyway, beat England, and beat Wales by a single point in the semi-final. In the final at **Eden Park** they lost to New Zealand **8–7**: one try each, the whole thing settled by kicks, and the narrowest margin any World Cup final has been decided by. A team that had been beaten by Tonga three weeks earlier came within a point of the world title.

**2023** they hosted, and opened on **8 September** by beating **New Zealand 27–13** at the Stade de France, coming from behind after the All Blacks had scored the fastest try in the tournament's history inside ninety-one seconds. In the quarter-final, on **15 October at the Stade de France**, they met **South Africa** and lost **29–28** — seven tries, one point, at home, to the side that went on to win it.

Two World Cup defeats, twelve years apart, by a total of two points. In between, in **July 2024**, the same country won an Olympic gold medal on the same ground: France beat **Fiji 28–7** in the sevens final in front of nearly seventy thousand people, ending a Fijian run of seventeen straight Olympic wins, with **Dupont** coming on at half-time to score twice. The first French gold of those Games was won at rugby.

### Laporte

And then the federation, again.

On **13 December 2022** the FFR president **Bernard Laporte** was found guilty on four of five counts, alongside **Mohed Altrad**, the president of Montpellier. Laporte received a **two-year suspended prison sentence and a €75,000 fine**, and was banned from any role in French rugby; Altrad got eighteen months suspended and €50,000. The court found that Laporte had taken **€180,000** under an image-rights contract signed in March 2017 — a contract that was never actually performed — and had in return ensured decisions favourable to Altrad's club, among them the reduction of a federal disciplinary fine from €70,000 to €20,000.

He kept the presidency at first, pending appeal, and **resigned in January 2023**. The **appeal opened in Paris on 9 September 2026** and was still being heard as this account closes.

Set that beside the last two generations. Béziers, in the 1970s, selected its team by secret ballot and admitted every player who wanted it into every working part of the club. Ferrasse then governed the federation alone for twenty-three years. And the presidency ended, in this generation, in a criminal conviction over payments from a club owner.

French rugby has spent a hundred and thirty years oscillating between the most democratic instincts in the sport and the most concentrated power in it. It has never settled the question, and 2022 is not an aberration from the pattern but the far end of it.

### Where things stood, mid-2026

France go to **Rugby World Cup 2027** in Australia as **back-to-back Six Nations champions**, captained by a scrum-half widely reckoned the best player in the world, coached by a man who has been in the job since 2019, and supplied by a domestic league richer than any that has ever existed.

They have won two World Cup knockout matches against New Zealand, an Olympic gold medal, and no World Cup. They have a federation whose former president is in a courtroom in Paris. And they have, still, the thing the Home Unions expelled them for in 1931 and the thing that has never left: a game that belongs to the small towns below the Loire, played harder than anyone else plays it, and paid for.

### What this generation leaves behind

In **1892**, on a field in the Bois de Boulogne, Baron Pierre de Coubertin refereed a match between two clubs of the Parisian professional class, played by young men for whom the entire point was that nobody was paid.

A hundred and thirty-four years later, the country he was refereeing in runs a competition that pays five hundred and twenty men an average of a quarter of a million euros a season, and it does so in Agen, Castres, Perpignan, Toulouse, Pau, Bayonne and Brive.

Coubertin's amateur ideal did not survive in France. What survived was the wine towns, the market towns, the villages below the Loire that took the game off him — and the thing they built out of it, which by any measure the founders would have recognised as everything they were trying to prevent, and which is now the richest rugby in the world.
