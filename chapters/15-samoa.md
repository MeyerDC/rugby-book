# Samoa

## Generation 0: Lotu (1830–1919)

**7 November 1918. Apia harbour.**

The steamer *Talune* comes in from Auckland by way of Fiji. Influenza has been running through her decks for days. As she docks, a passenger calls down from the rail — the words are remembered in Samoa — "**There is a sickness on this boat.**"

Ports across the Pacific have already closed. The New Zealand administrators in Apia wave her in after a cursory health inspection, and the passengers walk ashore.

They fan out. Some go into Apia town. Some ride goods wagons up into the interior. A missionary walks the coast road from village to village with a cough.

Within days people are dying. Within weeks whole villages are dead. Within two months **about 8,500 Samoans have died — between a fifth and a quarter of the entire population.** At Sale'aumua a hundred died. At Lalomanu, eighty-eight. Funeral custom collapsed; carts went round daily to collect bodies from the roadside; some houses in which whole families had died were simply set alight. Albert Wendt's father, eight years old and one of the few in his village unaffected, was put to work carrying the dead out to the road.

Set that against New Zealand's own losses. The historian Toeolesulusulu Damon Salesa: combine New Zealand's war dead and its influenza dead and "probably 3 or 4 percent of the New Zealand population died, and that was horrific." In Samoa, "**between 22 and 25 [per cent] of Samoa's population died in just a matter of weeks.**"

Now the part that makes this a chapter about institutions rather than a chapter about a virus.

**Forty miles to the east, in the other half of the same archipelago, speaking the same language, worshipping in the same church, nobody died of the flu at all.**

The eastern islands — Tutuila and Manu'a — had been American since 1900. In the American accounts of it, the naval commandant at Pago Pago, **John Martin Poyer**, heard what was happening in the west, imposed a total quarantine with the backing of the local chiefs, and held it. American Sāmoa recorded no influenza deaths, and Poyer was given the Navy Cross.

And New Zealand's museum records what happened when the two halves spoke to each other. Telegraph requests from Apia to Wellington asking for help "were rejected, but **the Administration also refused American aid when assistance from Eastern Sāmoa was offered.**"

One people. One archipelago. Two administrations, nineteen years after a line was drawn through the middle of it by three imperial powers in a room in Washington.

That line is this chapter. **Everything that follows — including which football code each half of Samoa plays — is downstream of it.** Rugby has not arrived yet; it is two years away. The institutions that will carry it are already in place, and so is the border that decides which sport they will carry.

Go back, then, eighty-eight years, to a ship anchoring off Savai'i.

---

### The village, the church and the man it keeps

**24 August 1830.** John Williams of the **London Missionary Society** sighted the coast of Savai'i and landed at **Sapapali'i**, looking for **Malietoa Vainu'upo**.

He had built his own ship. He had brought with him another European missionary, Charles Barff, and **eight teachers from Tahiti and Rarotonga**, and he had picked up in Tonga a Samoan named **Fauea**, who told him where to find the Malietoa. As they came along the coast, canoes off Falealupo brought news that the chief **Tamafaiga** had been assassinated at Fasito'outa. Fauea told Williams that the main obstacle to preaching the Gospel in Samoa had just been removed.

Conversion was very fast, and it was not individual. It was a village decision, ratified by a village act. Samoans had built no temples and carved no idols, so, as the Samoan historians describe it, a village that decided to *lotu* gathered in front of the missionary and **ate their aitu** — cooked and ate the creatures that had been sacred to their ancestors, their village or their district, desecrating the spirit in each so that it could never be worshipped again.

And then came the arrangement that this whole chapter turns on. Here it is in the words of the standard Samoan history, written by Samoans:

> "**When a village decided to become Christian they built a church and a house for a teacher or pastor, and began to contribute to the church by supporting the pastor with food and services, and by contributing coconut oil** to the senior representative of the mission in each district."

Read it again slowly, because it is the mechanism.

**A Samoan village collectively feeds, houses and maintains a man it has chosen, so that he can do on the village's behalf a thing the village could not do for itself — and the village takes the credit for what he does.** The oil went into barrels, and the barrels went to England to be sold, and the money funded the mission.

That is the *faifeau*, the pastor. It is the oldest export arrangement in Samoa, and it is the direct ancestor of everything in the second half of this chapter. In the professional era the village will hold a fundraiser to send a young man to a trial in Auckland or Toulon, and it will be exactly the same institution doing exactly the same thing with exactly the same expectations attached — including the remittance.

The section this chapter sits in is called *the church and the export*. In Samoa those are not two things.

### The Marists, and a handshake between Englishmen

Two more arrivals belong here, and both of them decide something.

The first is a Wesleyan near-miss. Samoans had already met Methodism through Tonga — the chief **Saiva'aia** of Tafua-Salelologa brought news of the *Lotu Toga* home in **1828** — and many on Savai'i with Tongan relatives expected a Tongan mission. It did not come, because **Williams had informally agreed with the Wesleyans in Tonga that Samoa would be left to the London Missionary Society, since the Wesleyans were establishing themselves in Fiji.**

Stop on that for a second. Two Englishmen in the Pacific divided a region between their churches over a conversation, and the reason Fiji's dominant denomination is Methodist and Samoa's is Congregational is, in part, that handshake. This book has watched a committee at Le Havre decide what code France's oldest club would play, and a headmaster at Nasinu decide who was intelligent. Here, two missionaries decided which church a hundred thousand people would be introduced to first.

The second arrival is the one that matters for the football. On **25 May 1845**, two French **Marist** priests — **Father Foudaire** and **Father Violette**, known in Samoan as Lutovi'o and Silipele — anchored at Falealupo off *L'Étoile de la Mer*, and the Catholic Church came to Samoa.

**Seventy-five years later it is the Marist Brothers who bring the ball.**

This book keeps finding teaching orders at the moment a country picks a code. In Uruguay the Christian Brothers chose rugby at Stella Maris while the Jesuits chose football, and the difference produced Old Christians and the shamrock. In Australia, Ted Larkin went and got the Marist Brothers' schools for rugby league in 1913 and league never had to ask again. Seven years after that, the same order handed Samoa rugby union — and the order that arrived in 1845 to save souls is the one that arrived in 1920 with a ball, and Samoa has played that code ever since.

### The line through the middle

On **2 December 1899**, in the Tripartite Convention, Germany, the United States and Britain partitioned the Samoan archipelago. It took effect on 16 February 1900.

Germany took **Upolu and Savai'i**, the two large western islands. The United States took **Tutuila and Manu'a** in the east, for the harbour at **Pago Pago**, the finest natural anchorage in that part of the Pacific and the reason the United States wanted the islands at all. Britain withdrew its claim in exchange for compensation elsewhere.

No Samoan signed the Convention. The chiefs of Tutuila and Aunu'u signed a Treaty of Cession four months later, in April 1900, after the fact.

In 1914 New Zealand troops took German Samoa, and New Zealand administered the western islands from then on — under a League of Nations mandate, then a United Nations trusteeship, for forty-eight years.

So by 1919 the archipelago has been running two experiments on one population for two decades, and the first results are in, and they are the 8,500 dead in the west and the nobody in the east.

### What this generation leaves behind

Samoa enters the 1920s with a church in every village, a system for collectively maintaining a chosen man, a Catholic teaching order in place, a border through the middle of the nation, a New Zealand administration that has just presided over one of the worst per-capita mortality events recorded anywhere in the twentieth century, and no football of any kind.

Both halves are about to be given a game. Neither half will choose which one.

## Generation 1: The Brothers and the Tree (1920–1938)

**18 August 1924. Apia Park, seven o'clock in the morning.**

Samoa is about to play the first international match in its history, against Fiji, and it is being played at dawn so that the Samoan players can get to work afterwards.

There is a tree on the halfway line.

Fiji win **6–0**. The visitors are praised for their speed and their handling. The Samoans get a different adjective, and it is the first adjective ever applied to Samoan rugby by an outsider: they are "**the big, sturdy men of Apia**."

A hundred and two years later people were still writing that sentence.

Now consider what had to happen for those men to be on that field at all, because the whole thing was assembled inside four years.

---

### Four years

Rugby was introduced to Samoa by the **Marist Brothers in 1920**, the date Samoan rugby's own centenary accounting gives.

In **1924** — four years later — three things happened at once. The **Apia Rugby Union** was founded, the first national governing body, and affiliated itself to the **New Zealand Rugby Football Union**. **Vaiala Ulalei**, the first rugby club in Samoa, was founded, in a village a short walk from Apia Park. And by August a national squad had been assembled and had played a Test match.

Four years from first ball to first cap. Compare Fiji, where rugby arrived in 1884 and the first Test was in 1924 — forty years. Compare Uruguay, where the game arrived in the 1860s and the first Test was in 1948.

Some of that is simply that Samoa was late enough to copy an existing template. But some of it is the village. The institutional work of forming a club — finding people, agreeing to meet, raising money, choosing who represents you — was work Samoan villages had been doing continuously since 1830, for the church. Handed a new activity, they did not have to invent an organisational form for it. They already had one.

The affiliation matters too, and it will matter for the next hundred years. **Samoa's rugby union was, from its first year, an affiliate of New Zealand's.** The game arrived from New Zealand, through the administering power, and the pathway it created ran in one direction.

### The Mau

The other thing that happened in Samoa in the 1920s was that the country tried to get itself back.

The **Mau** was a non-violent independence movement, revitalised in **1926** through the efforts of **Ta'isi Olaf Frederick Nelson**, a prominent businessman who travelled to Wellington that year to lobby for Samoan self-rule. He was **exiled to New Zealand in 1928** for it.

On **29 December 1929**, a peaceful Mau demonstration in Apia was fired on by New Zealand military police. **Eleven Samoans were killed and about fifty injured.** Among the dead was **Tupua Tamasese Lealofi III**, who had led the movement since Nelson's exile, and who was **shot from behind**.

It became known as **Black Saturday**. Two weeks later, on 13 January 1930, the New Zealand administration declared the Mau seditious and made it illegal to wear its uniform — a navy blue lavalava with a white stripe.

There is no way to write Samoan rugby's first decade without that, and there is no honest way to connect the two either. The men who founded Vaiala and played at dawn against Fiji lived in the country where this happened, under the administration that did it, eleven years after the same administration had let the *Talune* dock. It is the weather the game grew up in.

What can be said is what it did to the meaning of a national team. Samoa had a rugby side representing it before it had a government representing it, and would do for another thirty-three years. The blue jersey was, for a long stretch of the twentieth century, the only institution flying a Samoan flag that the administering power could not dissolve.

### What this generation leaves behind

By 1938 Samoa has a union, a club, a Test record, an affiliation to New Zealand, and a dead paramount chief.

What happens next is the strangest gap in this book: for most of the next two decades, there is barely any Samoan rugby to write about at all.

## Generation 2: The Long Absence (1939–1961)

**1942. Pago Pago, in the American half.**

The United States Marine Corps arrives in force. The harbour that the Tripartite Convention was fought over turns out to be worth exactly what the admirals thought it was worth, and for the duration of the Pacific war American Sāmoa is a base — men, money, construction, uniforms, and an entire generation of Samoan boys watching Americans do American things.

That is the moment the eastern islands' orientation hardens. The anthropologist **Lisa Uperesa**, who grew up in Tutuila and whose father coached football there, describes the sequence in her study of the American Samoan game: under the colonial and territorial governments, copra and **military service** were what drew local people into waged work, and then, "**starting in earnest during the post–World War II period, waves of emigration tied to military service helped establish Samoan communities in the United States.**"

Hold that beside the western islands in the same years, where the record of organised rugby goes very quiet.

---

### The gap

Something happened to Samoan rugby between the 1930s and the 1950s, and this book cannot tell you exactly what.

The account carried by the Australian Broadcasting Corporation's centenary coverage of Samoan rugby, in the caption to its own programme, is this: "**Rugby was banned in Samoa for several decades because of spectator antics when their team didn't win, but returned in the 1950s.**" The programme itself is headlined "**Once banned for being too violent.**"

That is the entire evidentiary basis. A caption and a headline on a radio segment. No date, no ordinance, no authority, no account of who imposed it or lifted it.

It is stated here because it is what Samoan rugby says about itself and because a decades-long absence does need explaining — but it is stated as a claim and not as a finding, and nothing in this chapter is built on it. If a reader of this book can find the ordinance, the book would like to know.

What is not in doubt is the shape of the period. Samoa played its first Test in 1924 and did not become a serious international presence for another two-thirds of a century. The country was under a foreign administration, then a trusteeship. Travel cost money nobody had. There was no competition to enter and no fixture list to be part of. And its union was an affiliate of the union of the country administering it.

### 1962

On **1 January 1962** — the day after this generation closes — Western Samoa became an independent state, **the first Pacific Island nation to do so.**

It is worth pausing on the order of events one final time before the rugby takes over. Samoa was Christian for a hundred and thirty-two years before it was independent. It had a national rugby team for thirty-eight years before it had a government. The church came first, then the border, then the ball, then the flag.

### What this generation leaves behind

Two halves of one people come out of the mid-century pointed in opposite directions. The eastern islands have an American economy, an American military pathway and, shortly, an American game. The western islands have independence, a village structure entirely intact, a rugby union nearly forty years old, and almost nothing to play.

The man who changes that is a coach in Apia who will not tolerate a missed training session.

## Generation 3: Mr Rugby (1962–1986)

**Apia, 1970. A radio.**

The Samoan squad for the following year's South Pacific Games is being read out on the wireless, name by name, and every family in the country with a son of the right age is listening.

**Pafelio Momoisea**, who captained Vaiala, was not listening. He was out with his friends.

> "The families heard the names read out and were all very proud and excited. **I was out somewhere with my mates and never heard my name read out but when I got home everyone was buzzing and celebrating.**"

That is how a Samoan found out he was an international. Not a letter, not a phone call, not an agent. He walked into his own house and the house already knew.

Six men from Vaiala were selected. The next year, in Tahiti, they won.

---

### La'auli Alan Grey

The coach was **La'auli Alan Grey**, and in Samoa he was simply called **Mr Rugby**.

Momoisea's description of him is the best short account of how a poor union builds a good team, and it has nothing to do with money:

> "There was a real care for every player on and off the field and we had the best coach in Samoa at the time. Grey was called the 'Mr Rugby' of Samoa. A no-nonsense coach and he had an astute rugby brain. **Alan was very strict on training, if you missed one session, you were out of the playing squad the next game. Everyone was treated the same** which built purpose, unity and competition within the team."

*Everyone was treated the same.* In a society organised by rank — by *matai* titles, by village standing, by orator's precedence — a coach imposed a rule in which none of that counted, and it was the rule itself that people remembered fifty years later.

Under Grey, Samoa did the things a small union does when someone competent is finally running it.

**1971:** at the **South Pacific Games in Tahiti**, Samoa beat the **Cook Islands** in the final and won **its first gold medal in rugby union.**

**1972:** Samoa played **two Tests against the Māori All Blacks in Apia** — seven of the side came from Vaiala.

Under Grey, Samoa went to its first **Hong Kong Sevens**, into the tournament that the Fiji chapter describes Fiji making its own. And Grey was, in the account of the people who were there, **instrumental in getting Samoa to the 1991 World Cup**. He was given the Western Samoa Order of Merit in 1993, and when he died in 2018 the country gave him a **state funeral**.

### The man who scored the first try

And now the story that this entire section of the book exists to tell.

In **1986**, a young openside flanker born in Auckland to Samoan parents played a Test match for **Western Samoa**.

One cap. That was it.

The following year — because Samoa was not going to the inaugural Rugby World Cup, and because the eligibility rules of the time let a man do it — he switched allegiance to **New Zealand**, and on the opening day of the tournament, on his All Blacks debut, against Italy, **Michael Jones scored the first try in the history of the Rugby World Cup.**

He scored again in the final. New Zealand beat France 29–9.

He is generally reckoned one of the two or three greatest opensides who ever played. He won more than fifty caps and, in World Rugby's own assessment on the Hall of Fame page that carries all of this, "**had it not been for injuries and his faith, Jones would have won far more.**"

His faith. Michael Jones would not play on Sundays.

At the **1991 World Cup**, his refusal "**restricted him to three appearances**." For the **1995 World Cup**, in World Rugby's flat phrasing, "**he was not considered.**"

Put the pieces on the table.

A **Samoan** international, raised in a faith he would not set aside for a fixture list, scores the **first try in Rugby World Cup history**, for **New Zealand**, in a tournament **Samoa was not invited to** — and the thing that costs him two World Cups of his own is **the Sabbath**.

Then, when he retired, he went back and **coached Samoa at two World Cups**.

The church and the export are not two forces in Pacific rugby, pulling against each other. They are one thing, and Michael Jones's career is what it looks like from the inside.

### 1987

Samoa was not invited to the first Rugby World Cup.

That is the flat fact, and Pat Lam — who was in his last year at St Peter's College in Auckland at the time — described what it meant:

> "At that time it was just one Test match against a visiting team, so having all those countries here was amazing. The All Blacks were dominant at that stage, **Samoa was not invited and there was nothing for young Samoans to aspire to at that stage**, particularly those living in New Zealand… every young Pacific Islander in those days wanted to play for the All Blacks."

Read that as a statement about institutions rather than about ambition, because that is what it is. The reason a generation of Samoan boys in South Auckland wanted the black jersey was not that they preferred it. **It was that the blue one was not on the table.** There was nothing to be exported *from*.

There is one further indignity in the record and it belongs here. When Fiji's May 1987 coup raised doubts about whether the Fijians could travel to the tournament, **Western Samoa was put on standby** to take their place. The New Zealand chapter and the Fiji chapter both carry it.

Samoa's relationship to the first Rugby World Cup was as a reserve.

### What this generation leaves behind

Samoa comes out of 1986 with a gold medal from Tahiti, a coach the country will bury with state honours, a village system producing forwards that New Zealand provinces are already scouting, one of the greatest players in the history of the sport who has just gone to play for somebody else, and no seat at the table.

The seat arrives in 1988. Three years later Samoa uses it in Cardiff.

## Generation 4: Cardiff (1987–1995)

**6 October 1991. Cardiff Arms Park.**

Wales are playing Western Samoa in a World Cup pool match. Wales have been playing international rugby for a hundred and ten years. Western Samoa have been affiliated to the game's governing body for three.

The Samoan side is captained by **Peter Fatialofa**, a **furniture remover from Auckland**. Their openside, **Pat Lam**, is making his Test debut. In the pack and behind it: **Apollo Perelini**, **Frank Bunce**, **Brian Lima**, **Stephen Bachop**, **Aumua To'o Vaega**. The try that people remember comes off a play between Vaega and Fatialofa, and **Sila Vaifale** catches it.

**Western Samoa 16, Wales 13.**

It produced one of the most repeated jokes in the history of the sport — *thank heavens Wales weren't playing the whole of Samoa* — which is a very funny line and also, when you look at it directly, a joke about a country being small enough to be beaten by only part of itself. It has been told at Samoa's expense for thirty-five years by people who meant it as a compliment.

Now pull back, because the more interesting question is not how Samoa won. It is who paid for them to be there.

---

### Who paid

Rugby was still amateur in 1991. Samoa had no union revenue worth the name, no broadcast deal, no sponsor of any size, and a squad drawn largely from men working ordinary jobs in Auckland.

The answer, in the account of the Samoans who were there, is that because "rugby had not yet turned professional", **raising the finances to get the team to the World Cup "mostly fell on businessmen" — on La'auli Alan Grey and on a man the record names simply as Annandale.**

That is worth stating plainly against everything this book has watched. England's clubs were arguing about broken time in a Westminster hotel in 1893. France's clubs were finding their players jobs in local businesses in the 1920s. Fiji's players were flying to Hong Kong with what Waisale Serevi called their little bags. And Western Samoa reached a World Cup quarter-final because two or three men in Apia went round and got the fares up.

Nothing about that is romantic. It is what happens when an institution exists but has no money, and it is why the results of 1991 could not be banked.

### What happened next in the tournament, and what happened next in Auckland

Samoa were then **narrowly beaten by Australia at Pontypool** — Australia won the tournament — and beat **Argentina at Pontypridd**, and reached the **quarter-final at Murrayfield**, where Scotland ended the run.

Both of the World Cup quarter-finals Samoa has ever reached fall inside this generation — **1991** and **1995**. They have not reached one since — a fact stated without ornament by the Samoan reporting itself: Samoa has not made a World Cup quarter-final **since rugby turned professional**.

But the thing 1991 actually changed was not in Wales. It was in South Auckland.

Pat Lam went back to his classroom afterwards — he was a teacher — and found the room different. His own account of the change is a straight before-and-after: "Compared to '87 when I was a schoolboy, **we didn't have any Samoa to aspire to** and every young Pacific Islander in those days wanted to play for the All Blacks." Then he came home from Wales, and the number of Samoan children in front of him with something else in mind had changed.

**Kevin Senio**, Fatialofa's nephew, was eleven, watching at a cousin's house:

> "It was the belief and the pride that we all had as Samoans from watching all around the world but witnessing that as a young kid and then realising '**We can do that too!**'"

In 1986 there was nothing for a Samoan boy in Auckland to want. In 1991 there was. That is the whole of what a national team is for, and Samoa built one on donated air fares.

### What this generation leaves behind

Western Samoa ends 1995 as the most admired small team in world rugby: two World Cups, two quarter-finals, a beaten Wales, a joke told in its honour, and a generation of Samoan children in two countries who now know the blue jersey exists.

In August 1995 the game goes open, and everything that made 1991 possible — amateurism, availability, a squad of working men who could take four weeks off — stops existing in the same month.

## Generation 5: The Diaspora Team (1996–2010)

**1999. Hong Kong.**

The **Manusina** — the Samoan women's team — play their first ever tournament, at the Hong Kong Sevens. The following year the fifteen-a-side side plays its first Test match, at home in Apia, against Japan.

That first Test came seventy-six years after the men played Fiji around a tree.

Nobody organised it from above. It was built, in the words of one of the players who was there, by a handful of people who decided to build it: **Dawn Rasmussen**, the campaign manager, "a stalwart for women's sport" already well known in Samoa for athletics, and **Tauanu'u Nick Bakulich**, the team manager, "instrumental in advocating for girls and women's rugby." The Manusina have since qualified for three Women's Rugby World Cups.

The men's team, in the same period, was going the other way — and the reason is the subject of this generation.

---

### What professionalism did

In August 1995 rugby union went open, and the arrangement that had produced 1991 ceased to exist.

The 1991 side had been a squad of working men — a furniture remover, a teacher, men in ordinary Auckland jobs — who could be released for a month because nobody's employer was a rugby club. After 1995 the best Samoan players had employers, and the employers were in Auckland, Sydney, London, Toulon, Tokyo and Osaka, and those employers had a view about when their assets were available.

There was no Samoan professional competition to set against them, because there was no Samoan economy that could fund one. What Samoa had instead was what it had always had: an extraordinary supply of players, and a village system that had been raising money to send chosen men away since 1830.

The result was a national team drawn almost entirely from other people's leagues — and it was still, on its day, formidable. On **14 October 1999**, at the Millennium Stadium in Cardiff, **Samoa beat Wales 38–31** at a second World Cup, with five tries: two for **Stephen Bachop**, one each for **Pat Lam**, **Lio Falaniko** and **Silao Leaega**, against two Welsh penalty tries. Samoa reached no World Cup quarter-final at all after 1995 — a fact Samoan writing about Samoan rugby states without flinching. And the ceiling was never about the players.

### The one-way valve

Then there was the rule.

For the whole of this period, rugby's eligibility regulations worked in one direction. A Samoan-qualified player capped by New Zealand or Australia was gone permanently. He could not come back, and the sums were not close: a Test career with the All Blacks or the Wallabies came with a professional contract, a Super Rugby franchise and a salary. A Test career with Samoa came with a jersey and an air fare.

It is important to see what that regulation actually was. It was not a plot against the Pacific. It was an integrity rule, written to stop players moving between countries for money, by unions who were thinking about Europe. But applied to a country whose best players were born or raised in the country that recruited them, it functioned as a **one-way valve**: talent flowed out of Samoa and could not flow back.

The anthropologist **Lisa Uperesa**, writing about the other half of the archipelago and the other football code, gives the general form of it. Elite athletes, she notes, quoting the anthropologist Thomas Carter, are treated as national treasures — "in some sports, like rugby, they are also seen as **national possessions**" — and this "veneer of celebrity actually **obscures the processes of transnational capital, the structures of labour in a transnational industry, and how states are complicit in structuring this industry.**"

And on the Pacific specifically, she is exact: the "**Pasifika rugby-playing diaspora**" builds on "**historically contingent labor migration schemes to former or existing colonial powers**, as well as a dispersed system of sport scholarships and club contracts."

*Labour migration schemes to former or existing colonial powers.* Samoa's players go to New Zealand, which administered Samoa until 1962. That is not a coincidence of talent. It is a pathway that was laid down by an administration, travelled first by labourers and nurses and soldiers, and then by footballers.

### The other half of the archipelago

Because forty miles east, in the American islands, exactly the same thing was happening with a different ball.

Uperesa's account of the American Samoan economy is a list of the ways a body can be converted into a wage. Under the colonial and territorial governments it was copra and **military service**. By mid-century it was the military plus **cannery** work — StarKist and Van Camp, supplying "**up to 50 percent of the US tuna market**", largely with non-resident labour — plus the federally funded bureaucracy. From 1975 to 2000, she writes, "the economy stagnated, with the tuna canneries and federal financial aid accounting for virtually all growth." What is left, for a young man in Tutuila, is "the federally funded bureaucracy, **military enlistment**, and outmigration."

And American football, through what people call the **Polynesian Pipeline** — "an elaborate network with multiple points of entry… destinations include high schools, **military prep academies**, junior colleges, and four-year colleges and universities across the nation."

How big? The honest figure is Uperesa's own, and it is smaller than the folklore. Her calculations for the documentary *In Football We Trust*, based on the 2013–2014 NFL season rosters and available demographic data, found **Samoans and Tongans to be twenty-eight times more likely to make it to the NFL than other ethnic groups in the United States.** (A figure of fifty-six times circulates widely and has no traceable method behind it. Twenty-eight is the one with arithmetic attached.)

Her judgement on what that visibility does is worth carrying into the rest of this chapter: "Today, the islands enter the US national imaginary primarily through gridiron football; this **hypervisibility works to erase Samoan history and the fullness of Samoan experience outside of sport.** Moreover, it obscures a century of US imperialism."

The same sentence is true of the western islands and rugby, and this book is not exempt from it.

**One people. Two empires. Two codes. One trade.**

### What this generation leaves behind

Samoa comes out of 2010 with a women's team that had to be built by volunteers, a men's team that beats Wales and cannot get past a pool, a governing regulation that lets its players leave and not return, and a diaspora larger and richer than the country.

The next generation is the one in which the players themselves say something about it, out loud, in London.

## Generation 6: The Union and the Players (2011–2019)

**November 2014. London.**

Samoa are due at Twickenham to play England, and the Samoan players are threatening not to play.

Senior members of the squad have written to the **International Rugby Board** setting out concerns about their own union: **misuse of funds**, and **interference in team selection**. Beneath that sit the grievances that had been accumulating for years — what the players were being paid, and what they were not being told.

The chairman of the Samoa Rugby Union at the time was **Tuilaepa Sailele Malielegaoi**, who was also the **Prime Minister of Samoa**. He called the players **spoilt children**, and said they were putting the nation to ransom.

The boycott was called off. The players met the IRB and the international players' association, the match went ahead, and very little in the newspapers the following week was about the letter.

Now go back and look at what that fixture actually was, because the shape of it explains the whole generation.

---

### Three parties, and none of them is a villain

Here is the arrangement, laid out flat.

**The union.** Samoa Rugby Union was chaired by the country's head of government. In a country of around two hundred thousand people, where rugby is the national sport and the union is a significant public institution, that is not the scandal it would be in England; it is closer to the norm in the Pacific, and it reflects the plain fact that in Samoa there is not a deep bench of people with the standing and the time to run a national body. It also means that criticising the union is criticising the government.

**The players.** Almost all of them were professionals employed by clubs in France, England, New Zealand, Australia and Japan. They were, for the most part, better paid, better advised and better connected to the international game than the body selecting them. When they wrote to Dublin rather than to Apia, they were not going over their union's head out of arrogance. They were using the only lever that existed.

**The governing body.** World Rugby — the IRB as it then was — found itself brokering between a member union and that union's own players, over the finances of a Test match staged in England for the benefit of a broadcast market in the northern hemisphere.

And what was the fixture worth? A November Test at Twickenham is one of the most valuable ninety minutes in the sport, and the money goes overwhelmingly to the home union. Samoa were there because the fee, whatever it was, was worth more to them than any match they could stage at home — the same calculation the Fiji chapter watches Fiji make, at greater scale, twelve years later.

By **2017**, in remarks widely reported at the time, the union's chairman — still the Prime Minister — acknowledged that **Samoa Rugby Union was insolvent.** (This book was not able to open the report at source; it is carried here as reported, and flagged as such.)

### The thing the strike was actually about

It is easy to file 2014 as a governance story, and it was one. But the players' complaint had a structural root that no change of chairman would have fixed.

Samoa's national team is assembled from men employed by foreign clubs, released for windows set by competitions Samoa does not sit on, to play matches scheduled for markets Samoa is not in, under a union funded largely by the governing body's development grants and the fees from those same matches.

Every party in that sentence is behaving rationally. The outcome is a national team that its own country can barely afford to watch.

### What this generation leaves behind

Samoa ends the 2010s with a union that has said out loud that it has no money, a squad that has demonstrated it will not simply absorb whatever it is handed, and a decade with no World Cup quarter-final in it.

The next generation brings a professional team at last, a rule change written with Samoa in mind, one point at Lille — and then, in a stadium in Utah and a stadium in Chile and a stadium in Dubai, the answer to the question of where Samoa now sits.

## Generation 7: Twenty-Fourth (2020–2026)

**18 November 2025. Dubai.**

Samoa are playing **Belgium** for the last place at the Rugby World Cup.

Not a play-off for seeding. Not a group match. The **twenty-fourth and final place** in the field, at the Final Qualification Tournament, against Belgium, Namibia and Brazil, on a neutral field in the Persian Gulf.

Samoa had beaten Brazil 48–10 and beaten Namibia. Against Belgium they needed a draw. They got one: **13–13**, and it was enough to win the tournament by a single point and take the last ticket to Australia.

This is the country that beat Wales at Cardiff in 1991 and beat Wales again in 1999, that reached two World Cup quarter-finals, that produced Brian Lima and Peter Fatialofa and the man who scored the first try in the tournament's history.

Twenty-fourth. By one point. Against Belgium. In Dubai.

Go back five years and watch how a country arrives there, because none of it is a story about Samoan players getting worse.

---

### Moana Pasifika, and where it had to be

In **2022**, for the first time, a professional rugby team existed for Samoan players — **Moana Pasifika**, entering **Super Rugby Pacific** in the same season as the Fijian Drua, built to give Samoan- and Tongan-eligible players a professional pathway and to build depth for both national teams. **Sekope Kepu** was its first captain. It lost its opening match **33–12** to the Crusaders on 4 March 2022 and finished twelfth in its first season, with a win over the Brumbies at Mt Smart along the way.

It is based in **South Auckland**.

Put that beside the Fiji chapter, and the difference is the entire chapter. Fiji's professional team plays in Fiji. Samoa's professional team plays in New Zealand — and not because anyone was careless, but because that is where the Samoans are. Auckland has one of the largest Samoan populations of any city on earth. A team built to develop Samoan players was built where the Samoan players live, and the Samoan players live in the country that administered Samoa until 1962.

*Samoa's professional pathway is in another country, and it is in that country for reasons that were settled long before rugby.*

### The rule turns round

In **November 2021** World Rugby amended **Regulation 8**, with effect from the first day of 2022: a capped player could change union once, after a **thirty-six-month stand-down**, if born in the new country or with a parent or grandparent born there. The Fiji chapter sets out the detail.

For Samoa it was the reversal of the one-way valve after a century of its running the other way. Players who had been capped by New Zealand and Australia — men who, under the old rule, were simply lost — became selectable.

**Lima Sopoaga**, an All Black, played fly half for Samoa at the 2023 World Cup.

It has to be said clearly what this did and did not do. It did not increase the number of good Samoan players; there were always plenty. It did not put money in the union. What it did was let Samoa field, for the first time, something close to the team its own bloodlines could produce — and it did so at the point in each of those players' careers when the wealthy unions no longer wanted them.

That is not nothing. It is also not the same as a pathway.

### Lille

**7 October 2023. Stade Pierre-Mauroy, Lille.** Samoa play England in the last match of Pool D.

Ollie Chessum scores for England. Then **Nigel Ah-Wong** scores twice in the first half, and Samoa lead at the break. The coach is **Seilala Mapusua**, who played in the shirt himself.

England bring on **Danny Care**, and Care scores the try that wins it, and **Owen Farrell** — who passes Jonny Wilkinson's England points record in the same match — sees it out.

**England 18, Samoa 17.**

Lima Sopoaga was named player of the match, on the losing side.

One point. It is the closest Samoa has come to a Tier 1 scalp at a World Cup in a quarter of a century, and it was achieved with a squad assembled from six countries' club competitions and a rule change less than two years old.

### The centenary, and who was not asked

In **2024**, Lakapi Samoa marked **a hundred years** since the founding of the Apia Rugby Union and the Vaiala club. There was a capping ceremony honouring everyone connected with Samoan rugby since 1924, and a thanksgiving service, and a centenary match against Tonga.

The Samoan reporting of it carried the sting in the same paragraph as the celebration: **there was controversy, because former players from both the men's and the women's teams said they had not been invited.**

**Seilala Mapusua**, at the centenary, gave the best available statement of what the team is for:

> "Regardless of how hard or how tough things are, this team can bring happiness or give our people hope that a small nation can compete on an international stage with the major teams around the world. **When Manu Samoa wins, every Samoan is happy** … in terms of Manu Samoa, when they play, **our people see themselves**, and that's really special."

And, on the year they were having: "Yes there are tough times and they're going through tough times at the moment … but I think that if you look back on our history **we've always endured, we've always come through and we've always found a way**."

### Apia, Salt Lake City, Viña del Mar

Then came thirteen months that put the whole argument on the scoreboard.

**18 July 2025, Apia.** Scotland finish their Pacific tour in Samoa and win **41–12**, seven tries to two, 22–0 up at half-time. **Benjamin Petaia Nee-Nee** and **Duncan Paia'aua** score for Samoa.

Six days earlier that same Scotland side had lost **29–14 to Fiji in Suva**. Two Pacific islands, one touring team, one week, and opposite results.

**20 September 2025.** Samoa play Chile in the first leg of a Rugby World Cup qualifying play-off. Chile are the country the Chile chapter follows from the nitrate ports of Iquique to the CARR at Parque Mahuida — a nation that reached its first World Cup in 2023 after a hundred and twenty-nine years of trying.

The first leg is Samoa's home fixture. It is played at **America First Field, Salt Lake City, Utah.**

Samoa go **25–8 down at half-time**, come back, and draw it **32–32**.

**27 September 2025. Estadio Sausalito, Viña del Mar.** Before **21,754 people**, **Chile beat Samoa 31–12** — Chile's first win over Samoa or any of the great Pacific sides — and become the twenty-third team to qualify for the 2027 World Cup.

Samoa go to Dubai.

### The second tier

And then, in 2026, the world formally split in two, and Samoa found out which half it was in.

The inaugural **Nations Championship** took the six Six Nations countries, the four of the Rugby Championship, **Japan and Fiji**. Below it, the inaugural **World Rugby Nations Cup** took the twelve teams that had been made to qualify for the 2027 World Cup through regional competitions: **USA, Chile, Samoa, Tonga, Uruguay and Canada** in one pool; **Georgia, Portugal, Spain, Romania, Hong Kong China and Zimbabwe** in the other; each side playing the six teams from the opposite pool across the July and November windows.

Fiji went up. Samoa and Tonga did not.

Samoa's three July matches were all listed by the broadcasters as **home** fixtures. All three were played in **Chile**.

- **4 July 2026, Estadio Nacional Julio Martínez Prádanos, Santiago: Samoa 66, Hong Kong China 19.** Ten tries. Tuna Tuitama inside two minutes, then Aki Seiuli, then Miracle Fai'ilagi, then **Warren Solomona twice**, then Manaaki Boyle-Tiatia, Iakopo Mapu, Connor Tupai, and **Elisapeta Alofipo twice in the last four minutes**. AJ Alatimu kicked eight conversions.
- **11 July 2026, Estadio Sausalito, Viña del Mar: Georgia 33, Samoa 12.** **Luka Matkava** kicked Georgia's points from the fourth minute onwards — the same Matkava whose penalty beat Wales at Cardiff in November 2022, which is where the Georgia chapter opens its final generation.
- **18 July 2026: Samoa 37, Romania 38.** By one point, against a country whose own chapter in this book is about a golden age that ended when the regime that funded it fell.

Look at the second of those again. **Samoa's "home" match against Georgia was played in the stadium where Chile had knocked them out of World Cup qualification nine months earlier**, on the same weekends, and twice in the very grounds where Chile was playing its own Nations Cup fixtures, as the undercard of somebody else's home season.

### Three

There is one more number, and it is the one to end on.

The *Samoa Observer* published the Manu Samoa squad for that Nations Cup campaign. **Thirty-two players.** By location: **thirteen in New Zealand, six in France, four in Australia, two in the United Kingdom, two in Japan, two elsewhere.**

And **three in Samoa**.

Their names are **Taunu'u Niulevaea** of Aana, **Elisapeta Alofipo** of Fa'asaleleaga, and **Warren Solomona** of Falealili. The head coach, **Lemalu Tusiata Pisi**, was careful about how he described them:

> "**I don't really like using the term local players, because we're all one.** But the players that do play here (in Samoa), obviously these three here at the moment, have been outstanding in terms of their performance."

Solomona, on being picked:

> "I feel very proud and happy this morning, especially as I am **representing my village of Falealilili** and that I am a proud member of the Laumua o Tumua."

In Santiago on 4 July, Warren Solomona scored two tries on his debut. Elisapeta Alofipo came off the bench and scored two more.

**Two of the three players based in Samoa scored two tries each in the same match.**

### Where things stood, in the middle of 2026

On **15 July 2026** the World Rugby men's rankings had **Samoa nineteenth** and **Tonga twentieth**, both down a place after their Nations Cup results, with **Fiji ninth**. In the women's rankings the Manusina stood **fifteenth**, with the Fijiana thirteenth and Tonga twenty-eighth.

Head coach: **Lemalu Tusiata Pisi**. Ahead: the November leg of the Nations Cup, and then **Rugby World Cup 2027 in Australia**, where Samoa — the twenty-fourth qualifier — were drawn in **Pool E with France, Japan and the United States**.

### What this chapter leaves behind

Start with the border, because everything in this chapter comes out of it.

On **2 December 1899** three imperial governments divided one people. Nobody Samoan signed it. Germany, and then New Zealand, took the west; the United States took the east and the harbour. Within nineteen years the two administrations had been given the same test on the same week, and one of them lost **between a fifth and a quarter of its population** while the other lost nobody, and when the eastern islands offered help the western administration **refused it**.

Then each half was handed a football.

The west got rugby, from **Marist Brothers in 1920**, because the Marists had been there since 1845, because the LMS had been there since 1830, and because the administering power played rugby. The east got American football, because the administering power played American football, and because from the 1940s onward the routes out of Tutuila ran through the US military, the canneries and the American college system.

**Neither half chose. Both halves are, per head, among the most prolific producers of professional athletes on earth. Both halves export them to the metropole that took them in 1899.** One trade, two balls, one border.

That is this book's thesis in its most literal available form, and it required no rugby institution to produce it. **Rugby has no class, and in Samoa it has no ethnicity either — it has a colonial boundary, drawn twenty-five years before Samoa's first Test match, by men who had never seen the place.**

Now the second half of the argument, which is about the village.

The institution that carries Samoan rugby is the one Samoans built for the church in the 1830s: **a village that collectively feeds and maintains a chosen man so that he can do, on its behalf, something the village cannot do itself.** That produced the *faifeau*. It produced the six Vaiala men whose names went out on the radio in 1970. It produced the fundraising that got a squad of Auckland tradesmen to Cardiff in 1991. It produced Warren Solomona of Falealili, who is proud to be representing his village, and who scored twice in Santiago.

It is a magnificent institution and it is also the reason the export cannot be stopped by anyone in Samoa. **The village's interest and the country's interest are not the same.** A village that raises money to send its best young man to a French academy is doing exactly what it has always done, correctly, and every such decision makes Lakapi Samoa weaker. There is no villain here and there is no reform that fixes it, because the thing producing the outcome is the strongest and most admirable institution in Samoan life.

And so the record reads as it does.

Samoa beat Wales at Cardiff in **1991** and at Cardiff again in **1999**. It reached World Cup quarter-finals in **1991** and **1995** and has reached none since the game turned professional. It produced the man who scored **the first try in Rugby World Cup history**, for another country, in a tournament it was not invited to, and who lost two World Cups of his own to the Sabbath. It had to be talked out of a strike at Twickenham in **2014** by the sport's governing body. Its union was reported to have declared itself **insolvent** in 2017. Its professional team plays in **Auckland**. It lost its World Cup place to **Chile** in Viña del Mar, took the last one available from **Belgium** in Dubai, played its July 2026 home matches in **Chile**, and named **three home-based players** in a squad of thirty-two.

And two of those three scored two tries each on the same July afternoon, in Chile, in a competition for the countries that were made to qualify.

*"When Manu Samoa wins, every Samoan is happy,"* Seilala Mapusua said at the centenary. *"When they play, our people see themselves."*

That is what the blue jersey is for, and it is why none of this has stopped it. The village keeps sending men. The men keep coming back for the jersey. And the institution that makes them one of the great exporters of rugby players in the world is the same institution that makes them impossible to keep.

Tonga is next, and it does all of this from twentieth in the world, with half the population, and one further complication: it was never annexed by anybody.
