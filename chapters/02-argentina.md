# Argentina

## Generation 0: The British Enclave (1873–1964)

**14 May 1874.** On a country estate in Caballito, on the western edge of Buenos Aires, the members of the Buenos Aires Football Club met before kick-off to settle an argument they had been having, on and off, for seven years: which rules to play by. They voted for the laws of rugby union. Then Mr Trench's side played Mr Hogg's side under those laws, on David Mathven's land, and the match is remembered as the first game of rugby ever played in Argentina.

It did not take. Within a year the club had gone back to association rules, because the rugby games kept injuring people. To see why the game could arrive and then keep vanishing, you have to go back to the room it came out of — a room full of Englishmen who could not agree on what the word *football* meant.

---

When Thomas and James Hogg founded the Buenos Aires Football Club on **9 May 1867**, on Temple street — Viamonte today — football in England was still a set of regional dialects, not a single language. A man from one town used his feet, a man from the next used his hands, a third used both. So when the club took the field that June on the Buenos Aires Cricket Club ground, the members simply played the way each had played at home, and the game came out a hybrid with no fixed laws, negotiated fixture by fixture.

For a decade they cycled through codes. Modified FA rules and eight a side in 1867; six or eleven men by 1868, with the ball carried if you caught it clean; then the vote for rugby in 1874, a reversion to association in 1875 over the injuries, a carry-the-ball compromise in 1876, rugby again weeks later. It was less a sport finding its shape than a group of homesick men arguing about which version of home they missed most. In 1870 the argument stopped entirely: yellow fever swept Buenos Aires, killed something like eight percent of the city, and the club ceased to exist. Hogg refounded it on **25 April 1873**. The club's last recorded matches, in 1881, were rugby friendlies — one of them against **Montevideo Cricket Club**, the marooned enclave that opens the Uruguay story, reaching across the River Plate to the only neighbours who played its game.

Thomas Hogg gave *The Standard* his prediction for that game's future, and it marks the fork in the road cleanly. The sport would spread slowly even among the British residents, he wrote, but they should persist, because it was the best, cheapest pastime available for middle-class youth and for the common people. Cheap; for the people. He was describing **football** — and no one ever said anything of the kind about rugby.

---

The early clubs admitted only English members. Natives were taken in rarely, and as a deliberate exception; from the union's founding in 1899 through to 1903 every club in Argentine rugby had been founded by Englishmen or their descendants, on the stated grounds that only they knew the rules and their families were high society.

The barrier was not simply British snobbery, and the record shows it running the other way too. One contemporary British account complains that local friends — men glad to play *cricket* alongside the British — took offence at being asked to join the football club. A militia officer named Gallino said he had not gone mad enough to play football, since it could get him discharged from the army on the spot. To an Argentine officer in the 1870s, taking up the game read as a kind of denationalisation, a career risk. The exclusion was mutual.

The first criollo rugby team appears only in **1904** — the Centro de Estudiantes de la Facultad de Ingeniería, grown out of a students' association called *La Línea Recta* that engineering men had founded in 1894. It took thirty years for the game to produce a native side; the following year they beat a team of British residents 21–5. Football moved faster. The revolt against exclusion at Quilmes produced **Argentino de Quilmes in 1899**, remembered as the first creole club, which adopted the national **light blue and white** to say exactly what it meant. Football had its nationalist breakout, in flag colours, five years before rugby managed a single faculty side — and that gap is much of the story of the two games in Argentina.

How the state itself read rugby is caught in a single incident. In **1890**, a match in Buenos Aires ended with both teams and some **2,500 spectators arrested**: President Juárez Celman's police, jittery after the Revolution of the Park that July, took the gathering for a political meeting in disguise. Two things sit inside that. Rugby already drew crowds of thousands. And the authorities, looking at a field of Anglophile gentlemen, saw something foreign and suspect — a reading of the game that would cling to it for decades.

---

Then the institutional turn — the point at which Argentina quietly assembles the machinery Uruguay never would.

On **10 April 1899**, clubs from Buenos Aires and Rosario founded **The River Plate Rugby Union**; the founders usually named are Buenos Aires FC, Belgrano, Flores and Lomas, with Rosario Athletic. That season it ran the first club championship — the ancestor of today's Torneo de la URBA — won by **Lomas**. The union was **affiliated to the English Rugby Football Union until 1932**, which places its rulebook and its loyalties exactly. It is one of the older unions in the game — younger than the founding home nations and South Africa, but older than France's or Australia's, and half a century older than Uruguay's. (In November 1951 it renamed itself the Unión Argentina de Rugby — because Uruguay, too, sits on the River Plate, on the eastern bank, and had said so.)

Underneath the union sat a mechanism that becomes visible only when the clubs are lined up side by side: Argentine rugby's great clubs were, overwhelmingly, **football clubs that quit football when football turned professional**. The clearest case is **CASI** — Club Atlético de San Isidro — founded on 24 October 1902 when thirty-three members of two clubs voted to merge. By its own history the club played twenty-six consecutive seasons of Primera División football, from 1906 to 1931, winning the Tie Cup and running as league runners-up more than once. Rugby, by contrast, failed there twice over: a first XV formed in 1908 out of Central Argentine Railway employees dissolved in 1911 without entering a competition, and the game had to be restarted in 1917 by the club president, Rafael Cullen. Then, in **1931 — the year Argentine football went professional — CASI walked away from football altogether**. It is now the most successful rugby club in the country's history, with more than thirty URBA titles.

The pattern repeats. Lomas left football in 1909. Club Atlético del Rosario left in 1916, after which Plaza Jewell — the oldest sports ground in the country, site of the first inter-club match in 1886 — has been used for rugby alone ever since. Córdoba Athletic, founded 1882, dropped football when it professionalised in 1931 and turned to the rugby it had added in 1898. Belgrano Athletic, one of the leading football clubs of the 1900s, is remembered as having made the same move around 1919, carrying its resources into rugby, cricket and hockey — though the details of that club's early switch survive mostly in unofficial histories.

The logic behind the exodus was consistent. When football professionalised, the Anglo-elite clubs found paid sport vulgar and left it, taking their grounds, their money and their gentlemen's-amateur creed straight into rugby; the union then guarded that creed, RFU-fashion, for sixty years. Uruguay never had this sorting event. Argentina got a purpose-built, land-owning, well-capitalised rugby network almost as a by-product of the gentry's distaste for paid football.

Just how narrow that world stayed is written into the schism that created SIC. In **1935**, the customary post-match gathering after a CASI–Atlético del Rosario fixture was held in CASI's Salón Cullen, with no women present; the argument over that culture fed the split that produced **San Isidro Club**. SIC moved to Boulogne in 1939, onto land crossed by a ditch, and wore the nickname *El Zanjero*, the ditch-digger. The first CASI–SIC match, on 9 May 1937, went to SIC 3–0, and the fixture became the Superclásico of Argentine rugby — the defining rivalry of the sport, a schism inside the Anglo-Argentine gentry of a single suburb.

---

Set against all of that institutional building is the international ledger, and it reads the same way for ninety-one years. Argentina did not leave South America, and did not beat a touring side. Not once.

The **first international** came on 12 June 1910, at Sociedad Sportiva Argentina in Palermo, against a British Isles team — later counted as the first **British Lions** tour of the country — sent out for the centenary of the May Revolution. Managed by Oxford University, nominally England but carrying three Scots, and known locally as the Combined British, the tourists won every fixture and took the Test 28–3. Buried in the reports is the one thing worth keeping: every player on the field for Argentina was Argentine-born. After thirty-seven years the game had produced native players, even where the institutions above them stayed Anglo.

The Lions returned in **1927** and won heavily, but left behind the jersey the country still wears: **Abelardo Gutiérrez, of Gimnasia y Esgrima de Buenos Aires, proposed the light blue and white stripes**, and Argentina wore them against the tourists for the first time. The **Junior Springboks** came in **1932** and won all eight matches; the **Lions** came again in **1936** and won the Test 23–0. **France** arrived in **1949**, the first European national side to visit, and won both Tests; **France** returned in **1954** and won all eleven of its Argentine matches, taking both Tests; the **Junior Springboks** came back in **1959**, played fifteen matches and won all fifteen, both Tests included. **France toured again in 1960.** Three Lions tours, two Junior Springbok tours, three French tours across fifty years, and not one Argentine Test win among them.

What Argentina did win was the South American Championship, repeatedly and to little purpose. The third consecutive title in 1961 was built on results like **Brazil 60–0**, all played at Carrasco Polo in Montevideo — the same Carrasco that anchors the Uruguay story. The problem was plain and often named: geographical isolation. Argentina towered over Chile, Uruguay and Brazil, had no real match on its own continent, and was never invited off it. Beating Brazil 60–0 taught nothing. That was the trap in a sentence — too strong for the neighbours, unable to beat anyone else, and never once asked to travel.

---

Two things were being built underneath the losing.

The first was the **Campeonato Argentino**, launched in the 1940s, which set the Buenos Aires selection against representative teams from the **Interior provinces** — the one mechanism that pushed the game out of the capital. It is why Argentina eventually had **Tucumán**, the flag-burning provincial heartland that would supply its hardest forwards, and why Uruguay grew nothing comparable. It also planted a tension that outlived the era: Buenos Aires held the strong clubs and the money while the provincial unions stayed poor.

The second was the slow arrival of the schools and the Catholic orders. The Buenos Aires English High School, founded in 1884 by Alexander Watson Hutton — remembered as the father of Argentine *football* — had carried rugby among its sports; by 1955 the **Marist Brothers'** college was fielding a side called *Los Tábanos*, the Horseflies, which became Club Manuel Belgrano in 1958. The orders were moving in, exactly as the Christian Brothers were in Montevideo — but in Argentina they fed an ecosystem that already held scores of clubs, where in Uruguay they more or less *were* the ecosystem.

---

Ninety-one years of losing left a great deal standing. A club network of rare depth — dozens of clubs across Greater Buenos Aires, most of them ex-football clubs that had fled professionalism with their grounds and their money intact. A union from 1899, RFU-affiliated, with an amateur creed held like doctrine. A national jersey from 1927 and native players from 1910. A provincial competition quietly building a base beyond the capital.

And, against all that, a social ceiling and a settled sense of inferiority — a game a militia officer had once feared would cost him his commission, that admitted no criollos until 1904, whose defining schism turned partly on whether women might attend post-match drinks. Everything was in place except belief and a method.

In 1964, the South African Rugby Board sent both to Buenos Aires, in the person of a bow-tied Afrikaans schoolmaster who had escaped an Italian prisoner-of-war camp.

## Generation 1: The Breakout (1965–1973)

**19 June 1965. Ellis Park, Johannesburg.** In the last minutes against the Junior Springboks, the Argentine centre **Marcelo Pascual** flung himself at the line and dived over — *la palomita*, the little dove, the swallow-dive that Argentine rugby has replayed in its memory ever since. Argentina won 11–6. It was the first time in ninety-two years of playing the game that Argentina had beaten a touring representative side of any kind, and it happened in the country that had invented much of what the visitors knew. The players were reluctant to leave the field; a crowd met them when they flew home. How twenty-six amateurs from a country that had never won got to that afternoon is the whole of this generation.

---

It began, as these things often do, with a defeat. In **1959** the Junior Springboks toured Argentina, played fifteen matches and won all fifteen, both Tests included. On the ledger it was one more Gen 0 entry — visitors arrive, visitors win, visitors leave. But the South Africans went home impressed, not by the results but by the setting: the depth of clubs across Greater Buenos Aires, the crowds, the seriousness of a rugby culture that the gentry's flight from paid football had accidentally built. In **1964** the South African Rugby Board sent the UAR an invitation to tour South Africa — the first time in ninety-one years that anyone had asked Argentina to travel rather than merely hosting them.

The South Africans did more than invite. Danie Craven advised on the preparation, and the board sent a coach from Durban to ready the Argentines and work alongside the young local pair, **Alberto Camardón** and **Ángel Guastella**. The man they sent was **Izak van Heerden**, and he would matter to Argentine rugby more than any other outsider in its history.

He was born in Utrecht, in Natal, in 1910, qualified as a teacher at Natal University College, and became a schoolmaster at **Durban High School**, where he taught Afrikaans and coached the school's rugby so well that the province noticed. During the war he served as a gunner in North Africa and was captured at Benghazi alongside another Durban High master and coach, **Bill Payn**; interned first in North Africa and then in Italy, he escaped a camp near Rome and spent nine months in the hills before British forces found him. He came home to teaching and to the Natal coaching job — a large, imposing figure who arrived at training straight from school in a jacket and a **bow tie**, quick-tempered, funny, free with hard language.

What kept him out of the South African set-up was simple: he had never been a Springbok. Beyond a 1962 series in which his Natal side comprehensively outplayed the touring Lions, his ideas were never sought at national level, on the settled assumption that great players make great coaches and Van Heerden had not been one. The best rugby mind in the country was left teaching schoolboys in Durban — and so was available, in 1964, to a union at the far end of the world.

---

What he taught them still shows in an Argentine pack. Van Heerden had, in effect, invented the **"tight loose"** — the close-quarters work around the breakdown, forwards holding continuity and possession through the churn rather than treating each phase as a separate event. It became the signature of the Argentine game and never left it. His creed was expressed plainly, in the manner of a man used to fifteen-year-olds: the ball itself is the advantage; treat every piece of possession like a **nugget**, and nobody kicks a nugget away. Keep the ball.

Those who saw his teams vouched for the results. **Tommy Bedford**, who captained both Natal and the Springboks, called him a genius streets ahead of his time, a coach who hunted for two qualities in a player — the ability to think and the nerve to decide on the field — and whose sides played an instinctive, expressive rugby. **Kitch Christie**, who would coach South Africa to the 1995 World Cup, said openly that Van Heerden had been light years ahead of everyone, that he had used his thinking as the blueprint for his own team, and that much of what modern coaches say was first said by Izak. The tactical mind behind South Africa's 1995 title had, thirty years earlier, handed Argentina its playing identity — because South Africa would not employ him. Van Heerden took leave from Durban High, moved to Buenos Aires, and learned fluent Spanish.

---

The tour itself ran on preparation Argentina had never attempted before. Twenty-six amateurs trained through 1964 and into 1965, a long pre-selection of sessions and warm-up matches against Universitario de La Plata, Obras Sanitarias, Alumni, Newman, Córdoba, Rosario, Duendes, Old Georgian. Then they travelled — some 46,252 kilometres by air and land over two months — and played sixteen matches, winning eleven, losing four, drawing one, under captain **Aitor Otaño**. The two results that mattered they won: Southern Universities, and then the Junior Springboks at Ellis Park, the same outfit that had swept Argentina six years earlier. At home the win was received as the first landmark victory in the country's rugby history.

The name came off that tour, out of a deadline. **Carl Kohler**, of the Johannesburg paper *Die Transvaler*, wanted a catchy nickname in the tradition of All Blacks and Springboks; he asked Van Heerden, and the two looked at the UAR crest and its spotted cat. Kohler knew the Americas had both jaguars and pumas, was under pressure to file, and guessed. The animal on the crest is a **yaguareté** — a jaguar — but he wrote *Pumas*, and it stuck. The Argentines adopted it themselves and wore it with pride, and the crest went on showing a jaguar until 2023: for nearly sixty years the team was named for one cat while wearing a picture of another. *(The scene is told differently in places — one version attributes the coinage to a writer at* The Farmer's Weekly *after the opening loss to Rhodesia in Salisbury. The Kohler version is better documented; what is not disputed is that the name is a naturalist's error made by a South African sportswriter.)*

---

The tour also put the Argentines inside **apartheid South Africa**, and they saw it. The delegation was met with real warmth from most of the Black South Africans they encountered, and experienced the segregation system directly. The Argentine account of the trip is that they won the crowds over with intelligent rugby and good conduct, and that South Africa — where the game was close to a religion — opened for them a door onto the first-world rugby map that they never walked back out of. That is the honest mechanism of Argentina's rise: South Africa let them in when no one else would. The debt incurred there — the invitation, the coach, the legitimacy — is why, fifteen years on, Argentine players would tour apartheid South Africa under a flag of convenience while their own government forbade it. The moral knot of Generation 2 is tied here, in 1965, in gratitude.

---

Then the handover that turned an episode into a lineage. Van Heerden went home, and **Ángel Guastella** succeeded him in 1965 — a name barely known outside Argentina, and the connective tissue of the modern game. A founding member of Club Pueyrredón, a fly-half capped between 1956 and 1960, he won four South American Championships as head coach before resigning in 1973. But he returned in 1978 to rebuild the side from nothing, with José Imhoff and Aitor Otaño as assistants, and his first act was to name a young fly-half, **Hugo Porta**, as captain, building around him with the veteran Héctor Silva and a wave of youngsters — Campo, Iachetti, **Marcelo Loffreda**, Madero, Petersen, Travaglini. That squad is Generation 2, and Loffreda would one day coach the golden generation to third in the world. The line runs unbroken: Van Heerden to Camardón and Guastella, to Silva and Loffreda, to 2007 — and at every World Cup Argentina has contested, at least one former Puma has sat in the coaching staff, the lone exception being Wales in 1999. Guastella himself lived out his last years in **Tucumán**, chairman of its rugby union — the custodian of Van Heerden's method ending up in the provincial heartland, which is as neat a summary of the game's escape from Buenos Aires as one could want.

Van Heerden returned to Durban High School, was promoted to vice-principal, and in June 1973 — the year Guastella first stepped down — dropped dead one morning in his office. A field at the school carries his name.

---

In nine years Generation 1 left a playing identity that has never been replaced — the tight loose, the ball as nugget, forward continuity, thinking players deciding for themselves. It left a **name**, arrived at by a sportswriter's error and worn ever since without irony. It left a **door**: international legitimacy, granted by South Africa, that Argentina held open from then on. It left a **coaching lineage** running from a Durban schoolmaster to a World Cup medal. And it left a **first win** — Ellis Park, 1965 — after ninety-two years as someone else's fixture. All of it achieved by twenty-six amateurs under a bow-tied Afrikaans schoolteacher his own country had passed over because he had never worn the green jersey.

The bill for the gift came due soon enough. In November 1973 the Argentine government banned all sporting contact with apartheid South Africa — and into that collision walked a boy who had nearly signed for Boca Juniors, and chose rugby instead.

## Generation 2: The Porta Generation (1971–1987)

**3 April 1982. Free State Stadium, Bloemfontein.** A week earlier, in Pretoria, the Springboks had beaten the visiting South American side 50–18, a formality watched by a half-empty Loftus; nobody rated the tourists as a real international team. Seven days later the same Springbok side lost 21–12, and one man scored all twenty-one of the visitors' points — a try, a conversion, four penalties and a drop goal. His name was **Hugo Porta**, and the team he was playing for did not, officially, exist. To understand how a phantom side captained by a converted footballer came to beat South Africa, you have to start with the boy and the politics that boxed him in.

---

In the early sixties there was a boy in Buenos Aires who was very good at football — good enough that Boca Juniors were interested, good enough that the Bombonera and the national obsession were genuinely open to him. **Hugo Porta**, born 11 September 1951, chose rugby, and joined **Banco Nación**, the club he would play for his whole career and, in December 2018, come to preside over. He walked away from the national religion for a game that had admitted no criollos until 1904 and had never beaten a touring side — and became the greatest player it ever produced.

He debuted on 10 October 1971, aged twenty, at fly-half against Chile in a South American Championship match, and won four caps that first year against the neighbours. By 1973 he had a dozen caps against South American sides, an unofficial South Africa XV, Romania and an Ireland XV — learning his trade in the same vacuum every Argentine had: too strong for the continent, with no access to anyone beyond it. And then the vacuum closed almost completely, because of politics.

---

The politics are inseparable from the era. In **1971** the government forbade the Pumas from playing a scheduled match in Rhodesia during their South African tour. In **March 1973** it challenged the permit the UAR had granted **San Isidro Club** to tour South Africa and forced the union's entire federal committee to resign; a new one was elected on 24 April 1973. Then, in **November 1973**, it banned the contact outright — no Argentine team to South Africa, no South African team to Argentina. The ban held until 1991.

The position that left Argentina in was bitter. The only country that had ever invited them out, sent them a coach, given them their name and their first great win, was now closed to them by their own government's law, on anti-apartheid grounds — and no one else was calling. The legitimacy South Africa had granted in 1965 had been confiscated at home in 1973.

---

The workaround became one of rugby's great grey-area manoeuvres. In **1980** the South African Rugby Union, desperate to break its own isolation, invited the UAR to send a team; the UAR could not send Argentina, so it sent something that was not, technically, Argentina. On paper it was **Sudamérica XV**, a South American representative side drawn from Argentina, Uruguay, Chile, Paraguay, Brazil — and, by 1984, Spain — in a white jersey striped blue, red and gold, its badge carrying four animals: a **puma** for Argentina, a **condor** for Chile, a **tero** for Uruguay, a **yacaré** for Paraguay. (The tero is Uruguay's lapwing, the bird from the Uruguay chapter, now sharing a crest with the jaguar that everyone called a puma.) Everyone called the side **the Jaguars**, and it was a shadow national team the UAR did not officially recognise — invented to slip past the government's prohibition.

The 1982 tour makes the fiction plain. Fourteen matches, a party of forty-two players — two fixtures were scheduled on every itinerary date, Test days included, so two squads of twenty-one were needed — and of the forty-two, thirty were Argentine, with five Chileans, five Uruguayans and two Paraguayans. The sides that played the two Tests were all-Argentine to a man, captained by Porta. South Africa awarded those Tests full cap status; Argentina never did — which is why South African supporters can still say, accurately and beside the point, that the Springboks have never lost to the Pumas.

At Bloemfontein the machinery was Gen 1 inheritance weaponised. Porta steered territory through the **"bajada"**, the coordinated eight-man scrum shove that became Argentina's signature, and finished with his boot: the try came off a jink past **Naas Botha** and **Ray Mordt**, and the rest was kicking. It was the only Test the Jaguars ever won against South Africa — one from eight across the fixtures — and Van Heerden's forward continuity married to Porta's goal-kicking was, from then on, the Argentine formula.

---

Reducing Porta to a kicker misreads him, though the boot alone was extraordinary: he retired with 590 Test points and a then-record tally of drop goals, and twice landed three drop goals in a single international — against Australia in 1979 and New Zealand in 1985. But he also scored ten Test tries, had the soft hands and the sidestep, and is remembered in the same breath as **Naas Botha, Mark Ella, Phil Bennett and Barry John**, the great fly-halves of the age.

The results that came in those years were the first real ones Argentina had ever had. In **1977** Porta's Pumas drew with a touring France. In **1978** they drew **13–13 with England at Twickenham** — a first result against a home union on its own ground. On **27 October 1979** they beat **Australia 24–13** in Buenos Aires, Porta scoring 18 with two conversions, a penalty and three drop goals; they lost the second Test, but it was the first time Argentina had beaten a major nation. In **1980** Porta led two wins over Fiji, and that same year the Springboks — denied Argentine visas under the ban — played the Jaguars in Uruguay and Chile instead; in their touring party was **Errol Tobias**, who the following year, against Ireland in 1981, would become the first black player to start a Test for the Springboks. In **1981** England came to Buenos Aires for the first *official* Tests between the countries, a 19–19 draw and a 12–6 England win, both at Ferrocarril Oeste. Then **1982**: Bloemfontein — and, days earlier, the start of the **Falklands War**, which froze England fixtures until 1990. In **1983** Argentina went to **Ballymore, Brisbane**, and beat **Australia 18–3** on their own soil. In June **1985** they beat **France 24–16**.

And on **2 November 1985**, at Ferrocarril Oeste before some twenty-five thousand people, Argentina drew the second Test with **New Zealand, 21–21** — Porta scoring every point, four penalties and three drop goals. It remains the closest Argentina has come to beating the All Blacks. That year Porta was named **Clarín Sportsman of the Year**, in a country where football is the overwhelming obsession — a rugby player, in Argentina, its athlete of the year.

By then Guastella's 1978 rebuild had matured — Porta as captain, the veteran **Héctor Silva** alongside a blooded generation of Campo, Iachetti, **Loffreda**, Madero, Petersen and Travaglini — and between 1985 and 1987, with Silva as head coach and Guastella his assistant, the Van Heerden line was still visibly running.

---

The era closed in 1987, and in the right order it reads as a climb, not a collapse. First came the **inaugural Rugby World Cup** in May and June. Argentina had been *invited* to it and only then admitted to the IRB — the union founded in 1899, older than France's or Australia's, had not been a member of the game's governing body until a pool needed filling. Porta captained them at thirty-five; they lost to **Fiji 9–28**, beat **Italy 25–16** in Christchurch, lost to **New Zealand 15–46**, and went out in the pool.

Then, that October and November, **Australia** toured. Argentina drew the first Test 19–19 on 31 October and won the second **27–19 on 7 November**, at Vélez Sarsfield — a series win over one of the world's best sides, sealed by a late try from **Cristian Mendy** and 23 points from Porta's boot. He was thirty-six, and he retired after it, returning only briefly in 1990. The career totalled 58 caps by the usual count (some say 57), 34 of them as captain, across 1971 to 1987 — and, counting the unofficial matches, 71 games and 656 points.

---

Then the story turned, in a way stories rarely do. In **1991**, the year the ban on contact with South Africa was finally lifted, Porta was appointed **Argentina's ambassador to South Africa**, serving through the turbulent years just before Mandela took power. The man who had captained a shadow side under a flag of convenience to slip past the boycott, and scored all 21 at Bloemfontein against a state his own government would not recognise, became his country's diplomat in the new South Africa. He was a regular at Loftus, is the only Argentine ever to have played for the South African Barbarians, and later served as Argentina's national sports minister. *Midi Olympique* named him the world's best player in 1985; *Rothmans* named him the best fly-half of the 1980s; he sits in the International Rugby, IRB and New Zealand halls of fame.

---

What Generation 2 left, above all, was **proof**. Gen 0 had given Argentina clubs and money and a union and no belief; Gen 1 had given a method, a name and a single win; Gen 2 gave evidence — Australia beaten home and away, France beaten, England held at Twickenham, New Zealand drawn 21–21, and South Africa beaten at Bloemfontein by a team that officially did not exist. It also left a moral knot Argentine rugby has never much liked to pick at: that its greatest era rested partly on defying an anti-apartheid boycott under an invented flag, out of gratitude to the country that had let it in when no one else would.

And it left the thinnest of foundations. At Bloemfontein and against the All Blacks, one man had scored every point — the same man, twice. Argentina had not built a team so much as a genius, wrapped in a very good pack and running a Durban schoolmaster's system. When Porta walked off in November 1987, the question was whether any of it would survive him. For about a decade, the answer was no.

**Gen 3** is where that answer plays out: professionalism arrives in 1995 and detonates the amateur creed the Anglo clubs had guarded since 1919, the best players vanish to France and England, the Pumas spend the nineties being hammered — and out of that exile, almost by accident, a generation is forged in Europe's professional leagues that will come home and finish third in the world.

## Generation 3: The Self-Inflicted Wound (1987–1999)

**20 October 1999. Stade Félix-Bollaert, Lens.** Argentina beat Ireland 28–24 to reach a World Cup quarter-final for the first time in their history — a side made up mostly of amateurs, under a head coach who had been in the job a fortnight and had run two training sessions with them. To see how a team that thin got to that afternoon in northern France, you have to walk back through the worst decade Argentine rugby ever had, and the rule that caused it.

---

When Porta walked off in November 1987, what Argentina had built went with him. It had never been a system — it was a man wrapped in a very good pack, running a Durban schoolmaster's method — and once he left, the weakness underneath surfaced all at once. The cause was almost too neat: the amateur creed that had *created* Argentine rugby now strangled it.

The creed was the Gen 0 inheritance. Belgrano had left football around 1919, CASI in 1931, and the rest walked away from paid sport as vulgar, carrying grounds, money and gentlemen's amateurism into rugby, which the UAR then guarded for sixty years. So when the game turned professional worldwide after **1995**, the union had a rule on its books that became a slow suicide: any player who turned professional was barred from playing for Argentina. In a country whose best players were suddenly being offered contracts in France, England, Italy and Australia, the rule did not stop the exodus — it made it permanent, because every man who signed abroad was, in the same act, retiring from the national side. **Patricio Noriega**, the tighthead, went to Australia and ended up a **Wallaby**; the hooker **Federico Méndez** went to South Africa. The nineties Pumas watched their best players leave and were then forbidden to pick them.

The results mapped the damage. In **1990** the Pumas toured the British Isles, playing a first away Test against England and their first-ever Tests against Ireland and Scotland. At the **1991 World Cup** they lost all three pool games — Australia 19–32, Wales 7–16, and, tellingly, **Western Samoa 12–35**. At the **1995 World Cup** they went out in the pool again, beaten by Samoa once more; the one bright spot was a pack the international press admired, and it was on that showing that Noriega and Méndez earned the contracts that took them away. In **1997**, New Zealand beat Argentina **93–8** at Athletic Park in Wellington — still the heaviest defeat in the country's history, the second Test's 62–10 barely registering beside it. In **1998** a single tour brought Test defeats to **Japan** (44–29), Italy, France and Wales. A decade after a 21–21 draw with the All Blacks, Argentina were losing to Japan.

---

Into that wreckage came an unlikely figure: **Alexander "Grizz" Wyllie** (1944–2025), a gruff, moustachioed New Zealander, Canterbury to the marrow — 210 matches for the province from 1964 to 1979, a Ranfurly Shield captain, an All Black from 1970. He arrived carrying a grievance. An assistant when the All Blacks won the inaugural 1987 World Cup, he had succeeded Brian Lochore as head coach in 1988 — over Auckland's preference for John Hart — and after a stumble in 1991 the union had appointed **Hart as joint coach** for that World Cup. Two men, one team; it went as badly as it sounds, and Wyllie resigned afterwards. His first contact with Argentina had been coaching against them at the 1987 tournament and taking the All Blacks on an unbeaten tour there in 1991. He came to Buenos Aires around 1996 as assistant to head coach **José Luis Imhoff** — Guastella's assistant from the 1978 rebuild, the lineage surfacing again.

What Wyllie changed was not tactics but culture, and the change was blunt. The Argentines called their own rugby world *casual*; he made them train with proper kit and keep to timetables, and the detail every one of them repeats is that **ten o'clock meant nine-fifty, and ready**. A team that had produced Hugo Porta was being taught to turn up on time. **Santiago Phelan**, later a Pumas coach himself, called him hard and rustic and utterly demanding, a man who changed how they trained, prepared and *lived* the game; the lock **Alejandro Allub** put it more simply, that Argentine rugby was casual and Wyllie was the one who pushed them out of it. He was harsh-voiced and told hard truths, but the players found a friend under it — on an under-20 tour to Cape Town he shed a tear when a squad holding a young **Felipe Contepomi** and thirteen future internationals gave him a signed jersey. And he wanted nothing to do with the internal politics that had long poisoned Argentine selection, which is much of why they trusted him.

---

The run-up to Lens was a farce. By 1999 the squad had turned on Imhoff; relations between him and Wyllie had soured, with Wyllie the central issue while refusing to fight over it, and the team had gone badly through 1998. The players wanted Imhoff out; he was asked to step aside and refused, then had to accept it. The under-20 coach **Héctor Méndez** was elevated with Wyllie as his deputy — and then, a fortnight before the squad flew to Cardiff for the opener against Wales, Méndez resigned. Wyllie stepped up, flew into Buenos Aires, ran a couple of sessions, and left for the tournament. He was head coach of Los Pumas for exactly five matches in his life, all of them at that World Cup, in charge of a side still mostly amateur.

They lost the opener to Wales, 23–18, close and respectable. Then came **Samoa** at Stradey Park — the side that had knocked them out in 1991 and 1995 — and a dreadful Argentine first half. Wyllie's half-time address, by every account, could not have been broadcast. Argentina won 32–16, then beat Japan 33–12 to finish second in the pool and drop into the quarter-final play-off against **Ireland at Lens**, where they won 28–24 and reached the last eight for the first time. They lost the quarter-final proper to **France, 47–26, in Dublin**, but by then it hardly mattered. The tournament's leading scorer was **Gonzalo Quesada**, who kicked 102 of Argentina's 137 points; the scrum-half **Agustín Pichot** scored two tries, as did the winger **Diego Albanese**.

The Pichot thread compresses the whole era into one relationship. A section of Argentine rugby saw him as a mercurial talent who had gone to Europe to cash in — the old amateur guard's contempt aimed at a twenty-four-year-old — and in 1999, when some argued for dropping him from the squad entirely, Wyllie backed him to the hilt. He kept backing him for the rest of his life, flying from New Zealand to France for Pichot's final match in 2009 and speaking up for him as Pichot climbed into rugby administration and became one of the most powerful figures in the world game. The New Zealander knifed by his own union defended the Argentine branded a mercenary by his.

---

Look at who was in that Lens changing room and it stops being a story about a quarter-final and becomes one about a nursery. **Pichot** would become one of Argentina's greatest captains and then a global administrator; **Contepomi** would be head coach of Los Pumas from 2023; **Quesada**, the tournament's top scorer, head coach of Italy from 2024; the hooker **Mario Ledesma**, Pumas head coach from 2018 to 2022; the flanker **Phelan**, Pumas head coach from 2008 to 2013. Four future international head coaches came out of one Wyllie squad; *The Rugby Paper*'s obituary of him put it plainly, that everything good in modern Pumas rugby flows from that moment at Lens. Van Heerden had given them a method and Porta the proof; Wyllie gave them professionalism — not the contracts, which the UAR still resisted, but the habits. The nine-fifty.

The romance should not hide the structural failure, which was large and shaped the next twenty years. Argentina came late to professionalism, embracing it only in 2010, fifteen years after France, Wales and New Zealand. The union's amateurism was so entrenched that when the IRB tried to broker Argentina into the Tri Nations in 2007 — *after* a third-place World Cup finish — that amateurism, and the absence of any professional structure at home, was reported as one of the biggest obstacles. The gentlemen who had left football rather than touch money were, eighty years on, still holding back their own national team. So the golden generation that would finish third in the world in 2007 was built entirely in exile — scattered across the top clubs of France, England and Ireland, professionalised at other people's expense, assembling a few times a year in sky blue and white to topple the hosts on opening night in Paris.

Which is where **Gen 4** begins.

## Generation 4: The Team That Didn't Exist (2000–2011)

**7 September 2007. Stade de France, Paris.** Eighty thousand people came to watch the hosts open their own World Cup against Argentina, and everyone in the ground knew the script — France ranked third in the world, Argentina the designated opening-night sacrifice for the third tournament running. France dominated the ball, threw it about like the great French sides of memory, had a try chalked off for a forward pass, and camped on the Argentine line through the closing minutes of the half; Yannick Nyanga got closest and dropped it as he reached to ground it. Argentina led 17–9 at the break and held on to win **17–12**, Ignacio Corleto's intercept the only try. It was the second time in history a World Cup host had lost its opening match — and it was no accident.

---

The coach who built it was **Marcelo Loffreda**, appointed in April 2000, and by now a familiar name. He had been one of Guastella's youngsters in the 1978 rebuild, a centre at Carisbrook in 1979 when Argentina first played the All Blacks and at Vélez in 1987 when they beat Australia. The line ran unbroken from a bow-tied Afrikaans schoolmaster in 1964, through Guastella and Porta's side, into the man who would take Argentina to third in the world. He held the job seven and a half years, and inherited a paradox no one had faced before: **a world-class professional team belonging to a country with no professional rugby.**

The UAR still clung to amateurism, and there was no domestic professional structure at all, so every one of Loffreda's best players had gone abroad — Roncero, Hasan, Contepomi, Hernández, Corleto, Fernández Lobbe, Ledesma, Pichot, scattered across the Top 14, the Premiership and the Celtic League, coached and conditioned at other clubs' expense. Loffreda's task was to take thirty men living in four countries, trained by thirty coaches and assembling a few times a year, and beat the best sides on earth. The cost was visible in a single month: in November 2004 the Pumas beat the defending Six Nations champions, France, 24–14 in Marseille, then lost heavily at home to the Springboks weeks later, missing around ten regular starters who had gone back to their European clubs. Two different teams, same country, same month.

The near-misses piled up. At the **2003 World Cup** Argentina lost to **Ireland 16–15** and went out in the pool, made to play four matches in a fortnight where Ireland played the same four across four weeks — a schedule that treated them as a minor nation. On **23 May 2005** in Cardiff, a depleted Pumas side drew **25–25** with a **British & Irish Lions** team warming up for New Zealand. The theme of 2000–2006 was exactly that: good enough to beat almost anyone, and given no competition to do it in. Argentina were the only Tier 1 nation on earth without an annual championship, playing a handful of Tests a year against whoever would have them.

---

The men who carried it are the most beloved group in Argentine rugby's history. **Agustín Pichot**, the scrum-half and captain, the player the amateur guard had wanted dropped in 1999 and Wyllie had defended — long-haired, magnetic, now in the World Rugby Hall of Fame. **Felipe Contepomi**, fly-half and centre and goal-kicker and a qualified medical doctor, a fifteen-year international and fellow Hall of Famer, the young back from that 1997 tour to Cape Town. **Juan Martín Hernández**, *El Mago*, the Magician. **Ignacio Corleto**, one of the fastest men ever to play for Argentina. The props **Rodrigo Roncero** and **Omar Hasan** — Hasan a trained opera singer. **Fernández Lobbe**, **Patricio Albacete**, **Mario Ledesma** in the pack. Every one of them earning his living in another country.

After the opener they kept going: **Georgia 33–3** in Lyon, **Namibia 63–3** in Marseille — the biggest winning margin in Argentina's World Cup history — and **Ireland 30–15** to top the pool unbeaten, then **Scotland 19–13** in the quarter-final back at the Stade de France. Argentina were in a World Cup semi-final, and the run did something at home that is hard to overstate. In the most football-obsessed country on earth — the country of Maradona, of the boy who turned down Boca — the **Superclásico was moved**. Boca against River, the biggest fixture in Argentine sport, was rescheduled so it would not clash with the Pumas' quarter-final. As the only major Spanish-speaking side at the tournament, they carried the support of fans across Spain, Uruguay and Latin America through the whole five-match run.

South Africa ended the run in the semi-final, 37–13 — no near-miss; the Springboks were simply better and went on to win the tournament. So Argentina returned to Paris, to the **Parc des Princes** on 19 October, to play France again for third place. France came out the more fired-up side and led 3–0, then it turned: Contepomi levelled, took a delayed pass from Pichot to force over in the left corner, and after Hernández rattled a drop goal off the post, Hasan emerged from a pile of bodies having grounded the loose ball — 17–3 at half-time, against the run of play. **Federico Martín Aramburú** jinked inside Christophe Dominici in the second half, off a fifty-metre Corleto break; then, a man down after Juan Manuel Leguizamón's yellow card, Argentina scored again anyway, Horacio Agulla putting **Corleto** clear to dive into the corner as he had on opening night. Minutes from time, Contepomi scored his second, stopped, turned to the crowd and **took a bow**. **France 10, Argentina 34** — five tries, a nineteen-point haul for Contepomi, third in the world. Pichot said afterwards they had played for something they had talked about all tournament, for how they *feel* the game, for a sentiment of romanticism. It remains the best result any team from the Americas has achieved at a World Cup, and it matches or betters the best of founding nations Wales, Scotland and Ireland. On the touchline stood Loffreda — Guastella's kid, Porta's centre — coaching his last Argentina match before leaving for Leicester Tigers.

---

And then nothing changed. Argentina finished third in the world and still had no competition to play in. Through 2007 the IRB was reported to be brokering their entry into the Tri Nations, and the biggest obstacle named was, again, the UAR's amateurism and the absence of any professional structure at home; by that August it was clear the tournament could not expand until SANZAR's News Corporation media contract expired in 2010. So the best team in Argentine history went back to scattered Tests while its players went back to Europe. **Santiago Phelan** — the flanker Wyllie had shaped — succeeded Loffreda on 13 March 2008 and kept them third in the rankings, beating Scotland 21–15 in Rosario, but won no Test series under the strain: a draw with Scotland in 2008, a draw with England in 2009, a 2–0 series loss to Scotland in 2010. The golden generation aged in a vacuum, with nothing to play for.

The last act came in New Zealand. At the **2011 World Cup**, in the Wellington rain, Argentina beat **Scotland** to reach the quarter-finals, where the host and eventual champion ended it. A month later, on **23 November 2011**, the announcement finally came: Argentina would join the Rugby Championship. Pichot had retired in 2009 — Wyllie flew from New Zealand to France for his final game — but he did not disappear; he went into administration, climbed, and in 2016 was elected vice-chairman of World Rugby, the player the old guard had wanted dropped in 1999 now helping run the world game and dragging his country to the top table by force of will.

Generation 4 left proof at the highest level — not a heroic draw but a tournament, the hosts beaten twice, the pool topped, third in the world — and it left **legitimacy**, the Rugby Championship and the end of exile. It left another generation of coaches: Phelan, Ledesma, and **Contepomi**, who had bowed to the Parc des Princes crowd and would one day coach Los Pumas himself. And it left an indictment: the side that finished third in the world had, in a real sense, been built by France and England and Ireland — Argentina produced the players, other countries made them professionals, and the union's inherited nineteenth-century amateurism had been the single biggest obstacle to its own national team for the whole decade. That had to end, and in 2010 it did.

**Gen 5** is where Argentina finally builds the thing it should have built in 1996: professionalism at home — the Pampas XV, the Jaguares in Super Rugby, entry to the Rugby Championship and 29 defeats in the first 33 matches, then Durban in 2015 with twenty of the original 1965 Pumas in the stands, and, in November 2020, the All Blacks.

## Generation 5: The Jaguares (2009–2022)

**8 August 2015. Kings Park, Durban.** Argentina beat South Africa 37–25 — a hat-trick from the winger Juan Imhoff, the first time in twenty-two years the Pumas had beaten the Springboks and the first time ever they had done it on South African soil, in full sky-blue-and-white with no flag of convenience. In the stands sat around twenty of the original 1965 Pumas: the men who had flown 46,252 kilometres as amateurs, been coached by a bow-tied schoolmaster their hosts would not employ, been named by a journalist's error, and won at Ellis Park and refused to leave the field. Fifty years on, they watched the debt to South Africa repaid in full. How Argentina got a team good enough to do that — and how quickly it would be taken away again — is the whole of this generation.

---

It began in 2009, when **Agustín Pichot** stopped playing. He had spent his career as the living contradiction of Argentine rugby — gone to Europe because there was nothing at home, branded a mercenary for it, defended by a gruff New Zealander, and then captain of a side that finished third in the world — and he knew better than anyone what was broken. He did not retire into punditry; he went into the machine. That same year, under his influence, the UAR quietly launched a team few outside Argentina noticed, **Pampas XV**, with **Daniel Hourcade** brought home from Europe to run it. Everything in this generation comes from there.

The problem it was built to solve was stark: Argentina joined the **Rugby Championship in 2012** while still having no professional league, because the union's own architecture — the amateur gentlemen's-club inheritance of men who had left football in 1931 — still forbade one. They had the fixtures and not the country. Pampas XV was the workaround: with no league of their own, the UAR simply entered a side in someone else's. From 2010 to 2013 Pampas played in South Africa's **Vodacom Cup**, winning it in 2011 with an eleven-match unbeaten run; when finances forced a withdrawal, they relaunched into the **Pacific Rugby Cup** and won that in 2014 and 2015. A team with no home league, winning other people's leagues on two continents.

The Pumas, meanwhile, walked into the Rugby Championship and got what everyone expected. An early sign of life came fast — a 16–16 draw with the Springboks in only their second match, in 2012 — and then the grind: they lost 29 of their first 33 games. Hourcade left Pampas to take the Pumas in 2013, and slowly the long project showed. In 2014 they beat **Australia 21–17** for a first Rugby Championship win, and in 2015 came the Durban afternoon this generation opened on. That autumn, at the **2015 World Cup**, they reached the semi-finals for a second time, finishing fourth, with a commanding quarter-final win over **Ireland in Cardiff**; the losses were to New Zealand in the opener, Australia in the semi and South Africa in the bronze match. The image everyone in Argentina kept, though, was not a try but **Diego Maradona dancing with the squad in the changing room** after they beat Tonga 45–16 in Leicester. If Gen 4's symbol was football moving the Superclásico out of the Pumas' way, Gen 5's was football's god turning up to celebrate with them.

---

Then, in 2016, the thing itself. SANZAR had sold its Super Rugby broadcast package for 2011–15, so no format change was possible until 2016 — a television contract is genuinely why Argentina had to wait. In early 2014 the expansion from fifteen to eighteen teams was confirmed, an Argentine franchise taking one of the new places, based in Buenos Aires and playing in the South African conference; on 16 December 2015 the name was announced — the **Jaguares**, the jaguar, the animal actually on the UAR crest, the one Carl Kohler had failed to name in 1965. Fifty years after the mistake, Argentina named a team after the right cat. The home ground was **José Amalfitani**, capacity 49,540; the first head coach **Raúl Pérez**, Hourcade's closest assistant, by his own admission stepping into a world he did not know. The point of the whole exercise was the roster: the UAR **brought the players home from Europe**, and for the first time since professionalism arrived in 1995, Argentina's best were paid to play in Argentina. Twenty-one years late, but done.

The Jaguares were poor at first — 13th in 2016, 10th in 2017 — until **Mario Ledesma**, the hooker from the 1999 Lens side, took over and the team began to move: seven straight wins in 2018 and knockout qualification. Ledesma was good enough to be promoted to the Pumas, and in came **Gonzalo Quesada**, the top scorer of the entire 1999 World Cup, back from a long European career as player and coach. Where Ledesma had done the confidence work, Quesada added structure — real competition for places, young players like **Mayco Vivas, Lucas Paulos and Santiago Carreras** jumping straight from the Pumitas into the side, a system that did not miss Nicolás Sánchez when he left, a scrum rebuilt by **Andrés Bordoy** and a transformed lineout. In **2019** they won eleven of their last twelve, took the Brumbies apart 39–7 in the semi-final before a record 31,000 at Amalfitani, and reached the **Super Rugby final**, losing to the **Crusaders 19–3** in Christchurch on 6 July. An Argentine franchise four years old, from a country that had outlawed professionalism within living memory, in the final of the southern hemisphere's premier competition — a decade after the whole thing began as an idea in Pichot's head, playing the Vodacom Cup.

---

And then, on **14 November 2020**, in the strangest year sport has known, at Bankwest Stadium in Sydney, in the middle of a pandemic: **Argentina 25, New Zealand 15** — a first-ever win over the All Blacks, Nicolás Sánchez scoring all twenty-five points, a try, a conversion and six penalties. A hundred and ten years after the first men pulled on the light-blue-and-white, fifty-five after Van Heerden, thirty-five after Porta kicked all 21 at Ferrocarril Oeste and could still only draw — they beat the All Blacks.

The cruelty was that the team behind it was already gone. COVID had broken Super Rugby; the 2020 season was abandoned, travel restrictions forced the competition to regionalise, and the 2021 formats had no place for the Jaguares. On **30 July 2020**, UAR president **Marcelo Rodríguez** announced the team could no longer afford its players and released the entire senior squad and staff. Everything Pichot had started in 2009 was gone in a single announcement, and the players scattered back to Europe — Lavanini, Bosch and the rest into the Top 14 — the exodus the Jaguares had been built to end simply resuming. The South African sides left for the URC, the competition relaunched around Australasia and the Pacific, and Argentina, a finalist in 2019, was not in it. The Jaguares were formally disbanded in **December 2022**, split into two development teams — **Pampas XV** in Buenos Aires and **Dogos XV** in Córdoba — for the new **Super Rugby Americas**, alongside **Peñarol Rugby**, the Uruguayan and Argentine threads meeting at last in one league.

By the **2023 World Cup**, 31 of Michael Cheika's 33-man squad came from foreign clubs. Argentina had built a professional structure, reached a Super Rugby final with it, and watched a virus and a broadcast restructure take it away — back, almost, to the exile of the nineties. Almost, but not quite, because the Jaguares had done something that could not be undone: they had put a whole generation of Argentine players in front of Europe's top clubs on their own terms, and by the mid-2020s there were more Argentines at elite European clubs than ever, partly *because* the Jaguares had existed. The talent export grew richer even as the home structure fell. At home, Argentina remained the weakest side in the Rugby Championship on aggregate — nine wins in fifty-eight matches over eleven years, off the bottom only twice — improving steadily, never quite there.

Generation 5 left the **All Blacks beaten**, and with them every major rugby nation on earth; it left **Durban**, with the 1965 Pumas watching the debt repaid; it left a **Super Rugby final built from nothing in ten years**; it left proof that professionalism at home works, and the bitter proof that outside forces can take it away; and it left **Maradona dancing in a changing room** in Leicester. The generation that finally got the tools, used them beautifully, and had them confiscated.

**Gen 6** is what they built from the rubble: Michael Cheika, the first foreign head coach since Wyllie, and a 2023 semi-final reached with no home league at all; then Contepomi — the man who bowed to the Parc des Princes crowd in 2007 — taking over, beating the All Blacks *in New Zealand*, beating the British & Irish Lions, sweeping all three Rugby Championship rivals in 2024, Argentina around fifth in the world and bidding, through Pichot, to host the World Cup in 2035.

## Generation 6: The Diaspora (2020–2026)

**23 August 2025. Vélez Sarsfield, Buenos Aires.** On the ground where Hugo Porta had beaten Australia in 1987, Argentina beat the All Blacks on home soil for the first time in their history. They had beaten New Zealand in Sydney in 2020, in Christchurch in 2022, in Wellington in 2024 — never in Argentina, in fifteen previous home attempts across a century. With it, the head coach could claim something no Argentine side ever had: a home win over every Tier 1 nation on earth. And the team that did it had no professional league at home at all.

---

That is the paradox this generation begins in. The Jaguares had died in July 2020, and everything Pichot built since 2009 was gone — the players scattered to France and England, no franchise, no domestic competition. When **Michael Cheika** named his 2023 World Cup squad, 31 of the 33 came from foreign clubs; the two exceptions were a sevens specialist and a man then unemployed. Not a system — a **diaspora**. And what Argentina did with it was the most improbable stretch in the whole story.

Cheika — the Australian who had taken the Wallabies to a World Cup final, and the first foreign head coach since Wyllie — took over after Ledesma left in 2022 and steered that diaspora to the **2023 World Cup semi-final**, Argentina's third in five tournaments, Wales beaten in the quarter-final before England ended it in Paris. When he left, the UAR handed the job, in December 2023, to **Felipe Contepomi**: the doctor, the fly-half, the young back from the 1997 tour who had watched Wyllie shed a tear, the man who beat Wales at the Millennium Stadium in 2001 with 25 points and a try, and who had scored two tries in the 2007 bronze final and turned to eighty thousand French supporters to take a bow. Now he was head coach.

One older loop had closed months before he took over. In **April 2023** the UAR launched a new identity and replaced the jaguar on the crest with a **puma** — for fifty-eight years the badge had said *jaguar* while the world said *puma*, ever since Carl Kohler's deadline guess in 1965, and now the animal was changed to match the mistake. They kept the error and corrected the truth, which is what a nation does with a name it has come to love.

Contepomi kept Cheika's foundation and added the New Zealander **Kendrick Lynn**, recruited from the Highlanders as attack coach to bring width to a side always built on its pack, alongside **Andrés Bordoy**, who had rebuilt the Jaguares scrum, and **Juan Martín Fernández Lobbe** from the 2007 team, with the hooker **Julián Montoya** as captain. In his first season, 2024, the Pumas beat France, Australia, South Africa and New Zealand. On **10 August 2024** they won **38–30 in Wellington** — beating the All Blacks in New Zealand — and that year completed the first **Rugby Championship Triple Crown** in their history, beating all three southern giants in one tournament, including a **29–28 win over the world's number-one side, South Africa**, in a blistering Santiago del Estero, Albornoz's late penalty deciding it. They still finished fourth, on three losses; that is the Rugby Championship.

On **20 June 2025**, in Dublin, came another first. The **British & Irish Lions**, assembling and warming up for Australia, had never lost to Argentina — not in 1910, not in 1927 or 1936, not in the 2005 draw at Cardiff. Argentina won **28–24**, scoring three tries and leading 21–10 before the Lions surged ahead, then taking the lead back to win and lift the **1888 Cup**; three players — **Boris Wenger, Bautista Bernasconi, Simón Benítez Cruz** — made their debuts in it. A hundred and fifteen years after the Lions first came to Buenos Aires for the centenary of the May Revolution and won 28–3, at the eighth time of asking, Argentina beat them.

Then Vélez, the afternoon this generation opened on. The 2025 Rugby Championship gave Argentina two home matches against New Zealand for the first time — Córdoba's Estadio Mario Alberto Kempes hosting a Rugby Championship fixture at last, fewer than 1,500 of 57,000 tickets left by midweek, and then Buenos Aires. Afterwards Contepomi called that team **a connecting thread in the great history of Los Pumas** — that he had lived it as a player and tried to live it well as a coach, but that the success belonged to *them*, the Pumas of 2025 — and refused to rank them against the past. In the same match-day squad were **Pablo Matera**, who had passed Agustín Creevy's record against England that July to become the most-capped Puma in history, and the halves pairing of **Gonzalo García and Tomás Albornoz**, the first all-Tucumán combination Argentina has ever fielded — the provincial heartland the Campeonato Argentino began building in the 1940s, now supplying both nine and ten.

---

By mid-2026 the balance sheet was mixed and honest. Argentina did not win the 2025 Rugby Championship — South Africa took it in the final round, beating them **29–27 at Twickenham** on 4 October, a title decider played in London — though Argentina retained the **Puma Trophy**. That was the last Rugby Championship before the **Nations Championship** era and the competition's 2026 hiatus, which is why, in July 2026, Argentina played a Southern Hemisphere Series rather than the old tournament: they lost to **Scotland 38–47** in Córdoba — the most points Scotland had ever scored in Argentina — beat **Wales 35–21** in San Juan, and lost to **England 24–31** in Santiago del Estero, a last-gasp home try ruled out by the TMO, to finish the series with one win from three. They sat around fifth in the world. Contepomi was contracted through the **2027 World Cup in Australia**, where Argentina were drawn with **Canada, Spain and Fiji** in Pool C and widely expected to top it; they had reached the semi-finals in three of the last five World Cups and played in every one since 1987. And **Agustín Pichot** — the player the amateur guard had wanted dropped in 1999 — was at the heart of Argentina's bid to host the World Cup in 2035.

The wound underneath had not closed. Argentina still had no professional domestic league; the Jaguares were gone, and Pampas and Dogos played in **Super Rugby Americas** against Peñarol and the Chileans and Brazilians — a good development competition, not Super Rugby. The best players were again in the Top 14, the Premiership and the URC, and Contepomi managed a squad with no off-season, resting different men at different times because World Rugby had still not enforced a global calendar. And at home the mood was cool: after the extraordinary 2024 season the *Buenos Aires Herald* ran a piece under a headline that cut deep — *Los Pumas just had their best year and are top-tier. Most Argentines are still not impressed* — and when South Africa answered Santiago del Estero a week later with a **48–7 rout** in Nelspruit, the mockery of "historic wins" that never became trophies stood almost alone. The 2007 bronze remained their only piece of top-tier silverware. Football was still the religion.

---

Look back down the length of it: a hybrid game with no agreed rules, played by homesick Englishmen who changed codes four times in two years because the rugby version kept injuring them; an officer who thought playing it might cost him his commission; thirty years before a single criollo team appeared; some 2,500 spectators arrested because a president took a rugby match for a coup; ninety-one years without beating a touring side. Then a bow-tied Afrikaans schoolmaster, escaped from an Italian camp and passed over by his own country, who taught them to treat the ball like a nugget — and a journalist on deadline who looked at a jaguar and wrote *puma*. Then a boy who turned down Boca and scored all twenty-one points at Bloemfontein for a team that officially did not exist. Then a gruff New Zealander who taught them that ten o'clock meant nine-fifty, and a squad of part-timers who beat Ireland at Lens under a coach appointed a fortnight earlier. Then Paris in 2007 — the hosts beaten twice, third in the world, Argentine football moving the Superclásico out of the way. Then Durban, with the 1965 Pumas in the stands; Maradona dancing in a Leicester changing room; a franchise built from nothing that reached a Super Rugby final and was killed by a virus. And then, in 2025, at Vélez, a man who had once bowed to a French crowd watched his country beat the All Blacks at home — a connecting thread in the great history of Los Pumas.

---

That is the six generations of the national story. One thread remains to be pulled: the single place in South America where rugby genuinely escaped its class origins, where sugar mills stood in for cricket lawns and the provincial side burns the capital's flag on the terraces. To see how, we leave Buenos Aires and travel north, to Tucumán.

## The Interior: Tucumán

### The Englishmen in the Sugar Mills (1915–1941)

**5 October 1985.** A quiet Saturday afternoon in Buenos Aires, at the ground of Club San Martín in Sáenz Peña. The Buenos Aires selection — twelve of them Pumas, captained by **Hugo Porta** — were playing the provincial side from Tucumán in the final of the Campeonato Argentino. Buenos Aires had won nineteen consecutive titles; no team from the Interior had ever taken it from them. Tucumán won **13–9**, on a heroic defence and a maul that rolled straight over the top of them, and in the stands roughly fifty Tucumanos, fifty in a Buenos Aires suburb, started screaming and did not stop. Where that afternoon came from is a story that runs in parallel to the national one, and ends up inside it.

---

Everything in Tucumán comes down to one word: *azúcar*, sugar. Tucumán is Argentina's sugar province, and in the early twentieth century that meant sugar mills — British engineers, British managers, British money. The same informal empire that put railway workers in Villa Peñarol and Belgrano put a different set of Britons in the cane fields of the north. Around **1915** a handful of English students who had settled temporarily in San Miguel de Tucumán began playing rugby, and the local records are candid about how it went: they played *ante la incomprensión de los lugareños*, before the bafflement of the locals — the same reaction the Uruguayan spectator had in 1880, watching the scrum and seeing only heads without shoulders and legs without bodies. But the setting was not a gentlemen's cricket club in a leafy suburb. It was industrial: sugar mills, not polo lawns.

Then, for nine years, nothing. The first organised match anyone recorded, in **1924**, was not Tucumán playing at all — it was **CASI**, the River Plate champion, coming north to play a combined Buenos Aires side at the football ground of Atlético Tucumán, with the provincial governor watching. The capital had come to put on an exhibition; Tucumán were spectators at their own first big occasion. In **1935** came the first interprovincial game involving an actual Tucumán team, against Santiago del Estero, again at Atlético's stadium and again with the governor present, won 7–0 by a side playing under the name "Tucumán Rugby." Then it collapsed once more — the enterprise had no continuity, and the game in the province simply stopped. That 1935 "Tucumán Rugby" was not a club, did not survive, and has no institutional link to the modern club of the same name; but two men who played that day, **Isaías Nougués** and **Peter Bernan**, would later wear the green and black. Twenty years, three matches, two false starts.

---

### Building the Thing (1941–1965)

The turn came in **1941**, when **Natación y Gimnasia** — then the model club of the northwest, with the best facilities in the region — created a rugby section. It was the beachhead, the moment rugby stopped being a curiosity in Tucumán and became an institution. Then, in quick succession: on **5 September 1942**, **Tucumán Rugby Club** was founded — and this is the detail that matters, that it was the first club in the region created *as* a rugby club, deliberately, to spread the game, not a cricket club that acquired rugby or a football club that fled professionalism; its founders included **Roberto Alvarado**, **Isaías Nougués** from the ghost 1935 side, and **Freddy Gould**. On **21 September 1943**, students and professors of the engineering faculty at the National University of Tucumán founded **Universitario Rugby Club**. In **1944** came **Cardenales**, and on **29 February 1944** the four clubs founded the **Unión de Rugby del Norte**, which also looked after fledgling clubs in Salta, Jujuy and Santiago del Estero — provinces that later spun off their own unions, leaving the body to become simply the Unión de Rugby de Tucumán. The first **Anual Tucumano** was played that same year, Tucumán Rugby its first champions.

Set beside Buenos Aires, the origin is the whole point. The capital's rugby came from cricket clubs and from football clubs that quit football as vulgar, clubs whose defining schism in 1937 turned on whether women could attend post-match drinks. Tucumán's came from a swimming-and-gymnastics club, a university engineering faculty, and a group of men who built a club from nothing and made friendship its creed. That last is not decoration: when Gould, one of the most enthusiastic founders, had to leave Tucumán for work, he gave an emotional farewell to his friends and coined a phrase the club has carried ever since —

> **"Más que un club, una amistad."**
> *More than a club, a friendship.*

It is still on the club's website. And in this same era the **Campeonato Argentino** began, in **1945** — the annual competition setting the provinces against Buenos Aires, the mechanism that runs through the whole national story. It was the tournament Tucumán had been built to fight. For a long time, they were hammered in it.

---

### The Long Apprenticeship (1966–1984)

Two decades of coming second, and being structurally prevented from coming first. Tucumán reached their first Campeonato Argentino final in **1966**, and lost; runners-up again in **1975**. The deck was stacked: Buenos Aires entered *two* teams, Capital and Provincia, two bites at the title, and the strongest Interior side was Rosario in the 1960s and Mendoza in the 1970s, so Tucumán queued behind everyone. In the eighties they began hammering on the door — runners-up in **1981** in Salta, in **1982** in San Juan — while Buenos Aires kept winning, nineteen consecutive Campeonatos Argentinos, and every year Tucumán came south and lost.

---

### El Primer Naranjazo (1985–1993)

Back, then, to that Saturday in Sáenz Peña — 5 October 1985, the date given as 5 or 6 depending on the source. Tucumán against Buenos Aires, twelve Pumas in the Buenos Aires side, Porta leading them. Tucumán won 13–9. The men who did it were Molina, Le Fort and Hortas; Bunader and De Luca; **Pablo Garretón**, Ricci and Santamarina; Ferro and Merlo; Terán, Gianotti, Carrizo, Soler and Ríos. One of the heroes, **Pedro "Perico" Merlo**, described it afterwards as the brotherhood of warriors going into battle on unequal terms, and said the coaching staff — **Alejandro Petra, Cacho Castillo and Manolo Galindo** — had convinced them of what they were capable of, Petra's last words before kick-off being simply, "Let's go, we are champions." It broke the nineteen-year hegemony, and Tucumán remembers it as *el primer naranjazo*, the first orange blow.

Out of it grew something that had not existed anywhere else in South American rugby. The Tucumán selection are **Los Naranjas**, the Oranges, for the colour of the shirt, and what emerged after 1985 is called *la mística naranja*, the orange mystique. The titles came in a flood — 1985, 1987, 1988, 1989, 1990, 1992, 1993, then a long drought, a return in 2005, and 2010, 2013, 2014: eleven Campeonatos Argentinos and thirteen runners-up finishes, fourteen junior crowns, three Seven de la República titles. Everything about Tucumán rugby's identity dates from that *época dorada*.

And it is loved there in a way the game is loved almost nowhere else in South America. Away from Buenos Aires, where the culture is described even by neutral sources as traditionally refined, Tucumán is a heartland — passionate supporters who **burn the opposition's flag on the terraces**, at a rugby match, in a sport that in Montevideo is played in Carrasco by the alumni of an Irish Catholic private school. The province supplies Argentina with its hardest forwards; its own literature calls it a nest that raises future Pumas, and it has done so since the seventies. **Pablo Garretón**, from that 1985 team, went on to captain Los Pumas at the 1991 World Cup — the only man from the Interior ever to do it. **Daniel Hourcade**, of Universitario Tucumán, took over Pampas XV in 2010, built the professional pathway from nothing and coached Los Pumas from 2013 to 2018. And **Ángel Guastella** — Van Heerden's successor, the man who named Porta captain in 1978 — moved to San Miguel de Tucumán in 2000 and spent his last years as chairman of the provincial union, dying there in 2016. The method a bow-tied Afrikaner brought to Buenos Aires in 1964 lived out its life, in the person of its custodian, in the sugar province.

---

### The Ceiling (1993–2018)

Then the story turns bitter, because Tucumán conquered the provincial game and was never allowed to conquer the club game. When the UAR launched its first **Nacional de Clubes** in 1993, Tucumán Rugby reached the final and lost; they reached it again in 2007 and lost; **Los Tarcos** reached it in 2004 and lost. No Tucumán club has ever won the national club title. And they were squeezed structurally: in **1999** the Anual Tucumano — the provincial championship that had run since 1944, rated one of the best tournaments in the country and a nursery of Pumas — was discontinued, the clubs pushed into the Regional del Noroeste instead, with Tucumán Rugby having won it twenty times and Universitario seventeen. Their own competition, killed off; it returned only sporadically, a revival in 2018, a COVID-year edition in 2021, and then, in 2024, for the union's 80th anniversary, brought back annually for good. The **Campeonato Argentino** itself — the tournament that *was* Tucumán's identity, the stage of the naranjazo, the thing they won eleven times — ran its last edition in 2017 and is now defunct. The competition that made them no longer exists. Meanwhile URBA holds the strong clubs, the money, the Top 12, the professional pathway; the sources name the tension plainly, that Buenos Aires controls the resources while the provincial unions stay poor. The oldest story in Argentina, told in rugby.

---

### The Nine and the Ten (2019–2026)

And so to the ending. Return to the last generation of the national story — **23 August 2025, Vélez Sarsfield**, Argentina beating the All Blacks on home soil for the first time — and look at the half-back pairing that afternoon: **Gonzalo García at nine, Tomás Albornoz at ten**, the first all-Tucumán halves combination Argentina has ever fielded. Eighty-one years after four clubs met in San Miguel to found the Unión de Rugby del Norte; ninety years after a scratch team called Tucumán Rugby beat Santiago del Estero 7–0 in a football stadium and disbanded; a hundred and ten years after a few English students turned up at the sugar mills to play a game the locals could make no sense of — the two men steering Argentina to its greatest home victory were both from the sugar province. By the mid-2020s Tucumán Rugby had 3,000 members, 900 rugby players and 400 in hockey, four pitches, and a main ground holding two thousand, and it still called itself *más que un club, una amistad*.

---

#### What Tucumán Proves

Everywhere else in South America, rugby stayed roughly where the British left it. Montevideo Cricket Club refused to be governed by Uruguayans in 1900, and rugby retreated into Carrasco, the Anglophile suburb, where the Irish Christian Brothers grew it in a private school. Buenos Aires' rugby clubs were football clubs that walked out of football as vulgar, carrying their grounds and their gentlemen's-amateur creed into a sport they then guarded for eighty years. Tucumán is the one place it got away — not by anyone's design, but because of the shape of the thing. It arrived through sugar mills rather than cricket lawns; it was rebuilt not by an Anglo social club but by a public university faculty, a swimming club, and a group of men who founded a club from scratch and made friendship the point of it; and it was forged in a competition whose entire purpose was beating Buenos Aires. A sport cannot stay elite, exclusionary and capital-bound when its defining act is a provincial side beating the capital's twelve Pumas 13–9 while fifty men scream in an away stand.

Which is the whole argument of the book, made in one province: a sport's class character is not inherited from its founders but produced by whom it plays against, and why. Tucumán played against Buenos Aires, and became something Buenos Aires is not — and burns the flags on the terraces to prove it. Same sport, same decade, same empire. Three completely different animals.
