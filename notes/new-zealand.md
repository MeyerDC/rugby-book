## Thesis

France is where rugby became a *region's* property. New Zealand is where it became a *country's* — and
the book's clearest case that a sport carries no class in itself, because here the carrying
institutions were not schools or clubs defined against anybody. They were **provincial unions, country
clubs, freezing works, farm districts and garrison towns**, and a game that ran through all of them at
once could not be used to sort people by class. England invented amateurism to keep a game away from
working men; New Zealand adopted the same laws from the same body and produced a game working men
already owned. **Same rules, opposite result — because the institutions underneath them were
different.**

But that settles the class question in a paragraph, and a chapter needs an argument that runs.

### ⭐ THE SPINE (author decision, August 2026): **WHO OWNS THE NATIONAL GAME**

Because rugby in New Zealand belongs to everybody, the recurring fight is over **who gets to speak
for it** — and that fight is live in every single generation, from before there was a union to the
sale of the commercial rights.

- **Before 1892** there is no owner at all: clubs and provinces, and a national side that toured
  Australia without a national body behind it.
- **1888.** The team that invents the imagery — black jersey, silver fern, a haka — is a **private
  commercial venture**, not a national selection. The union that will claim that imagery does not yet
  exist. It is founded in **1892** and spends a decade persuading provinces to be governed.
- **1905/1907.** The Originals make the union rich and the players ask what their share is;
  **Baskerville's professionals answer by taking the game to the market**, and New Zealand gets its own
  small version of 1895 — see `07-england.md`.
- **1919–1986. The most expensive thing the union ever bought.** To keep the fixture it valued above
  all others, the NZRFU was willing to **spend its own Māori players** — 1919, 1928, 1949, 1960 —
  and, in 1981, to set its ownership of the game against the country's own view of it. Race is the
  great thread of Gens 3–5 and it is *inside* the spine, not beside it: the colour bar is the price the
  owner paid, and 1981 is the year the owner was told the game was not his to trade.
- **1995.** The players are nearly bought out from under the union wholesale by the **World Rugby
  Corporation**; New Zealand answers by inventing the most complete ownership model in the sport —
  centrally contracted players, the union as employer.
- **2008–2026.** That model wins everything, and then the money outgrows it: Silver Lake buys a slice
  of the commercial rights, and the Top 14 and Japan buy the players themselves.

**One sentence to aim at:** *New Zealand made rugby a national possession, and has been arguing ever
since about who is holding it.*

**Two supporting arguments (subordinate, not the spine):**
- **League as the internal counterpoint.** Rugby league in New Zealand — from Baskerville's 1907 All
  Golds forward — did not become a rival national code as it did in Australia (see ch. 13). It became
  a *regional and communal* game: West Coast, and above all **South Auckland and Māori/Pasifika
  Auckland**. The class fault England opened in 1895 exists in New Zealand too; it is just much
  smaller, and it maps onto neighbourhood and ethnicity rather than onto class alone. Test the claim
  before printing it.
- **The modern private-school pipeline, as the thesis under strain.** If the argument is "a national
  game with almost no class character," the honest chapter checks whether that is still true in 2026 —
  First XV recruitment, scholarships, Auckland Grammar/Hamilton Boys'/Christchurch Boys', and where
  All Blacks debutants actually come from. **Do not assert this either way without evidence.**

## PROPOSED GENERATION MAP (Step 0 — for author approval before any prose)

**✅ AUTHOR DECISIONS (August 2026):** the spine is **ownership of the national game** (above), and
**Gen 7 was SPLIT at 2019**. New Zealand therefore runs **Gen 0–8 — nine generations, the longest
spine in the book**, one past South Africa, England, Wales, Scotland and France. The justification is
that a single 2008–2026 generation would have had to hold two opposite stories at once: the most
dominant decade any team has had, and the decade the money took the game away from the union that
owned it. The divider is a real event — **Yokohama, 26 October 2019**, the semi-final lost to England,
after which Steve Hansen's eight-year tenure ended.

Every boundary below is an event, not a round number.

- **Gen 0 — The Game Monro Brought Home (1870–1888).** Charles Monro brings the rules back from
  Christ's College, Finchley; the **first match at Nelson, 14 May 1870**; the game moves out of the
  colleges into provincial clubs and unions within a decade; the first representative New Zealand team
  tours New South Wales (1884). *Boundary:* the Native team sails, 1888.
- **Gen 1 — The Natives (1888–1902).** The privately organised, largely Māori **New Zealand Native
  team** plays an enormous fixture list through Britain in 1888–89 — black jersey, silver fern, a haka
  — inventing the imagery of the national side before the national union existed. The **NZRFU is
  founded (1892)** and spends a decade absorbing provinces that did not want to be governed. *Boundary:*
  the first official Test, 1903.
- **Gen 2 — The Originals and the All Golds (1903–1919).** First Tests; **Dave Gallaher's 1905
  Originals**, thirty-odd matches and one defeat — **Wales 3–0, Cardiff, 16 December 1905**, and Bob
  Deans's disallowed try; the name "All Blacks" arrives on that tour. Then **Albert Baskerville's 1907
  professionals**, rugby league's arrival, and the country's own small 1895. War; Gallaher killed at
  Passchendaele; the **1919 King's Cup Army team and the two men left behind**. *Boundary:* 1919 sets
  the colour bar; Gen 3 lives with it.
- **Gen 3 — The Invincibles and the Colour Bar (1920–1948).** The **1921 Springbok tour** and the
  leaked telegram at Napier; the **1924–25 Invincibles**, unbeaten, George Nepia playing every match;
  **1928 — the first tour to South Africa without Māori players**; the **1937 Springboks**, still the
  only South African side to win a series in New Zealand. *Boundary:* 1949.
- **Gen 4 — No Maoris, No Tour (1949–1969).** 1949: the second Māori-less tour, whitewashed in South
  Africa on the same days a second-string side lost at home. The **1956 series win** over the
  Springboks; **1959** and Don Clarke's six penalties; the **1960 tour and the petition** that
  organised the objection into a national campaign; 1967. *Boundary:* the 1970 tour, made under South
  Africa's "honorary whites" formula.
- **Gen 5 — The Tour (1970–1986).** Māori and Pasifika players finally selected for South Africa, and
  what that cost to accept; **1973 cancelled** by the Kirk government; **1976**, arriving a fortnight
  after Soweto, and the Montreal boycott; **1981 — the 56 days**, Hamilton, the flour bomb, Eden Park;
  the **1985 injunction**; the **1986 Cavaliers**. *Boundary:* the 1987 World Cup.
- **Gen 6 — Open (1987–2007).** Hosting and winning the **first World Cup, 1987**; 1991; **Lomu and
  1995**, and the near-loss of the whole player base to the World Rugby Corporation; professionalism,
  Super rugby, the Tri Nations, the 1996 series win in South Africa; then four tournaments in a row
  that ended early. *Boundary:* Cardiff, the 2007 quarter-final.
- **Gen 7 — The Best Team in the World (2008–2019).** The rebuild out of Cardiff; **2011 and 2015**,
  the first side to retain the World Cup; the record winning run; Super Rugby's expansion and the
  central-contract model working exactly as designed. *Boundary:* **Yokohama, 26 October 2019** — the
  semi-final lost to England, and the end of the Hansen era.
- **Gen 8 — The Bill (2019–2026).** The money arrives faster than the union can hold it: **Silver
  Lake** and the sale of a share of the commercial rights, the salary gap, the drain to Japan and
  France; the **Black Ferns' home World Cup final at Eden Park (2022)** as the one unambiguously
  rising thing; **2023**; a dated **mid-2026** close pointing at RWC 2027 in Australia.

**⚠️ Everything above is an outline, not a verified fact base.** Dates, scores, fixture counts, the
NZRFU's founding year and the Nelson date are all to be verified in the per-generation research pass
before they enter prose.

## GEN 0 — verified by hand (August 2026)

**Primary source, opened and read in full: the New Zealand Rugby Museum's own 150th-anniversary
account.** [1870 – First Rugby Game](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/history-of-nz-rugby/1870-first-rugby-game).
Every quote below is exact page text.

### The first match — date, ground, teams

- **"The historic game took place at the Botanical Gardens on Saturday, May 14, 1870"** — Nelson
  Football Club v **Nelson College**. The date and ground are now safe to assert.
- The club existed before the code did: **"A 'Football Club' was formed in 1868 but there is no record
  of a game against opposition. In 1869 the club was reformed as 'Nelson Football Club' and played
  against Nelson College, the style of football being a hybrid mix of soccer and Victorian Rules."**
  ⭐ **Thread this to France Gen 0.** New Zealand's first club, like Le Havre, played a **hybrid** before
  it played rugby — "la combination" on the other side of the world. Two chapters, same finding: the
  origin story of a national game is usually a committee choosing between codes, not a founding.
- **"Early in 1870 Monro returned from England where he played the rugby rules at Christ's College. He
  became a member of the Nelson club and suggested to the players they try the rugby rules."**
- The College side had to be taught the game first: Monro **"approached the headmaster at Nelson
  College, Frank Simmons, a former pupil of Rugby School, offering to teach the pupils the rules and
  arrange a game against the club."**
- **First inter-district match: Petone, 12 September 1870.** Tennent wrote to Monro, then in Wellington;
  Monro **"rounded up some local footballers, taught them the rugby rules and the Nelson team crossed
  Cook Strait to play Wellington at Petone on September 12"** — Nelson won — **"the game being the
  first played between two districts, a forerunner to inter-provincial rugby."**

### ⭐ THE ANTI-MYTH NOTE — the museum makes it itself

- **"Monro has been credited as the founder of rugby in New Zealand, but it is more appropriate to
  credit him for introducing the game and as co-founder."** — *The keeper of the relics volunteering
  that the founder story is too simple.* Use this the way the book uses Webb Ellis (`07-england.md`),
  Le Havre (`11-france.md`) and the 1894 Iquique tradition (`03-chile.md`): **New Zealand is the one
  origin myth in the book that its own custodians have already deflated.** Do not build a cold open on
  a lone-founder scene; build it on the match.

### ⭐⭐ THE GEN 0 ARGUMENT: NOBODY OWNED IT — IT TRAVELLED IN PEOPLE'S LUGGAGE

The three men who started it were **a jeweller, a bank clerk and a politician's son**, and the game
spread across the lower North Island because two of them **moved towns for work**. This is the
ownership spine's opening position — there is no union, no school system and no governing body, only
private men carrying a game in their luggage.

- **Alfred Drew** — club **chairman and captain**, and **"not only the first to captain a rugby club,
  he was also the first point-scorer, he kicking a goal in the first game played in New Zealand"**
  (and first scorer again at Petone). **"Drew was a son of a jeweller and watchmaker"**; he attended
  Nelson College, **"then joined the family business."** Then the business moved, and the game went
  with it: Wanganui **1871**, where **"the following year he co-founded rugby in the town and captained
  the club team"**; **"the rugby game spread from Wanganui into Manawatu in 1876"**; New Plymouth
  **1874**, and **"before the 1874 winter had passed he had established rugby in the town and captained
  the Taranaki club."** The museum's own summary: **"Within the period of five years he was
  instrumental in spreading the rugby game from Nelson, to Wellington, Wanganui and Taranaki."**
- **Robert Tennent** — the club's **secretary-treasurer** and a player, **captain of Nelson
  representative teams 1872–73**; educated in England; **joined the Bank of NSW as a 16-year-old clerk
  in 1865**. **"Robert gained promotion in 1874 to manage a new branch in Patea"** — and was **"a
  founding member of Patea Football Club."** *A bank transfer founded a rugby club.*
- **Charles John Monro** — **"attended Nelson College 1863-65 before being sent to London for further
  education at Christ's College where he was introduced to rugby. The 19-year-old returned home early
  in 1870."** Played for Nelson to 1875. Father: **"politician Sir David Monro"**, died 1877. Later a
  land speculator and flax investor at Palmerston North; founding president of the Manawatu Golf Club,
  1895; **"in 1904 he took the train to Wellington and watched the first rugby international when New
  Zealand played Great Britain"**, and returned to Athletic Park in **1930, aged 79**, as a guest of
  the NZ Rugby Union. *He lived long enough to watch the thing he introduced become national property
  — and to be invited to it as a guest.* Strong Gen 0 closing image.

### The jersey, and who chose it — [Black, Black, Black](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/history-of-nz-rugby/black-black-black) (NZ Rugby Museum)

⚠️ *Gen 1 material, banked here because it was verified in the same pass.*

- ⭐⭐ **The union voted to adopt what a private venture had already made famous.** At the **first Annual
  General Meeting of the NZRFU, 27 April 1893**: **"It was resolved that the New Zealand Representative
  colours should be Black Jersey with Silver Fernleaf, Black Cap with Silver Monogram, White
  Knickerbockers and Black Stockings, on the motion of Mr Ellison (later that season to captain the
  first official New Zealand side), seconded by Mr King."** — **Thomas Ellison**, one of the **1888–89
  Natives**, moved the motion. The national jersey was proposed to the new union by a Māori player who
  had worn it on a **privately promoted** tour. **This is the ownership spine's founding image and it is
  quoted verbatim from the union's own minutes.**
- The museum keeps the reason open, and so should the chapter: **"Why Ellison opted for a black jersey
  remains one of the intriguing questions in New Zealand rugby history."** Two explanations, both
  hedged in the source — the Māori proverbs *"Mate atu he toa ara mai he toa"* / *"Mate atu he
  tetakura ara mai he tetakura"* ("When one warrior dies another arises" / "When one fern dies another
  arises"), given as **"the most likely explanation … we have heard … from a member of the Ellison
  family"**; and the pragmatic one — the Natives **"were to tour Britain, playing England (white),
  Wales (red) and Ireland (green). Having black jerseys avoided a colour clash."** **Do not assert
  either.**
- **The 1884 team was not black.** The museum holds **"a handstitched golden fern frond insignia from a
  jersey worn by Harry Roberts as part of New Zealand's first internationally touring rugby team in
  1884"** ([Four Jerseys of New Zealand Rugby](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/history-of-nz-rugby/four-jerseys-of-new-zealand-rugby)).
  **Golden** fern, 1884 — the silver fern on black is 1893, and the museum calls the **1905 Originals**
  **"the first true All Blacks team"**.
- Kit chronology for later gens: **white knickerbockers → black shorts in 1901** — **"No one knew it at
  the time but the change to black shorts was hugely significant, for it created the situation from
  which the 'All Black' story developed."** Same page confirms the **1924–25 "Invincibles"** as
  **"undefeated in its tour of the UK, Ireland, France and Canada."**

### The spread, 1873–1875 — and the class argument, documented, in 1875

From the Museum's **"On This Day"** archive, which reproduces **A. C. Swan, *The History of New Zealand
Rugby Football Vol. 1 1870–1945* (A. H. & A. W. Reed, 1948)** and contemporary newspaper text at
length. **This is how the chapter gets 19th-century primary quotation without Papers Past.**

- **Auckland, 3 May 1873** — [May 3 – 1873 Auckland Rugby early days](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/may-on-this-day/may-3-1873-auckland-rugby-early-days).
  A "scratch" first game, then **"Thirteen Colonials" v "Eight Outsiders"**, uneven sides being no
  obstacle. *The Daily Southern Cross*, Monday 5 May 1873: **"No goal was obtained by either side, but
  one 'touch down' was scored to the Colonials, who were slightly more numerous than their opponents.
  The play on both sides was good, and the new rules seem to have been well studied by the players."**
  That season Auckland also played **"Civilians" v "Volunteers"** (24 May), **"Colonials" v "The
  World"** (14 June), and a **"Heavy-Weights" v "Light-Weights"** match (5 July) — **"Players weighing
  over 10 st. 10 lbs. were classed 'Heavy-Weights.'"** *(Swan, p. 13.)* The early game sorted men by
  **weight and by whether they were born here** — never by class.
- ⭐⭐ **Greymouth, 1 May 1875 — "Banks and Lawyers" versus "All Corners."**
  [May 1 – 1875 start of Rugby on West Coast](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/may-on-this-day/may-1-1875-start-of-rugby-on-west-coast).
  **"The new Rugby game was introduced to other parts of New Zealand in 1875, Otago, Canterbury,
  Hawkes Bay, Marlborough and West Coast."** The Greymouth club was formed at a meeting at **Gilmer's
  Hotel**, advertised in *The Grey River Argus* of Friday 9 April 1875: **"A meeting of those
  interested in the formation of a Football Club for the approaching season will be held at Gilmer's
  Hotel, on Saturday, at 8 p.m."** The opening day, from the *Argus* of Monday 3 May: **"The day was so
  bright and pleasant that it was difficult to believe one's self in the Southern Hemisphere… Sides
  were chosen by Mr. Newton and Mr. Ahearn… The best of feeling was manifested throughout, everyone
  taking his 'spills' in good part."** And then the fixture list: **"Matches played by the members
  included a 'Banks and Lawyers' versus 'All Corners' game, which extended over two days, and a
  contest with a team from the local Fire Brigade."**
  **THIS IS THE CHAPTER'S CLASS ARGUMENT IN A SINGLE 1875 FIXTURE.** In England in 1875 "Banks and
  Lawyers versus All Corners" describes the social order the RFU was about to write into its bylaws
  (`07-england.md`); on the West Coast goldfields it describes **a game, played over two days, for
  fun, against the fire brigade.** Same code, same decade, same empire. Also note the setting: the
  **West Coast** is coal and gold country — the nearest thing New Zealand has to a coalfield, and
  later a rugby league stronghold. Thread to the **Welsh coalfield thesis** (`08-wales.md`) and pick it
  up again in Gen 2.

### ⭐⭐ 1882 — THE FIRST INVITATION ARRIVES, ADDRESSED TO A PROVINCE

[September 9 – 1882 Rugby Tours to NZ begin](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/september-on-this-day/september-9-1882-rugby-tours-to-nz-begin).
**The single best Gen 0 illustration of the spine, and it is documented.** Australia's **Southern Rugby
Union** (renamed the New South Wales RFU in 1892) wanted to invite *New Zealand* to tour in 1882 — and
had nowhere to send the letter, so it sent it to a province:

> **"This invitation was sent to the Wellington Rugby Union, whose secretary, Arthur Bate, had trouble
> in interesting the Canterbury and Otago unions in the proposal. Bate then wrote to Sydney advising of
> his lack of progress and suggested that the New South Welshmen might instead prefer to visit New
> Zealand."**

*The first international invitation in New Zealand's history failed because no one could speak for the
country.* The reply was the tour running the other way: NSW sailed from Sydney on **Thursday, 31 August
1882**, sixteen men including **player-manager Ted Raper**, who also captained, and a secretary,
**R. Thallon**, one of the forwards — six from the University club, three each from Wallaroo, Redfern
and Balmain, one from St Leonard's. A **seven-match** visit, opening **9 September** against Auckland
Provincial Clubs at the **Auckland Domain**, played over **four half-hour spells**; scoring was **two
points for a try, three for a conversion, four for any other goal**. Their ship **went aground coming
up the harbour**. NSW had played its own first-ever match against Queensland on **12 August 1882**.

### ⭐⭐ 1884 — TWO PRIVATE MEN ARRANGE A NATIONAL TEAM

[May 22 – 1884 First match by a New Zealand rep side](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/may-on-this-day/may-22-1884-first-match-by-a-new-zealand-rep-side).

> **"With no national rugby body Dunedin businessman Samuel Sleigh and Christchurch barrister William
> Millton (also a leading player) took upon themselves the arranging of the tour."**

- Selection gave **"more or less equal representation from the four provincial unions then in existence
  (Auckland, Wellington, Canterbury and Otago)"** — and **"a number of selected players withdrew,
  mostly through work requirements."** A team of **19** made the **eight-match** tour. **Sleigh
  manager, Millton captain.**
- ⭐ **The assembly of the team is the class argument as farce, and it is all in the source.**
  **"Warbrick missed the steamer taking the Auckland players to Wellington and had to take a later
  vessel direct to Sydney."** And: **"The Invercargill player James O'Donnell was arrested on a
  fugitive warrant at Clinton as he made his way to join the team in Wellington. He was returned to
  Invercargill where a number of local tradespeople to whom he was indebted had taken out the warrant
  after learning that O'Donnell was unlikely to return from Australia. However, when O'Donnell came up
  in court none of his creditors appeared so he regained his freedom and was able to continue his
  journey to Wellington."** *A national representative who had to be released by a court, and a squad
  depleted because men could not get leave from work.* Set this beside the RFU's 1886 laws.
- **That Warbrick is Joseph Warbrick** — who in 1888 organises the Natives. The handoff into Gen 1 is a
  man who missed the boat in 1884.
- **First match by a New Zealand representative side: 22 May 1884, Newtown Park, Wellington** — v a
  Wellington XV, postponed from Wednesday 21 May **"owing to the boisterous weather and greasy nature
  of the ground"**, then played on a sloppy pitch in **two 25-minute spells**. **New Zealand 9–0**:
  Ryan potted a goal after ten minutes, **Roberts** scored before halftime, Ryan converted; no second-
  half scoring. **"Taiaroa, Ryan, Allan and Roberts were considered by the press to have been most
  prominent."** Two of the touring party, **Ned Davy and Edward Millton, played for the local side.**
  Afterwards the team **"were escorted to the wharf by members of the rugby fraternity to join the
  steamer Hauroto, which had been held until the completion of the match to allow the team to
  embark."** ⭐ Fine Gen 0 closing image: a steamer held at the wharf for a football match.
- **Taiaroa** is Māori and prominent in the very first New Zealand representative side — nine years
  before Ellison moves the black-jersey motion, and four before the Natives.

### ⭐⭐⭐ 1892 — THE UNION IS FOUNDED, AND TWO PROVINCES WALK OUT OF THE ROOM

[April 16 – 1892 NZ Rugby Football Union established](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/april-on-this-day/april-16-1892-nz-rugby-football-union-established),
reproducing **the official minutes**. *Gen 1 material, banked here because it closes the Gen 0 argument
— and it is the best documented "who owns the game" scene in the chapter.*

- **E. D. Hoben, secretary of the Hawke's Bay union**, toured the country in **1891** promoting the
  idea; a Wellington meeting that November drew up a constitution for the provinces to examine.
- **The inaugural meeting: 16 April 1892, the Club Hotel, Wellington, 7.30 p.m.** Present:
  **G. F. C. Campbell** (Wellington, elected chairman), **H. Haliday** (Auckland), **Hoben** (Hawke's
  Bay), **W. D. Milne** (Otago), **T. S. Marshall** (Canterbury), **G. Newth** (Manawatu), **B.
  Ginders** (Wairarapa), **T. S. Ronaldson** (Taranaki).
- **The three strongest southern unions opposed it.** **"Otago Union only was opposed to a central
  Union, but when the time came for the second meeting of delegates it was noted that Canterbury and
  Southland Unions, too, had joined in the opposition to a change of control."** Southland wrote that
  **"owing to the distance from the seat of the Union they would rarely be represented at the meetings
  except by proxy."**
- The chairman's pitch was **authority, not glory**: **"There was certainly need for a supreme football
  Court of Appeal in the Colony, and a body sufficiently strong to settle any disputes which might
  arise."**
- **And then the walkout, in the minutes:** the motion that a New Zealand Rugby Union should be formed
  was **"carried, Messrs. Milne and Marshall alone dissenting. These gentlemen then withdrew, and the
  delegates resolved themselves into the first meeting of THE NEW ZEALAND UNION."**
  **Otago and Canterbury left the room, and the country's governing body was constituted in their
  absence.** New Zealand's national game was founded *over the objection of two of its strongest
  provinces* — and thirteen months later the same body adopted a Māori touring side's black jersey on
  Ellison's motion. **Gen 1 opens here.**

## GEN 1 — verified by hand (August 2026)

All from the NZ Rugby Museum's **"On This Day"** archive, which for the Natives reproduces long
extracts from **Greg Ryan, *Forerunners of the All Blacks* (Canterbury University Press, 1993)** —
the scholarly monograph on the tour — plus contemporary newspaper text. **Cite the Museum pages;
name Ryan in the prose as the source of the account.**

### The scale of the thing

- **"The 'Natives' tour of over 100 rugby matches and eight games of Victorian Rules in Great Britain,
  Australia and New Zealand occupied fourteen months, from June 1888 to August 1889, the most
  remarkable tour in rugby history."** ([June 23 – 1888 NZ 'Natives' tour begins](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/june-on-this-day/june-23-1888-nz-natives-tour-begins))
- **First match: 23 June 1888, Napier, v Hawke's Bay**, before the party had even fully assembled.
  **Nine matches in New Zealand and two in Melbourne** before they reached England on **27 September
  1888**. **"In all, fifty-two matches were originally arranged in England, Ireland and Wales."**
- ⭐ **They played rugby at Suez.** *"They probably made history in Egypt, playing a scratch game of
  rugby while the ship was waiting at Suez, very likely the first rugby played in that country."*
  Forward **Teo Rene** hurt his foot bathing in the canal and **missed the first 11 games in England**.
- **Scotland: one match only, at Hawick on 22 November**, *"owing to friction between the English and
  Scottish unions"*; later Scottish invitations declined for lack of space in the itinerary.

### ⭐⭐ THE JERSEY EXISTED BEFORE THE UNION DID — CONTEMPORARY PROOF

The *Daily Telegraph* (Napier) report of the **first match, 23 June 1888**, reproduced in full:
**"The local men were resplendent in black and white bars, while the Maoris wore black with a silver
fern tree on their breasts."** — **Black and the silver fern, in a newspaper, in June 1888: four years
and ten months before the NZRFU voted to adopt them on Ellison's motion (27 April 1893).** The union
did not design the national identity. It ratified a private venture's uniform.

- At Richmond on **3 October 1888** (v Surrey, the British opener): **"On taking the field, they became
  both the first team from New Zealand to perform a haka and the first to play in a black uniform."**
  The badge was not yet the plain fern — it bore **goal posts, a ball, a fern leaf and the motto "Play
  up New Zealand"**. British press: **"They are dressed in black knickerbockers and jerseys, which in
  the case of the Maoris, with their dusky hue of face and hands, gives them a rather sombre aspect."**
  **Goldsmith** scored the first try in Britain, **Ellison** the second, **McCausland** converted.
  Crowd **"variously estimated at 5,000 and 50,000"**; Ryan quotes a report that **"nearly all of the
  members of the Australian cricket team put in an appearance."** Referee that day: **George Rowland
  Hill, secretary of the Rugby Football Union** — relations **"amicable"**. Remember that name.
- The **1884** side's fern had been **gold** (see Gen 0). The black jersey is the Natives'.
- ⚠️ **One internal tension in the sources — the draft deliberately steps around it.** The Surrey page
  says that at Richmond the Natives **"became both the first team from New Zealand to perform a haka
  and the first to play in a black uniform"**, but the Napier report of **23 June 1888** already has
  them in **black with a silver fern**, three months earlier. Probably Ryan means the first to do
  either *in Britain*. **The draft asserts only the haka claim** ("the first ever seen in Britain from
  a side representing this country") and sources the black jersey to the Napier report instead. Do not
  "restore" the stronger claim.

### ⭐ LANSDOWNE ROAD, 1 DECEMBER 1888 — IRELAND 4, NATIVES 13

([December 1 – 1888 Test win for 'Natives' in Dublin](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/december-on-this-day/december-1-1888-test-win-for-natives-in-dublin), Ryan pp. 71–75.)
They crossed on **30 November** having played **thirteen matches in four weeks**. Ireland led **3–0** at
half-time (Walpole's kicking → **Waites**, converted), then **Woods** scored a second. Then the
collapse: **Keogh two tries, one from deep inside his own half**; **Williams and Ellison** combining
for a third "which swept the length of the field"; **Elliot** a fourth; **Maynard** a fifth from long
range; **McCausland four conversions**. Irish press refused to credit it — **"seldom has an Irish
international combination shown to less advantage"** — but Ryan notes **Ireland beat Wales that same
season**. ⭐ **Cross-reference `10-ireland.md`.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ BLACKHEATH, 16 FEBRUARY 1889 — THE SET-PIECE OF THE GENERATION

([February 16 – 1889 NZ 'Natives' beaten by England](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/february-on-this-day/february-16-1889-nz-natives-beaten-by-england), Ryan.)
**England won by a goal and four tries to nil — 7–0** under the scoring of the day. Contemporary wire
report: **"fully 9,000 spectators."**

- ⭐ **WHY THE MATCH HAPPENED AT ALL — and it is an `07-england.md` story.** England was boycotted by
  the other three home unions over the International Board: after a disputed 1884 try Scotland wanted
  an arbitration body, **England demanded a majority of the votes** on the basis that it had the most
  clubs, refused to attend IB meetings in 1886 and 1887, and the others refused to play it. **"This
  was to be the only international match played by England in 1888 and 1889. Indeed, some felt that
  the loss of their traditional opponents was the only reason England agreed to play against the Native
  team."** England joined only in **1890**, after arbitration by the **president of the Football
  Association, Major Marinden**; the RFU took **half the seats** on a board needing a **three-quarter
  majority**. *The Natives got their England match because England had nobody else to play.*
- **The referee was the RFU's own secretary, Rowland Hill.** Late in the first half he awarded two
  tries to **Bedford** when **Billy Warbrick** and then **Harry Lee** claimed to have touched the ball
  dead in goal.
- **⭐ THE STODDART INCIDENT, in Ellison's own words** (from his book *The Art of Rugby Football*):
  > **"I lured him into my arms by applying the feign dodge. By a quick wriggle, however, he escaped
  > but left a portion of his knickers in my possession. He dashed along and the crowd roared; then
  > suddenly discovering what was the matter he stopped, threw down the ball, and in an instant we had
  > the vulgar gaze shut off by forming a ring around him."**
  With the Natives encircling **Andrew Stoddart** and believing he had called dead ball, **Frank
  Evershed** picked up the ball and scored in the corner despite a tackle by **Madigan** — and then, as
  the Natives argued with Hill, **Evershed re-gathered the ball and replaced it under the posts** for
  **Sutcliffe** to convert. When Hill gave it, **Williams, Taiaroa and Sherry Wynyard walked off the
  field**. **Hill restarted the match without them**; **Scott** took several minutes to persuade them
  back. Sutcliffe scored again late. Ryan's verdict on the evidence: **"events do not become any
  clearer, although the contrast between Ellison's detailed account and the vagueness of the English
  press reports may offer some indication"**, and at least one prominent RFU member said England might
  have **"given way"** over Evershed's try.
- **⭐⭐⭐ ELLISON NAMES THE BOOK'S THESIS, IN 1889.** His complaint was not the decisions:
  > **"I may add that gross as these errors were, they were insignificant when compared with another
  > that Mr Hill committed at the outset of the game, viz, refereeing at all in that game; he being the
  > most important official of the English Rugby Union [sic] and the father of the team pitted against
  > us."**
  *A Māori player from a privately promoted colonial team, objecting in print that the man who owned
  the game had refereed it.* **This is the chapter's spine spoken aloud, and it is the single best
  quotation in the generation.**
- **⭐⭐ AND THEN THE RFU DICTATED THE APOLOGY.** The Union **"immediately demanded an apology"**.
  **McCausland**, captaining in place of the injured Joe Warbrick, wired from **Cambridge on 20
  February**: **"As captain of the New Zealand team I beg to apologise to the Rugby Union committee for
  the insults offered by my team to their officials on the field of play on Saturday last, and beg on
  behalf of my team to express their regret for their behaviour on that occasion."** — **It was his
  second attempt.** *"His first had been rejected by the Union who insisted that unless he provided
  another, at Hill's dictation, the tour would effectively be ended, because players affiliated to the
  Rugby Football Union would be barred from playing against the touring team."*
  **The RFU wrote the words, and enforced them with the only weapon that matters: control of who is
  allowed to play whom.** Six years before it used the identical weapon on the twenty-two northern
  clubs (`07-england.md`).

### ⭐ THE LAWS HAD NO OWNER EITHER — Sydney, 15 June 1889

([June 15 – 1889 NZ 'Natives' beat New South Wales](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/june-on-this-day/june-15-1889-nz-natives-beat-new-south-wales).)
Homeward, in **May and early June 1889**, they played **eight Victorian Rules matches (winning three)
and three rugby matches (all won)** around Melbourne — *a rugby team paying its way by switching codes*
— then beat **New South Wales 12–9** at the **Sydney Cricket Ground** before **"fully 10,000
persons"**. Tries to **Madigan**, **Elliott** (from a Taiaroa break), **Ellison** and **Keogh**.
And then the contemporary report's closing note, which is a gift:
> **"[Note. —Under the scoring points as observed in New Zealand the New South Wales team would have
> won by 6 points to 4.]"**
**The same match had two different winners depending on whose points table you used.** In 1889 nobody
owned the laws either.

### The Ranfurly Shield — the provinces' revenge, and the Gen 2 cold open

[August 6 – 1904 the first Ranfurly Shield challenge](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/august-on-this-day/august-6-1904-the-first-ranfurly-shield-challenge),
quoting the *New Zealand Herald*. **1901:** the **Earl of Ranfurly**, Governor and NZRFU patron, offers
a trophy. **1902 AGM:** the Union makes it a **challenge** trophy, awards it to **Auckland** (best
provincial record), and rules that **challenges are played at the holder's home ground**. Auckland's
1903 fixtures were all away, so the **first challenge was 6 August 1904**, Wellington at **Alexandra
Park, Epsom**. **Wellington 6–3.** ⭐ **The spine:** a national body converts a governor's gift into an
object that *lives with a province* and must be taken on its own ground. The provinces lost the 1892
argument about authority and won the argument about what the game feels like.
Herald colour, all quotable: every electric car pressed into service and still insufficient; seating
for **"close on 3,600 persons"** gone by 2.20; stands improvised from **"a discarded shop counter and a
dray"**; a man arriving with **"a cartload of roughly made stools… retailed out at 6d each and sold as
freely as hot-cross buns on Good Friday morning"**; takings of **£505**, **"a record for
interprovincial matches"**. **D. Gallaher** listed among the Auckland forwards (wing).
⚠️ Two McGregors in the report — **R. McGregor captained Auckland**, and a **McGregor** scored
Wellington's first try. **The draft names neither try-scorer.** Do not "fix" this without a source.

### ⭐⭐ The name came from the Natives — [" 'All Blacks' – The Name"](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/history-of-nz-rugby/all-blacks-the-name)

- **"The first known published reference to All Blacks was in relation to the Natives… in mid-1889."**
- **1893**, first official NZ team in Australia, an Auckland journalist: **"I expect to see the all
  blacks come out on top with a substantial majority."**
- Morning after the Originals' first 1905 match: **"The All Blacks, as they are styled by reason of
  their sable and unrelieved costume…"** → **"Whatever happened or did not happen in 1905, the team was
  known as the All Blacks when it arrived in Britain."**
- **The "all backs" printer's-error story is listed by the museum among the "incorrect rumours"** —
  told by a 1905 player at the **1955** jubilee (Hartlepool 63–0; the *Daily Mail*; the printer who
  **"had got the l in somehow"**). The museum's accepted explanation is the dull one: they were dressed
  all in black, and **"All"** was a common period prefix for a full international side (**"All
  Japan"**). **Do not retell the printer's error as fact** — it is this chapter's third deflated myth,
  after Monro-as-founder and, in `07-england.md`, Webb Ellis.

### ⭐⭐ The All Golds — [NZRL, Kiwis Roll of Honour: Albert Baskerville](https://nzrl.co.nz/kiwis-roll-of-honour/albert-baskerville)

**This host is reachable by curl** (the England chapter already cites it). Wellington postal clerk;
played for **Wellington and Oriental**; author of *Modern Rugby Football: New Zealand Methods*.
- ⭐⭐⭐ **The amateur weapon turns inward:** the party assembled **"Despite the threat of life bans from
  the NZRU (for the 'crime' of being financially compensated for working time lost while playing sport,
  thus surrendering their amateur status)"**. **"Working time lost" is broken time** — the 1893
  Westminster Palace vote (282–136) and the 1895 George Hotel walkout, `07-england.md`. **New Zealand's
  union used the RFU's weapon on New Zealand's players, eighteen years after the RFU used it on the
  Natives.** This is the generation's argument.
- **28 players, eight of them All Blacks**, tour of **more than nine months**; began **August 1907**,
  three matches v NSW in Sydney, **the NSWRL having formed earlier that month**; to England via Ceylon.
- **"All Golds"** was **the *Sydney Morning Herald*'s** derisive coinage.
- **35 matches in England and Wales, October 1907 – February 1908.** Baskerville played **one** match
  there (managerial, secretarial and journalistic duties), scoring in the **21–10 win over St Helens**.
- Home via Australia, **10 more matches**; Baskerville scored an intercept try in the **first
  New Zealand–Australia Test, won 11–10 in Sydney**; **died 11 days later, aged 25**, of pneumonia
  after a chill on the Sydney–Brisbane voyage, having watched a midweek match v Queensland from the
  grandstand. Second Test dedicated to him, **won 24–12 at the Brisbane Exhibition Ground**.
- **First league match on New Zealand soil: Athletic Park, Wellington, 13 June 1908**, an exhibition
  **to raise money for his widowed mother**.
- The **1 January 1908 Aberdare** international (**Wales 9, New Zealand 8**, Dai "Tarw" Jones's winning
  try, ~20,000) is already carried in **`07-england.md` and `08-wales.md`** — **keep all three
  chapters identical on it.**

### The King's Cup, 1919 — [April 5 – 1919 NZ defeat Mother Country](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/april-on-this-day/april-5-1919-nz-defeat-mother-country)

### ⭐⭐⭐ THE 1924 IMPERIAL RUGBY CONFERENCE — the spine's best single scene, and it is the same man

[December 12 – 1924 Imperial Rugby Conference, London](https://www.rugbymuseum.co.nz/stories/on-this-day-in-new-zealand-rugby/december-on-this-day/december-12-1924-imperial-rugby-conference-london).
The Museum holds **a 90-page verbatim record** of the proceedings — remits submitted by New Zealand
and/or New South Wales, several of them advocating changes **"that did not in some cases become rugby
law until after World War II."**

- ⭐⭐ **New Zealand had no seat of its own:** **"England seems to have had additional representation
  because the three Colonial Rugby Unions were in 1924 affiliated to the Rugby Football Union rather
  than to the IRB."** The best team in the world was a dependant of one of the parties.
- ⭐⭐⭐ **THE CHAIRMAN WAS ROWLAND HILL — the same man who refereed the Natives at Blackheath in 1889
  and dictated McCausland's apology.** By 1924 he is **"an ardent supporter of the amateur code and the
  outstanding official figure in the game"** (RFU president 1904–07). **"At the start of the Conference
  he took a hard line in refusing to allow discussion of a New Zealand remit re professionalism,
  despite New Zealand protests that when remits were called for there was no suggestion of some matters
  being off limits."** **Thirty-five years apart, same man, same mechanism.** This is the strongest
  through-line in the chapter — set it up in Gen 1 and land it here.
- **Remit No. 9** (Hill: **"rather a controversial one"**) — that a knock-on recovered before the ball
  reaches the ground should not be blown. The Welsh delegate **"hoped Mr Wilson (of New Zealand) will
  not press this matter"**; Hill: **"I do not see how you are going to alter the rule."** **Adopted in
  1973 — over fifty years later.**
- **Stan Dean** — manager of the 1924–25 All Blacks *and* chairman of the NZRFU management committee
  for **a quarter of a century, 1922–47**. **Wilson** — Canterbury president, NZ selector 1908/10/13/14,
  NZRFU president 1923.

## GEN 4 — verified by hand (August 2026)

**⭐ THE SOURCE FOR THIS GENERATION IS [Trevor Richards, "Neither forgotten nor forgiven", *e-Tangata*](https://e-tangata.co.nz/history/neither-forgotten-nor-forgiven/)**
— Richards was one of the fourteen founders of **HART** (Auckland University, **July 1969**) and its
first chair, 1969–80. **Reachable by curl.** It is a participant account and covers 1919–1966 end to
end; it is also already cited in `06-south-africa.md`. **This is the single richest source found for
the chapter so far** and it should be mined again for Gen 5.

- **1949, the union's own words:** **"In view of the domestic policy of South Africa, the players
  cannot be other than wholly European."** Richards' framing: from **1921 to 1966** the NZRFU **"failed
  to confront South African racist political pressure"**, sending all-white teams in **1928, 1949 and
  1960** — **"This is the same NZRFU that, throughout HART's life, extolled the virtues of keeping
  politics out of sport."**
- **New detail not in `06-south-africa.md`, and it belongs to this chapter:** at Napier in 1921, **"As
  the Māori players began their haka before kickoff, the Springboks turned their backs."** Winger
  **Jack Blake**: the team were **"seething with anger"**. And **Nepia**, who watched, on the leaked
  cable: it **"provoked a reaction and bitterness which within the heart of the Maori race have neither
  been forgotten nor forgiven."**
- **1928:** the excluded pair were **George Nepia and Jimmy Mills**; the exclusion was **"hailed in
  South Africa as a 'diplomatic triumph'"**.
- **1937:** no Māori fixture; **Te Arawa** sought a boycott — **"the first organised, nationwide
  protest"** — supported by **Te Puea Herangi**.
- **⭐⭐ 1939, the letter:** **Everard Jackson** (father of **Syd** and **Moana Jackson**) and **Tori
  Reed**, both of whom played all three Tests against the 1937 Springboks, were asked by the union
  **"if they would make themselves unavailable for selection the following year to save the rugby union
  from embarrassment. Well, the Second World War arrived and saved them from embarrassment."**
  (Moana Jackson, at the HART 50th-anniversary symposium.)
- **⭐⭐⭐ Kippenberger, September 1948, RSA function, Christchurch** — commanded the Māori Battalion at
  Monte Cassino, **stood on a mine and lost both feet**: **"If you had the Māori Battalion on your
  flank, you were secure."** … **"I am not going to acquiesce to any damned Afrikaner saying they
  [Māori] cannot go. To hell with them."** Reply from **A. T. Carroll** (Ngāti Kahungunu): **"If it is
  good enough for the members of our race to fight side by side with South Africans, then it is good
  enough for our players to oppose them on the rugby field."** **Eruera Tirikatene** MP asked on whose
  initiative Māori were excluded — **"His question was met with silence."**
- **⭐ 1956:** **"even the Communist Party supported that year's Springbok tour. Alone in its opposition
  to the tour was the Māori Women's Welfare League."**
- **1958–60, CABTA** — the **Citizens All Black Tour Association**, **"the first organisation in New
  Zealand or elsewhere specifically established to campaign against rugby contact with South Africa"**,
  **20+ branches from Kaikohe to Invercargill**, slogan **"No Maoris — No Tour"**.
  **⭐⭐ Nepia's telegram to the inaugural meeting: "Best of luck. Let me know if you need a fullback."**
  **The petition drew 160,000 signatures** — *not* the 150,000 sometimes quoted. **18 June 1959**, 500+
  students marched to Parliament (the first such demonstration); 1,000 at the state farewell; 2,000–
  3,000 in Auckland on the eve of departure. **Walter Nash**'s government would not intervene — the
  first government anywhere to state a position — and Nash was a friend of NZRFU chairman **Cuthbert
  Hogg**. Sociologist **Richard Thompson** (1975): the **"most vigorous controversy since the
  prohibition issue"**.
- **⭐ CABTA's own limit, quoted exactly** (keeps the chapter honest and fits the no-villains model):
  the protest was **not "against the racial policy of the South African government, but rather against
  an act of racial discrimination committed by a New Zealand sports organisation, in New Zealand, and
  concerning a New Zealand national sports team."**
- **1965:** fourth Springbok tour; small protests (**30 at Whenuapai**, ~200 at the Auckland Town Hall,
  speakers **Hone Tuwhare** and **Whetu Tirikatene**) — **but for the first time inspired by opposition
  to apartheid itself.** **5 September 1965:** South Africa's PM announced the 1967 All Black team
  would again be all-white.
- **⭐⭐ 3 February 1966 — Holyoake ends it:** **"In this country we are one people; as such we cannot
  as a nation be truly represented in any sphere by a group chosen on racial lines."** Richards: **"a
  clear non-directive directive"** — **the 1967 tour was off.**
- **⭐ And the counterweight, which the draft keeps** (no villains): before **Frank Corner** left for the
  UN in 1961, Holyoake's only advice was to avoid the word *abhorrent* about apartheid — **"My people
  don't like it."** NZ voted against or abstained on most UN resolutions on South Africa **1960–72**.
  Corner: **"so long as we could get away with it domestically, we could be great internationalists"**;
