## THE SPINE (author decision, 23 August 2026)

Fiji's chapter asked why an imported class instrument failed. **Samoa's chapter is the book's cleanest
natural experiment, and the variable is a colonial border drawn through one people in 1899.**

> On **2 December 1899** the Tripartite Convention partitioned the Samoan archipelago without a single
> Samoan signature. Germany took **Upolu and Savai'i** in the west; the United States took **Tutuila
> and Manu'a** in the east, for the harbour at Pago Pago. One people, one language, one *fa'amatai*,
> one church — cut in half by three European powers, and handed to two different empires.
>
> **The western half plays rugby. The eastern half plays American football.** Both halves export
> young men's bodies at an extraordinary rate. Neither chose its sport.

That is the control case, and it is tighter than Limerick and Cork, tighter than Tucumán, tighter than
the Borders — because the two populations are *the same population*, and everything except the flag is
held constant. Print the two outcomes and let them stand.

**And the border proved itself within nineteen years, before either sport arrived.** In November 1918
the **SS Talune** docked at Apia and the New Zealand administration waved it in. **About 8,500
Samoans died — between a fifth and a quarter of the population — in two months.** Forty miles east,
the US Navy commandant quarantined American Sāmoa and **not one person died of the flu**; the New
Zealand administration in Apia was offered American help and **refused it** (Te Papa). The same
people, the same weeks, two administrations. *This is what the border did before it ever touched a
football.*

**The mechanism that carries the game is the village, and its prototype is the pastor.** Meleisea:
when a village became Christian "they built a church and a house for a teacher or pastor, and began to
contribute to the church by **supporting the pastor with food and services**." The Samoan village
raises money to keep a man it has chosen, so that he can do something on the village's behalf that the
village could not do itself. **A hundred and fifty years later it does the same thing with a rugby
player, and the section's two words — church and export — turn out to name one institution.**

**The landing is a ranking and a number.** By 2026 Samoa — three World Cup quarter-finals, twice the
conquerors of Wales — was **nineteenth in the world**, in the **second-tier Nations Cup** while Fiji
and Japan went up into the Nations Championship; it had qualified for RWC 2027 as the **twenty-fourth
and last team**, on a 13–13 draw with **Belgium**, in **Dubai**; its July 2026 "home" matches were all
played **in Chile**; and its squad of thirty-two contained **three players based in Samoa**.

*Do not blunt the last one. The coach's own line about those three is the chapter's closing note.*

## GEN 0 — Lotu (1830–1919)

- **24 August 1830.** John Williams of the **London Missionary Society** sighted Savai'i and landed at
  **Sapapali'i**, looking for **Malietoa Vainu'upo**. Meleisea gives the detail: Williams sailed the
  *Messenger of Peace*, took Charles Barff and **eight teachers from Tahiti and Rarotonga**, and
  picked up at Tonga a Samoan named **Fauea**, who directed him to Sapapali'i. Hearing that the chief
  **Tamafaiga** had been assassinated at Fasito'outa, Fauea told Williams "that the main obstacle to
  preaching the Gospel in Samoa had been removed."
- **The conversion was fast and it was collective.** Meleisea: Samoans built no temples and no idols,
  so conversion was marked by a village ceremony in which people **"ate their aitu"** — cooked and ate
  the creatures sacred to them, so that the spirit "could never again be worshipped."
- **⭐⭐ THE MECHANISM — QUOTE THIS.** Meleisea: "**When a village decided to become Christian they
  built a church and a house for a teacher or pastor, and began to contribute to the church by
  supporting the pastor with food and services, and by contributing coconut oil** to the senior
  representative of the mission in each district." The oil went to England to be sold, to fund the
  mission. → *The village collectively maintains a chosen man so he can act on its behalf.* That is
  the Samoan export mechanism, two centuries before the rugby contract.
- **The Wesleyans got there first, unofficially.** The chief **Saiva'aia** of Tafua-Salelologa brought
  news of the *Lotu Toga* from Tonga in **1828**; Peter Turner visited in 1828 without founding a
  mission; Williams had informally agreed with the Wesleyans that **Samoa was the LMS's and Fiji was
  theirs** — which is a nice, exact statement of how two Pacific countries got two different churches
  by a handshake between Englishmen.
- **⭐ THE MARISTS, 25 MAY 1845.** Meleisea: the Catholic Church was established by two French Marist
  priests, **Father Foudaire (Lutovi'o) and Father Violette (Silipele)**, arriving from 'Uvea on
  *L'Étoile de la Mer* and anchoring at **Falealupo on 25 May 1845**. → **The same order that brings
  Catholicism in 1845 brings rugby in 1920.** Cross-link `01-uruguay.md` Gen 2, where the Christian
  Brothers *choose* rugby at Stella Maris and the Jesuits choose football: **a teaching order picks a
  code and a country follows.**
- **⭐⭐⭐ THE PARTITION, 2 DECEMBER 1899** (effective 16 February 1900). The Tripartite Convention:
  Germany took **Savai'i and Upolu**; the United States took **Tutuila and Manu'a**, for **Pago Pago**,
  described in the American accounts as the best naval base in the South Seas; Britain withdrew for
  compensation elsewhere. The **chiefs of Tutuila and Aunu'u signed the Treaty of Cession on 17 April
  1900.** No Samoan was party to the Convention itself.
- **New Zealand takes over.** German Samoa was seized by New Zealand forces in 1914 and administered
  by New Zealand thereafter (League of Nations mandate, then UN trusteeship).
- **⭐⭐⭐ THE TALUNE, 7 NOVEMBER 1918 — the chapter's Gen 0 set-piece.**
  - RNZ (Jamie Tahana, 7 Nov 2018): "'There is a sickness on this boat,' a passenger cried from the
    deck of the SS *Talune* as it steamed into Apia on 7 November, 1918… **Despite many other Pacific
    ports having already imposed quarantine, the New Zealand administrators in Apia waved the
    steamboat in.**" Within two months "about **8,500 people were dead - a fifth of Samoa's entire
    population**."
  - **Toeolesulusulu Damon Salesa** (Auckland): New Zealand's own combined war-and-flu death rate was
    "probably 3 or 4 percent… and that was horrific." "**Between 22 and 25 [per cent] of Samoa's
    population died in just a matter of weeks.**"
  - Detail worth using: villages sealed with mats; carts circling daily to collect bodies; **100 dead
    at Sale'aumua**, **88 at Lalomanu**; **Albert Wendt**'s father, aged eight, one of the few
    unaffected in his village, put to work carrying the dead to the road.
  - Te Papa: "The epidemic was mishandled by the New Zealand administration; no attempt was made to
    quarantine areas where the infection had already spread, and doctors were not available to Sāmoans
    who lived inland. Telegraph requests to New Zealand by the Administration asking for help were
    rejected, but **the Administration also refused American aid when assistance from Eastern Sāmoa
    was offered.**" A 1919 Royal Commission (Elliot, Wilson, Moorhouse) "put the death toll at 8,500
    people, but that figure was likely higher."
  - **And the control:** the US Navy commandant at Pago Pago, **John Martin Poyer**, imposed a strict
    quarantine on hearing of the outbreak in the west, with the support of the local chiefs, and
    **American Sāmoa recorded no influenza deaths**; Poyer received the Navy Cross.
    ⚠️ The Poyer detail comes from US naval and journalistic accounts (*Proceedings*, Retro Report,
    Lapham's), **not** from Te Papa or RNZ. Attribute it; do not merge it into the New Zealand sources.

## GEN 1 — The Brothers and the Tree (1920–1938)

- **1920: the Marist Brothers introduce rugby.** (ABC Pacific, Lakapi Samoa's centenary framing:
  the Apia Rugby Club was "officially established in 1924, **just four years after rugby was first
  introduced to the island**", "formally introduced by the Marist Brothers".)
- **1924 — three things at once.** ABC: "The **Apia Rugby Union** was established in 1924 as the first
  national governing body, and **by August of that year, the first Samoan squad had been assembled and
  played its first international test against Fiji in Apia**. **Vaiala Ulalei Rugby Club** was also
  established that year and made history as the first rugby club in Samoa." The union **affiliated to
  the New Zealand Rugby Football Union** in the same year.
  → Vaiala is "very close to Apia Park" — the club, the ground and the union are one neighbourhood.
- **The Test itself is told in `14-fiji.md`**: **18 August 1924, Apia Park, 7am so the Samoans could
  get to work, a large tree on the halfway line, Fiji in all black, Fiji 6–0.** Tell it from the
  Samoan side and do not repeat the Fijian framing wholesale. The contemporary description of the
  Samoans — "**the big, sturdy men of Apia**" — is the first sentence ever written about Samoan rugby
  by an outsider, and it has never stopped being written.
- **The Mau.** Te Papa: the Mau was "revitalised in 1926 through the efforts of **Ta'isi Olaf
  Frederick Nelson**"; Nelson was **exiled to New Zealand in 1928**. "On **29 December 1929**, a
  peaceful demonstration by the Mau movement ended in tragedy when New Zealand military police fired
  upon the protestors. **Eleven Sāmoans were killed and approximately 50 were injured; Tupua Tamasese
  Lealofi III**, who had led the movement since Nelson's exile, **died from his wounds after being
  shot from behind.** The tragic event became known as **Black Saturday**." The Mau were declared
  seditious and their uniform — **a navy blue lavalava with a white stripe** — was **made illegal on
  13 January 1930**.
  ⚠️ **Date discrepancy: Te Papa says 29 December 1929; several other accounts say 28 December.**
  Use Te Papa's date, attributed, or write "late December 1929". Do not assert a day flatly.
  ⚠️ The Mau's 1929 petition to George V is reported to have led on the 1918 influenza as its first
  complaint. Sourced only to summary; **hedge or omit**.

## GEN 2 — The Long Absence (1939–1961)

- **⚠️ THE BAN — the chapter's biggest sourcing problem, and it must be hedged.** ABC Pacific's image
  caption on its centenary piece reads: "**Rugby was banned in Samoa for several decades because of
  spectator antics when their team didn't win, but returned in the 1950s.**" Its programme headline
  is "**Once banned for being too violent**." That is the *only* support found: a caption and a
  headline on a radio segment. No date, no authority, no ordinance. **Attribute it to the ABC, say
  plainly that the detail could not be established, and build nothing on it.** It is too good a fact
  to assert and too interesting to omit.
- **The war.** American Sāmoa became a major US Marine base; the wartime build-up at Pago Pago is
  where the eastern islands' American orientation hardened. Uperesa: "Under colonial and territorial
  governments, agricultural export (copra) and **military service** were central to the incorporation
  of local people into waged work"; "**Starting in earnest during the post–World War II period, waves
  of emigration tied to military service helped establish Samoan communities in the United States.**"
- **The road out.** Western Samoa moved from League of Nations mandate to UN trusteeship to
  independence — see Gen 3.

## GEN 3 — Mr Rugby (1962–1986)

- **1 January 1962: Western Samoa becomes independent** — the first Pacific Island nation to do so.
- **⭐ LA'AULI ALAN GREY.** ABC Pacific, quoting **Pafelio Momoisea**, who captained Vaiala: Grey "was
  called the '**Mr Rugby**' of Samoa. A no-nonsense coach and he had an astute rugby brain. Alan was
  very strict on training, **if you missed one session, you were out of the playing squad the next
  game. Everyone was treated the same** which built purpose, unity and competition within the team."
  Under Grey, Samoa went to its first **Hong Kong Sevens**; he was "instrumental in getting Samoa to
  the World Cup in 1991"; he received the **Western Samoa Order of Merit in 1993**; and when he died
  in **2018** he was given the rare honour of a **state funeral**.
- **⭐ 1971 — the first gold.** ABC: **Iupeliauro Selefuti Valley Patu** began playing for Vaiala in
  **1964** on the wing; in **1970** there were trials for the **1971 South Pacific Games in Tahiti**,
  and **six Vaiala men were selected**. The 1971 side **won Samoa's first gold medal in rugby union**,
  beating the **Cook Islands** in the final. The next year **seven Vaiala men** were chosen when Samoa
  played **two Tests against the Māori All Blacks in Apia**.
- **The radio.** Momoisea: in the 1970s "the names of the players who had made the team were read out
  on the radio. **The families heard the names read out and were all very proud and excited. I was out
  somewhere with my mates and never heard my name read out but when I got home everyone was buzzing
  and celebrating.**" → Use it. It is the village, hearing.
- **⭐⭐⭐ MICHAEL JONES, AND THE FIRST TRY IN WORLD CUP HISTORY.** From **World Rugby's own Hall of
  Fame page** (inducted 2014): "**After a solitary cap for Samoa, Jones switched allegiance to New
  Zealand in time for the inaugural Rugby World Cup in 1987. In the opening match against Italy, his
  debut, Jones became the first player to score a try.** He scored again in the final as France were
  beaten 29-9. Jones also played at RWC 1991 but **his refusal to play on Sundays on religious grounds
  restricted him to three appearances. For that reason, he was not considered for RWC 1995.** … Had it
  not been for injuries and his faith, Jones would have won far more than 55 All Blacks caps… **On his
  retirement, he coached Samoa at two Rugby World Cups.**"
  → **This is the section's perfect story and it belongs to Samoa.** The first try in the history of
  the Rugby World Cup was scored by a Samoan international, playing for New Zealand, in a tournament
  Samoa was not invited to — and the thing that cost him two World Cups was **the church**. Then he
  came back and coached Samoa. *The church and the export, in one man.*
- **1987: Samoa was not invited.** ABC states it plainly. `12-new-zealand.md` and `14-fiji.md` carry
  the other half: **Western Samoa was put on standby** in case Fiji could not travel after the coup.
  **Samoa's relationship to the first World Cup was as a reserve.**
- **Pat Lam on that** (RugbyPass, 17 Oct 2021): "at that time it was just one Test match against a
  visiting team… **Samoa was not invited and there was nothing for young Samoans to aspire to at that
  stage, particularly those living in New Zealand.** … every young Pacific Islander in those days
  wanted to play for the All Blacks." → **The export is not explained by poverty alone. There was
  nothing to be exported *from*.**

## GEN 4 — Cardiff (1987–1995)

- **1988: Lakapi Samoa affiliates to the IRB.** (Oceania Rugby / Lakapi Samoa. One year after the
  World Cup they were not invited to.)
- **⭐⭐⭐ 6 OCTOBER 1991. CARDIFF ARMS PARK. WESTERN SAMOA 16, WALES 13.**
  - RugbyPass: "a blue-shirted hit squad of smiling assassins that included **Apollo Perelini, Frank
    Bunce, Brian Lima, Stephen Bachop** and the late great **Peter Fatialofa**", with **Pat Lam making
    his Test debut**. **To'o Vaega** ran at the Welsh line.
  - It "prompted one of rugby's more famous ever quips, '**Thank heavens Wales weren't playing the
    whole of Samoa**'."
  - ABC: the famous try — **Sila Vaifale** caught the ball via a play from **Aumua To'o Vaega** and
    Fatialofa.
  - Then "**narrowly beaten by eventual champions Australia at Pontypool**", a "clinical number on
    **Argentina in Pontypridd**", and the **quarter-final at Murrayfield against Scotland**, where
    "their odyssey came to an abrupt end."
  - **Who paid for it:** ABC — "Rugby had not yet turned professional, so **raising finances to get
    the team to the World Cup mostly fell on businessmen like Grey and Annandale**."
  - **Peter Fatialofa** was a **furniture remover in Auckland** (ESPN/RugbyPass; the popular version
    is "piano mover" — ⚠️ **use "furniture remover", which is what the sources say**).
  - **Kevin Senio**, Fatialofa's nephew, was eleven: "It was the belief and the pride that we all had
    as Samoans from watching all around the world but witnessing that as a young kid and then
    realising '**We can do that too!**'"
  - Lam, on going home to teach in Auckland afterwards: before 1991 "we didn't have any Samoa to
    aspire to"; after it, "the number of kids of Samoan heritage" changed.
- **Hall of Fame:** **Muliagatele Brian Lima** inducted **2011** — "**The Chiropractor**", and "one of
  only two players to have played in **five** Rugby World Cups". **Papali'itele Peter Fatialofa MNZM**
  inducted **2019**, posthumously; Anne and Jeremiah Fatialofa and Kevin Senio attended.
  **Seilala Mapusua** on that night: "seeing someone that looks like you and represents you, who wore
  the same jersey as you did … it's hard to put into words."

## GEN 5 — The Diaspora Team (1996–2010)

- Samoa reached World Cup quarter-finals in **1991** and **1995**, and has not reached one **since
  rugby turned professional** (ABC states this directly: "Despite Samoa not making a quarter-final of
  the Rugby World Cup since rugby turned professional…").
- **Manusina.** ABC: the women's team "made its first appearance in **1999** at the **Hong Kong
  Sevens**, and in **2000**, the XVs squad had its **first test match against Japan** at a home game
  in Apia." Since then the squad "has qualified and competed in the **Women's Rugby World Cup three
  times**." **Faamoana Leilua** (Manusina 2004–07) names **Dawn Rasmussen**, campaign manager, "a
  stalwart for women's sport… very well known in the motherland for her contribution to athletics",
  and **Tauanu'u Nick Bakulich**, former team manager, "instrumental in advocating for girls and
  women's rugby." Leilua now: "**The women's team can be a marquee team and I really hope for a valid
  connection between the union and the team.**"
- **The scholarship on what the export is.** Uperesa (*Gridiron Capital*, Duke, 2022), quoting the
  anthropologist **Thomas Carter**: in some sports, like rugby, elite athletes "are also seen as
  **national possessions**", and this "veneer of celebrity actually **obscures the processes of
  transnational capital, the structures of labour in a transnational industry, and how states are
  complicit in structuring this industry**." Uperesa's own framing: sport migration is a
  "**high-stakes lottery** with promises of prestigious rewards and the opportunity to bring their
  families honour and esteem, **even as the odds are not always in their favour**."
  She also names the Pacific case directly: "the emergence of a '**Pasifika rugby-playing diaspora**'
  builds on **historically contingent labor migration schemes to former or existing colonial
  powers**, as well as a dispersed system of sport scholarships and club contracts."

## GEN 6 — The Union and the Players (2011–2019)

- **⭐⭐ NOVEMBER 2014 — THE STRIKE THREAT.** Samoa's players threatened to boycott the Test against
  **England at Twickenham**. Senior players wrote to the **IRB** raising concerns about the Samoa
  Rugby Union including **misuse of funds and interference in team selection**; the grievances also
  covered low payments and a lack of transparency. **The union's chairman was the Prime Minister,
  Tuilaepa Sailele Malielegaoi**, who called the players "**spoilt children**" and said they were
  putting the nation to ransom. The boycott was **called off** after the players met the IRB and the
  players' association; the match went ahead.
  → Tell it from inside, with no villain. A national union chaired by the head of government, a squad
  of employees of foreign clubs, and a governing body in Dublin brokering between them.
- **2017: insolvency.** The chairman — still the Prime Minister — **acknowledged the union was
  insolvent**. ⚠️ The Japan Times report of this could not be read (JavaScript challenge). Cite it
  only via a source that was actually opened, or state it as reported and attribute.
- **World Rugby subsequently brokered new agreements** around Samoa's set-up and World Cup
  participation. ⚠️ Sourced from search summary only — **hedge**.

## GEN 7 — Twenty-Fourth (2020–2026)

- **Moana Pasifika**, 2022. Based in **South Auckland**, entering **Super Rugby Pacific** alongside the
  Fijian Drua, "geared mainly to create opportunities for Tongan and Samoan eligible players and to
  build local depth for those nations." **Sekope Kepu** named inaugural captain, 2 Feb 2022; first
  match **4 March 2022**, lost **33–12** to the Crusaders; finished **12th** in 2022; beat the
  **Brumbies 32–22** at Mt Smart in round 15.
  → **The contrast with the Fijian Drua is the point and must be made.** Fiji's team is in Fiji.
  Samoa's is in Auckland. *Samoa's professional pathway is in another country, because that is where
  the Samoans are.*
- **The 2021 eligibility change** (Regulation 8, effective 1 Jan 2022; 36-month stand-down, birth or a
  parent or grandparent) — the details are in `14-fiji.md`; Samoa was among its largest beneficiaries.
  **Lima Sopoaga**, an All Black, played fly half for Samoa at RWC 2023 and was **player of the
  match** against England.
- **⭐ 7 OCTOBER 2023. STADE PIERRE-MAUROY, LILLE. ENGLAND 18, SAMOA 17.** Ollie Chessum opened the
  scoring; **two first-half tries from Nigel Ah-Wong** put Samoa ahead at half-time; replacement scrum
  half **Danny Care** scored the winning try. **Owen Farrell** passed Jonny Wilkinson as England's
  all-time leading points scorer in the same match. **Lima Sopoaga was named player of the match on
  the losing side.** Head coach: **Seilala Mapusua**.
- **2024 — the centenary.** Lakapi Samoa marked 100 years with a **capping ceremony** honouring
  everyone affiliated with Samoan rugby from 1924, and a match against **Tonga**. ⚠️ ABC records the
  sting honestly and so should the chapter: "**there was controversy following former players from
  both the men's and women's teams stating they hadn't been invited.**"
- **18 July 2025, Apia Park: Scotland 41, Samoa 12.** Seven tries to two; Scotland led 22–0 at
  half-time; **Benjamin Petaia Nee-Nee** and **Duncan Paia'aua** scored for Samoa. It was the last
  match of Scotland's Pacific tour — **six days after Fiji had beaten the same Scotland side 29–14 in
  Suva** (`14-fiji.md`). ⚠️ One agency report places this match "at Eden Park", which is wrong;
  multiple others and Sky's form guide have it as a Samoa home fixture. **Say Apia and note nothing**,
  or say "at home in Apia".
- **⭐⭐⭐ SEPTEMBER 2025 — SAMOA LOSES ITS WORLD CUP PLACE TO CHILE.**
  - **First leg, 20 September 2025: Samoa 32, Chile 32** — Samoa came back from **25–8 down at
    half-time**. It was Samoa's "home" leg and it was played at **America First Field, Salt Lake City,
    Utah**.
  - **Second leg, 27 September 2025, Estadio Sausalito, Viña del Mar: Chile 31, Samoa 12**, before
    **21,754** people. `03-chile.md` already carries this in full from the Chilean side — **match its
    figures exactly and do not contradict it.**
  - Chile became **the 23rd team** to qualify for RWC 2027. Samoa went to the **Final Qualification
    Tournament**.

## AMERICAN SĀMOA — the other half, braided, never a separate chapter

From **Lisa Uperesa, *Gridiron Capital: How American Football Became a Samoan Game*** (Duke University
Press, 2022) — introduction read in full from the publisher's free PDF. Uperesa is Samoan, from
Tutuila, and the daughter of a football coach.
- "**A United States territory since 1900**, we know little about the place of Tutuila ma Manu'a in
  the larger narratives of American history and globalization. **Today, the islands enter the US
  national imaginary primarily through gridiron football; this hypervisibility works to erase Samoan
  history and the fullness of Samoan experience outside of sport.** Moreover, it obscures a century of
  US imperialism."
- **The economy that produces the pipeline:** copra and **military service** under the colonial
  government; then **cannery** and bureaucratic employment; **StarKist Foods and Van Camp Seafood**
  supplying "up to **50 percent of the US tuna market**, drawing largely on **nonresident labor**";
  "**From 1975 to 2000, the economy stagnated, with the tuna canneries and federal financial aid
  accounting for virtually all growth**"; and "the federally funded bureaucracy, **military
  enlistment**, and outmigration have become the important employment options for local residents."
- **The number to use — and only this one.** Uperesa: "**our calculations for the *In Football We
  Trust* film based on the 2013–2014 nfl season rosters and available demographic data found Samoan
  and Tongans to be 28 times more likely to make it to the nfl than other ethnic groups in the United
  States.**"
  ⚠️ **The widely repeated "56 times more likely" figure is folkloric** — it circulates without a
  traceable methodology. **Do not print it.** Print Uperesa's 28, with her own qualification that it
  is a calculation for a film, based on one season's rosters.
- **The "Polynesian Pipeline"** — "an elaborate network with multiple points of entry… destinations
  include high schools, **military prep academies**, junior colleges, and four-year colleges and
  universities across the nation."
- ⚠️ **The 1961 *Reader's Digest* article "America's Shame in the South Seas"** and the subsequent US
  infrastructure push (including high-school football) appears in secondary accounts but **was not
  found in the portion of Uperesa's book that is open**. **Hedge or omit.**
- ⚠️ **Rugby union has been played in American Sāmoa** and it has a union, but every account found was
  wiki-derived. **Say nothing specific about American Sāmoan rugby.**
