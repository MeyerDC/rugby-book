## THE SPINE (author decision, 23 August 2026)

Fiji is the chapter where **this book's own title is quoted back at it by a peer-reviewed source** —
and where the sorting happened anyway, on a different axis.

Stewart-Withers, Sewabu and Richardson, in the *Journal of Sport for Development* (2017), write that
**"rugby in Fiji is not bound by class… where even the poorest can participate."** That is the book's
thesis, asserted in the literature about one country. So the chapter cannot be about whether rugby has
a class in Fiji. It has to ask the harder question:

> **A colonial government built a school on the English public-school model at Nasinu in 1906, for the
> express purpose of producing two classes of Fijians — and that school became the greatest rugby
> nursery in the country, and the class never formed. In Sydney and Brisbane the identical institution
> succeeded completely. Why did it fail here?**

That is the **direct control case against `13-australia.md`**, whose spine is "in Australia the
institution is a headmaster." Fiji had the headmaster. Fiji had his opinions in writing. It did not
produce Australia's outcome, and the reason is that the institution underneath the school was
different: in Australia the school handed the boy to an old boys' club that existed to keep him among
people like himself; in Fiji the school handed the boy back to his **village**, where the club, the
chief and the church were the same body of people and there was nobody for the team to be sorted
*against*.

**But something was sorted, and the sorting holds to this day: race, not class.** Governor Gordon
imported 60,965 indentured labourers between 1879 and 1916 *specifically in order to keep indigenous
Fijians off the plantations and in their villages*. The Methodist mission split itself into separate
**Fijian and Indo-Fijian branches in 1901**. Rugby then reproduced the same shape — a European union
in **1913**, a "native competition" in **1914**, a **Fiji Native Union in 1915** — and soccer
reproduced it from the other side, with the **Fiji Indian Football Association** as the game's de
facto controlling body from **1938**. A hundred and forty years later rugby is the iTaukei game and
soccer is the Indo-Fijian one, and **no institution had to keep it that way, because the institutions
that made it that way were never rugby's.**

*In South Africa (`06-south-africa.md`) the state used rugby to build a race. In Fiji nobody had to:
the land policy and a mission board in Melbourne had already done it, and rugby simply inherited the
shape.*

**The section motif — the church and the export — resolves as a single mechanism.** The same
institution that carried the game (village + chief + Methodist church, the trinity Kanemasu and
Molnar call **"ratuism, religion and rugby"**) is the one that makes Fijians the world's most
exportable rugby players: a man plays for the village, the village expects remittances, and the
contract is abroad. **And rugby is not the only body Fiji exports.** The same men, from the same
villages, go to UN peacekeeping (from 1978), to the British Army (from 1961/62), and to private
military companies in Iraq (from 2003). The chapter tells rugby migration and military migration as
**one trade**, because in Fiji they are.

**The landing.** In **2026** Fiji was finally admitted to rugby's top table — the inaugural **Nations
Championship** — and played its **three "home" matches at Cardiff City Stadium, the Hill Dickinson
Stadium in Liverpool and Murrayfield**. The FRU's own announcement calls them "home" fixtures that
"take fans on a unique journey across the Northern Hemisphere." Twelve months earlier Fiji had beaten
Scotland **29–14 in Suva**. *The country that exports its players had, by 2026, exported its home
ground.* Do not blunt this — it is the chapter's last argument.

## GEN 0 — The Constabulary Ground (1874–1912)

- **Cession: 10 October 1874.** Fiji became a British crown colony "following an offer of unconditional
  cession." Close-Barry: the Methodists "preceded the cession of the Island group by nearly 40 years."
- **The Methodists arrived in 1835**, via Tonga, at Lakeba in the Lau group — Cross and Cargill —
  "isolated from European settlements." Cakobau's conversion followed in **1854**.
- **Gordon and the indenture.** Close-Barry, p.4: the first governor **Sir Arthur Gordon** and his
  successors "oversaw the migration of **60,965 indentured labourers to Fiji between 1879 and 1916**"
  (citing Gillion, *Population Studies*, 1956). "**Gordon had adopted this system in an effort to
  preserve customary Fijian society**, noting that indigenous engagement in plantation systems in the
  West Indies had been highly detrimental."
  → **This is the founding institutional fact of the chapter.** The two communities were separated
  by policy, at the moment of colonisation, and the separation was *benevolent in intent*.
- **Indirect rule.** Close-Barry, p.1–2: the mission "was organised in a way that **mirrored the
  systems of governance used in British colonies under indirect rule**… used to promote leadership
  through chiefs or an educated elite, while developing ethnicised categories of organisation based on
  conceptualisations of custom."
- **⭐⭐ THE MISSION SPLITS IN 1901.** Close-Barry: "The book opens with the mission's establishment of
  **separate 'Fijian' and 'Indo-Fijian' branches in 1901**, which formed the foundation for **two
  separate, ethnicised Methodist identities in Fiji**." An Indo-Fijian mission hall opened at Nausori
  on **19 December 1901**; Hannah Dudley had founded the first school exclusively for Indo-Fijian
  children in Suva after arriving in **1897**. *The church did it thirteen years before rugby did.*
- **Rugby arrives, 1884, at Ba.** FRU: "Rugby was first played in Fiji by **European and Fijian
  soldiers of the Native Constabulary at Ba, on Viti Levu Island**." JSFD says the same and adds the
  crucial clause: games "between European and Fijian policemen and soldiers of the Armed
  Constabulary, **developing along segregated lines based on ethnicity**."
  ⚠️ The FRU's own narrative panel hedges to "**believed first played… in the 1880s**" while its
  chronology gives 1884 flat. Say **1884** and note that the union itself hedges the decade.
- **1900/1904:** "reports of games began to appear in the press, and although most players were
  expatriates, a **club competition began in 1904**. Two of the earliest clubs were **Civil Service
  and Constabulary**." (FRU) *Both are arms of the colonial state. There is no cricket club and no
  university in this founding — there is a police force.*
- **1907:** **A. C. Holms**, the Wairarapa fullback, passing through Suva with three other New Zealand
  players, "not only played with Fijians on **Albert Park**, Suva, but predicted (in a Vancouver
  newspaper) that Fiji would one day make their mark in rugby football." (FRU)
- **1911:** warship crews provided frequent opposition; the first regular club, **Davies**, formed.
- **⭐⭐ QUEEN VICTORIA SCHOOL, AND THE CLASS THAT WAS ORDERED AND DID NOT ARRIVE.**
  From *The Fiji Times* (Serafina Silaitoga, 21 Jan 2019), quoting historic documents printed in 1953
  supplied by former High Commissioner **Emitai Boladuadua**:
  - **1879**: the **Bose Levu Vakaturaga** (Great Council of Chiefs) agreed to found a school for the
    *luve ni turaga* (chiefs' children) aged 12–16; approval 1881, classes from **1883 at Yanawai**
    with 130 students — "the first government school in the colony."
  - **1902**: "the Council of Chiefs asked the governor of Fiji to establish a school **modelled on
    English public schools or colleges** for the education of Fijian youths."
  - **1905 budget address**, Governor **Sir Everard Im Thurn**: a school at Nasinu "for the young
    generation of Fijians (iTaukei) **in order that as far as desirable they may imbibe European
    rather than Fijian habits of thought and work** and what is to my mind not the least important
    item — language."
  - **⭐ "Governor Im Thurn believed that there were two classes of Fijians and that so far as these
    performed two different functions — the chiefs as thinkers and overseers, the commoners as manual
    labourers. He recommended that a different education was desirable for each class."**
  - Gazetted **17 November 1905**; foundation works 1906; first headmaster **J. B. Thomson** arrived
    July 1906; **school opened 3 January 1907 with a roll of 32 boys**, roughly two per province,
    selected by the Native Commissioner and the Roko Tui.
  - "At first, only a fraction of the students were sons of chiefs, however, under the insistence of
    the headmaster, this policy was soon reversed and **by 1910, only the sons of chiefs were
    admitted**." Thomson: "After three years of experience, I have come to the conclusion that as a
    general rule, **a chief's son is far more intelligent than the son of a commoner**…"
  → Print Im Thurn's two classes and Thomson's sentence and let them stand. **This is the chapter's
  Gen 0 set-piece and the hinge of the whole argument.** The English public-school model was imported
  into Fiji *explicitly as a class instrument*, and it produced the opposite of Australia.
- **⚠️ Muscular Christianity, cited properly.** JSFD quotes a source (their ref. 47, p.25) that in
  former British colonies rugby was used by the colonials as a "prominent part in educational
  ideology, which strongly relied on the character-forming properties of **religion, athleticism and
  team sports (muscular Christianity)** to form young men." Attribute to the JSFD paper.

## GEN 1 — Two Unions, One Colony (1913–1938)

All from the FRU chronology unless noted.
- **1913 — the plumber.** "A group of New Zealanders came to build the **Grand Pacific Hotel** in Suva.
  One of them was plumber **Paddy Sheehan from Dunedin — a former captain of Otago**." He formed the
  **Pacific Club**; by the end of its first meeting three more clubs were planned — **United Services,
  Cadets and Rewa** — and together they formed the Fiji RFU, "although played only in Suva."
  **Sheehan became chairman.** Governor **Sir Ernest Bickham Sweet-Escott** donated the **Escott
  Shield**, won by the Pacific Club, "and nearly 90 years later is still competed for by Suva Union
  clubs." (JSFD independently: the Suva-based Fiji Rugby Football Union was founded **by New Zealand
  expatriates**.)
  → **Otago again.** `12-new-zealand.md` Gen 0: the province that walked out of New Zealand's founding
  meeting in 1892. Its former captain founds Fiji's union twenty-one years later, as a plumber on a
  hotel site.
- **December 1913:** the All Blacks played a friendly at **Albert Park** on the way back from
  California. (FRU narrative panel; the NZ Rugby Museum's "On This Day" has 13 December 1913.)
- **1914:** "a so called '**native competition**' began under the leadership of **Ratu Epeli Ganilau**
  and the first clubs were **Taipou, Tarirere, Hill and Ofisa (Police) RFC**."
- **1915:** businessman **J. J. Davies** presented the **Davies Cup**, first won by Gaunavou
  (Tarirere). "The same year a **Fiji Native Union** was begun and **became affiliated to the Fiji
  RFU**." → Affiliated, not merged. Two unions, one colony, for thirty years.
- **⭐ 1924 — the first Test.** FRU: "Fiji's first overseas match was in **Apia, Samoa, in August
  1924** while the team was on its way to Tonga. The match was played at **7am to allow Samoan
  opponents time to get to work afterwards** and was played on a pitch with **a large tree on the
  halfway line**. Fiji played in all black and won **6–0**." The narrative panel dates it **18 August
  1924**, adds that Fiji were "praised for their speed and agility," Samoa described as "the big,
  sturdy men of Apia," and quotes the verdict: "the game was **the cleanest and finest exhibition of
  football played in Samoa**."
  → Fiji's first Test was against **Samoa**, in Samoa, at dawn, around a tree — and both of those
  countries get their own chapters in this section. Hand it forward to ch. 15.
- **1924–26:** the Tonga leg — nine matches, **won seven, drew one, lost one** — "began the healthy
  relationship with the Tonga Rugby Union that continues to this day." Hand forward to ch. 16.
- **1926:** first visit by an overseas side, **Auckland University College**; Tonga also visited; and
  "**for the first time Fiji played in their present strip of white jersey, palm tree badge, and black
  shorts**."
- **⭐ 1928 — the cricket pitches.** A Fiji schools competition started, "but it was not until **1939**
  that the **Fiji Schools Union** was established. Up to this time, **all rugby took place in the
  Suva-Nausori area**. A union statement made it clear why: "**It is very difficult to establish the
  game outside Suva because almost every suitable ground already has a concrete cricket pitch at the
  centre.**""
  → **The cricket-club-incubator motif, inverted.** In Montevideo, Buenos Aires, Valparaíso and
  Melbourne the cricket club *hatched* the winter game. In Fiji the cricket square physically
  occupied the middle of every playable field and **held rugby out of the country for a generation**.
- **1938:** "**Fijians played in boots for the first time** in honour of the first **New Zealand Maori**
  team to visit Fiji. There was still a tendency to take off boots during the match and throw them to
  touch-line." The Māori beat the Fiji 2nd XV and Fiji Europeans (note: **a separate European
  representative side still existed in 1938**) before squaring the three-Test series.
- **⭐ The Deans Trophy was a gift from the Māori.** FRU's schools page: "The original Deans Trophy was
  **donated by the Maori All Blacks team that toured Fiji in 1938** to be competed for by the
  Secondary Schools of Fiji." The competition, "introduced in **1939**, is **the oldest competitive
  tournament in Fiji rugby**. It was first won by **Queen Victoria School**." RKS first won it in
  **1961**, Lelean Memorial in **1948**, Marist Brothers in **1965**.
  ⚠️ **Do not give title totals.** The FRU's own page (QVS 14 and five draws; RKS 16 and four) and
  secondary summaries (QVS 31 comprising 25 wins and six draws) disagree outright. Give the first-win
  years and say RKS and QVS have dominated it.
  → Cross-link `12-new-zealand.md` Gen 3/4: the Māori side, the one New Zealand kept leaving at home
  to protect the South African fixture, is the side that donated the trophy Fijian schools still play
  for.

## GEN 2 — Barefoot (1939–1954)

- **⭐⭐ 1939 — the tour nobody has repeated.** FRU: "Fiji toured New Zealand for the first time. With
  many players still preferring to **play barefoot**, the Fijians played with a care-free spirit,
  romancing the crowds with their unpredictable running game. Incredibly, they also created history by
  becoming **the first team to go through a full tour of New Zealand unbeaten, winning seven and
  drawing one, a record that stands to this day**."
  Last match: the tourists "**outclass the Maoris 14–4 in Hamilton**", and the *Waikato Times* called
  it "the most brilliant exhibition of football seen in Hamilton for many years" — "Almost uncanny in
  handling the ball, lightning in the pace of their sprinting, relentless in their dive tackling…and
  all the time pursuing methods of bright, open football, the Fijians gave a sparkling display and
  thrilled the large crowd." The paper suggested "**Fiji is destined to play a big part in world
  rugby**" — and the FRU's own gloss adds the sting: "unaware of course that it would be the
  abbreviated seven-a-side code that Fiji would eventually rule as their own."
  ⚠️ Secondary accounts say eight matches over August–September; the FRU says seven wins and a draw.
  **Say "seven wins and a draw, unbeaten" and do not state a match total** beyond that arithmetic.
- **The captain.** **Ratu Sir George Cakobau** captained the 1939 side. He is the great-grandson of the
  Cakobau who ceded Fiji in 1874, and he later became Governor-General. He also, before the tour,
  decided the team needed a war dance to answer the haka, and approached **Ratu Bola**, high chief of
  the warrior clan of **Navusaradave in Bau**, who taught them the **cibi**.
  ⚠️ **SOURCING WARNING — the cibi story is currently wiki-derived.** The FRU history page has section
  *headings* "The origins of the cibi" and "The meaning of Teivovo" but **no body text under them** —
  the panels are empty in the served HTML. Until a non-wiki source is found, **the cibi's teaching by
  Ratu Bola must be attributed, not asserted** ("the story Fijian rugby tells about itself is…").
  Cakobau's captaincy is safe. See STILL UNSOURCED below.
- **The war.** Kanemasu et al. (2017), citing Lal (1992): Fiji "has a long history of military 'muscle
  trade' originating from its involvement in World Wars, especially World War II, where **8,000
  Fijians joined the Allied forces in the Solomon Islands, and gained a reputation for bravery**."
  Then "the Malayan War, when Fiji sent **1,600 troops** to the Commonwealth forces."
  → The 1939 tourists came home to this. The same villages, the same age group, in the same decade.
- **⭐ 1945 — the two unions become one.** FRU: "**The Native Rugby Union had ceased to exist**, and by
  1953 Fiji and Suva had separate unions." JSFD puts it plainly: "**In 1945 the two separate unions
  merged.**" Thirty years after the Native Union was founded, and forty-four years after the church
  split itself in two, rugby put itself back together. *The church did not: the Methodist Church in
  Fiji has run Fijian and Indian divisions ever since.*
- **1951:** second tour of New Zealand — played 15, won 8, drew 2, lost 5.
- **⭐ 1952 and 1954 — Fiji rescues Australian rugby.** FRU: "**Fiji's first tour of Australia helps
  the Australian Rugby Union recover from the brink of bankruptcy.** The Test series is drawn 1–1 in
  front of record crowds." And 1954: "The second tour of Australia again sees record crowds. The Test
  series is drawn 1–1."
  ⚠️ The bankruptcy claim is the **FRU's own**, uncorroborated. Attribute it ("the Fiji union's own
  history claims…"). The drawn series and the crowds are safe.
  → **Cross-link `13-australia.md` Gen 3**, the thin 1950s, and note the 69-year arithmetic: Fiji's
  1954 win over Australia was not matched again until **17 September 2023**.

## GEN 3 — The Colony Leaves (1955–1976)

All FRU chronology unless noted.
- **1957:** Fiji tour New Zealand, beat the Māori in Dunedin and Wellington, then beat "a strong
  Auckland team **38–17**."
- **1963:** "The Fiji Rugby Football Union is renamed **Fiji Rugby Union**, administering by 1971,
  **750 clubs and 10,000 players in 300 islands over 100,000 square miles of the Pacific**."
  → Print that sentence. It is the answer to Im Thurn: 750 clubs is not a class.
- **1964:** first tour of Europe, "**lose narrowly to Wales 28–22** in a classic encounter that was
  talked about for years." (Cross-link `08-wales.md` — Wales's long winter, Gen 3.)
- **Independence: 10 October 1970**, ninety-six years to the day after cession.
- **1970:** "A rampant Fijian side **destroy the Barbarians 29–9 at Gosforth**."
- **1973 — the president's warning.** FRU president **Ratu Sir Penaia Ganilau**: "There is room for a
  great deal of work in improving grounds and other facilities… **administration is a constant problem
  in many unions** and I would like to see more former players putting their backs into the task… the
  **standard of refereeing has fallen since Independence** and clearly the Union will need to pay
  special attention to this… coaching too is in need of this… only a determined drive to eliminate
  weakness and build on strengths can confirm Fiji as a world power in rugby."
  → Quote it in full. It is a Fijian administrator diagnosing Fijian rugby from inside, in 1973, and
  every complaint in it is still live in 2026. (Ganilau later became the first President of Fiji.)
- **1974:** "A last-minute try sees New Zealand safely through **14–13 over Fiji in Suva**."
- **The ceiling.** Fiji had no seat at the IRB. JSFD: "**In 1986, the FRU became a member of the
  International Rugby Board (IRB), due to pressure from the IRB.**"
  ⚠️ Other accounts put the Tier 2 admissions (Fiji, Argentina, Italy, Japan, Canada, Romania) at
  **1987**. **Hedge to "the mid-1980s"** and attribute the 1986 date and the "due to pressure from the
  IRB" clause to the JSFD paper. Do not state a flat year.
- **The other export starts here.** Kanemasu et al.: "**Recruitment of Fijians into the BA [British
  Army] started in 1961/1962**" (citing Ware, 2012). And peacekeeping: "Fiji's participation in
  peacekeeping operations **started in 1978 with the UN Interim Force in Lebanon**."

## GEN 4 — The Short Game, and the Coup (1977–1991)

- **1977:** "At the **second Hong Kong Sevens**, Fiji triumph in the final over **Marlborough 28–18**."
  (FRU) → **The Melrose line.** `09-scotland.md` Gen 1: sevens was invented at Melrose in 1883 by a
  butcher's apprentice to raise money for a Border mill-town club. Ninety-four years later the game a
  Scottish mill town invented to pay its bills becomes the property of a Pacific archipelago — and
  eventually the thing that wins it an Olympic gold medal. *A format designed for clubs with no money
  found the country with no money.*
- **1982:** Fiji beat a Vancouver XV to begin a **15-match winning streak through to 1984**.
- **1984:** "Fiji **destroy New Zealand 26–0** in the final of the Hong Kong Sevens."
- **⭐⭐ 14 MAY 1987 — THE COUP, AND THE WORLD CUP EIGHT DAYS LATER.** The first coup, deposing
  **Timoci Bavadra**, was led by **Lieutenant Colonel Sitiveni Rabuka**, then third in command of the
  Royal Fiji Military Forces. The inaugural Rugby World Cup kicked off on **22 May 1987**.
  `12-new-zealand.md` Gen 6 already carries the other half of this from the organisers' side: the coup
  "raised real doubts about whether the Fijians could travel, and **Western Samoa was put on standby**
  in case they or anyone else defaulted." **None did.** Fiji went, and reached the **quarter-finals**,
  losing to **France 31–16** on **7 June 1987** — France's fly half **Guy Laporte** controlling it.
  → Tell it from inside: a team of soldiers, policemen and villagers flying out of a country under
  military rule to play in the sport's first world tournament, and reaching the last eight.
  ⚠️ The FRU's own chronology says only "The first World Cup sees Fiji reach the quarter-finals but bow
  out to France." The 31–16 and the date come from tournament reporting; **verify before print.**
- **1991:** "Fiji beat **Zinzan Brooke's New Zealand** in the final of the Hong Kong Sevens after the
  most amazing try in the tournament's history"; and "**The second World Cup is a disaster for Fiji as
  they lose all three pool games and come home early and embarrassed.**" (FRU — the union's own words.)
- **1992:** a third straight Hong Kong Sevens title, beating New Zealand again.

## GEN 5 — Open, and the Leak (1992–2006)

- **1995:** the game goes open (`07-england.md` Gen 5). **"Fiji fail to qualify for the World Cup."**
  (FRU) → The moment professionalism arrives, Fiji is not at the tournament.
- **⭐ 1997 — the Sevens World Cup, Hong Kong.** Fiji beat **South Africa 24–21** in the final, 21–23

## GEN 6 — The Body Trade (2007–2019)

- **⭐ 29 September 2007, La Beaujoire, Nantes — Fiji 38, Wales 34.** A winner-takes-all Pool B match

- **⭐⭐ THE OTHER EXPORT — TELL IT AS ONE TRADE.** From Kanemasu, Molnar et al., *International
  Migration* (2017), accepted manuscript, hand-read:
  - **Peacekeeping from 1978** (UNIFIL, Lebanon), then Sinai, Iraq, the Golan Heights. "A total of
    **1,040 peacekeeping personnel are sent annually**" (RFMF, 2016). "Indeed, **Fiji has sent more
    soldiers on a per capita basis on UN peacekeeping missions than any other nation**" (Siegel and

- **⭐ RIO, 11 AUGUST 2016 — Fiji 43, Great Britain 7.** Deodoro Stadium; **29–0 at half-time**; tries

  Ravouvou**; Dan Norton scored Britain's only try. It was **Fiji's first Olympic medal of any colour,

  reported as the biggest eligibility change since the game went open, and aimed squarely at Fiji,

## THE SOCCER SIDE — the division that never healed

James, Tuidraki and Tanzil, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*, **28 November 2022** (open
access, hand-read):
- "**by the middle of the twentieth century, Indigenous Fijians were gravitating toward rugby, as
  spectators, whereas the Fiji Indians were gravitating toward soccer**."
- The **Fiji Indian Football Association became the de facto controlling body of the sport in 1938** —
  which "reflects not only the community's passion for administering, watching, and playing soccer,
  but also **the role they were assigned by the British during colonialism** as the merchant class,
  the intellectual class, the trade unionists, the schoolteachers, and the politicians."
- **1961:** "Indian" dropped from the regulator's title. **1962:** the national league opened to all
  races.
- On the modern state: successive governments "portrayed it [rugby 7s] as being a **symbol of
  (Indigenous) Fijian achievement and masculinity** — it has been said that it encapsulates the Fijian
  warrior spirit."
→ Note the dates against each other: **soccer's governing body desegregated in 1961–62; rugby's in
1945.** Rugby got there first. Neither changed who plays.

## RATUISM, RELIGION AND RUGBY

Kanemasu and Molnar, "Problematizing the dominant" (abstract, Worcester eprint 3157 — **the PDF is
401; this is an abstract citation**): "**Today's indigenous Fijian culture is often said to be
represented by ratuism, religion and rugby.** Rugby is widely described as Fiji's 'national' sport,
with a considerable degree of social significance. **That rugby is listed alongside two powerful
institutions (ratuism and religion) in the life of indigenous Fijians is a clear indication of the
central cultural importance of the sport.**"

JSFD: "**Rugby union in Fiji is central to the vaka i taukei (indigenous way of life).**"

Close-Barry gives the older formulation of the same trinity: **Vanua** (way of the land), **Lotu**
(Christianity), **Matanitu** (state).
