# Ireland

## Generation 0: Dublin University and the Two Unions (1854–1879)

**15 February 1875. Kennington Oval, London.** The first Irish representative football team in history takes the field against England, and a good many of its players are meeting each other for the first time.

They have been picked from two cities that do not, in any organised sense, yet play the same game as one another. Nine of them come from Trinity College Dublin. Others have travelled down from Belfast. The World Rugby Museum's account of the day is blunt about the arrangement: most of the northerners "had never met or played with their Dublin teammates before."

Three thousand people come to watch. It is twenty a side, as internationals were in those years. England win by two goals and a try to nil.

Ireland has thereby become the third country in the world to play international rugby football — after England and Scotland, and six years before Wales — and it has done so with a team assembled by two rival administrations that had existed, between them, for about eight weeks.

---

The Irish game starts, as so much in this book does, inside a college.

**Dublin University Football Club was founded in 1854**, and it is by some distance the oldest thing in Irish rugby. The club's own history makes the careful claim: that 1854 "gives Trinity a substantial claim to be the oldest rugby club in continuous existence." The qualification is doing real work. **Guy's Hospital FC** in London, founded in **1843** and playing its early football on the Kennington Oval, is older — but it lapsed for some years in the nineteenth century, and Trinity did not.

What they were playing in the 1850s and 1860s was not rugby, or not yet. It had no written laws at all. One member recalled a club that "had no rules, written or unwritten. They just played and ran with the ball, no touch line, no goal lines." Against visiting military sides Trinity would, out of courtesy to their guests, play by **Eton** rules instead. This is the same primordial condition the England chapter found in the 1840s: dozens of local games sharing a name and agreeing on nothing, so that any two clubs meeting had first to negotiate what the afternoon would consist of.

The man who ended it has a decent claim on the title the club gives him — **the father of Irish rugby**.

**Charles Burton Barrington** captained Dublin University for three years, from **1867 to 1870**, and early in **1868**, in a secretary's rooms in the college's Botany Bay, he and a colleague named **Wall** sat down to write the club's laws. They took Rugby School as their model, which was less straightforward than it sounds, because — as the account drily notes — "Rugby [School] itself though had no written rules! They were traditional, like the British Constitution or the Secrets of Free Masonry."

So Trinity wrote down a tradition that had never been written down, and then did the thing that actually mattered. At a club meeting on **17 October 1868** it was recorded that "we have forwarded to the principal leading clubs the rules by which we play, in the hope that for the future we may be able to secure players thoroughly acquainted with the science of the game."

That is an act of institution-building, not of sport. A college club posted a rulebook around the country so that it would have somebody to play. Everything Irish rugby became grew from the network that answered.

### The clubs, and the two capitals

Answer it did, and quickly, in a pattern that would shape the next century and a half.

**North of Ireland Football Club** was formed in Belfast in **1868**. **Wanderers** followed in Dublin at the end of the decade, founded by former Dublin University men; **Queen's University** in Belfast in **1869**; **Lansdowne** and **Dungannon** in **1873**. The game spread through colleges, royal schools and the professional classes of two cities — a game of institutions with gates on them, which is the condition this book has found nearly everywhere and which Ireland would eventually complicate more interestingly than most.

But note the shape. Rugby in Ireland did not radiate outward from one centre. It grew in **Dublin and Belfast at the same time**, in parallel, among people who did not necessarily know each other. And by the early 1870s those two centres had to decide whether they constituted one game or two.

They decided, at first, that they were two.

### Two unions, and the reason

On **14 December 1874** the **Irish Football Union** was founded in Dublin. Its jurisdiction ran over the clubs of Leinster, Munster and parts of Ulster.

Its founding meeting is more interesting for who was missing than for who attended. Dublin University, Wanderers, Lansdowne, Bray, the Engineers, Portora Royal School, Dungannon Royal School and Monaghan were all represented. **North of Ireland FC — the most prominent club in Belfast — was not.**

Within weeks the Belfast clubs had a union of their own: the **Northern Football Union of Ireland**, controlling the Belfast area. And the World Rugby Museum is explicit about why it was created — to "make sure that northern clubs be proper represented in the side."

That is worth sitting with, because it establishes the terms of everything that follows. The first serious division in Irish rugby was not about religion, and it was not about the national question, neither of which was short of energy in the 1870s. It was about **selection** — about whether a body meeting in Dublin could be trusted to pick Belfast players for an Ireland team. The north's answer was to build an institution that would make ignoring it impossible.

And the remedy it chose is the one that matters. Belfast did not form a rival *country*. It formed a rival *union*, in order to be properly represented in a single Irish side. The dispute was over the terms of inclusion, and both parties took the existence of one Ireland team as the thing worth arguing about.

Five years later, in **1879**, the two unions agreed to amalgamate. The **Irish Rugby Football Union** was constituted with branches in **Ulster, Leinster and Munster** — a governing body for the whole island, built out of a quarrel about who got picked.

### What this generation leaves behind

A single union, seven years older than the border it would one day have to survive.

By 1879 Irish rugby had a written code that a college had drafted and mailed out; clubs in both of the island's principal cities; a Test record consisting mostly of defeats; and one governing body covering all thirty-two counties, arrived at not by consensus but by a northern revolt and a southern climbdown.

It also had a habit that would prove more durable than any of its results. Twice in five years — in the assembling of that first side at the Oval, and in the settlement of 1879 — Irish rugby had been faced with a straightforward division and had chosen, both times, to build a structure that contained it rather than to split along it.

Nothing in the 1870s suggests anyone thought this was a principle. It was administrative convenience, arrived at by men who mostly shared a religion, a class and a set of schools, and who found a single Ireland team obviously preferable to two weak ones.

But the machinery they built would be tested, within fifty years, by a question incomparably harder than which Belfast forward deserved a cap. And when it came, the machinery held.

## Generation 1: The Four Provinces (1879–1920)

**10 March 1894. Belfast.** Ireland beat Wales, and having already taken England at Blackheath and Scotland a fortnight earlier, they have won the **Triple Crown** for the first time.

It has been a long time coming. Ireland lost **nine consecutive matches between 1883 and 1887** and won only **three of their first sixteen** between 1883 and 1889. The World Rugby Museum's verdict on the 1894 side is that their pack "proved too strong for the other countries" — the first appearance of a characterisation that will still be attached to Irish teams a century later.

Look at who is on the field. Of the eighteen players used across the three matches, **thirteen came from three Dublin clubs** — Wanderers, Dublin University and Bective Rangers — and **five from Ulster**. The captain is **Edmund Forrest**. And in the 1890s, rugby in Ireland is "primarily a game for the Protestant middle class": the only Catholic in Forrest's side is **Thomas Crean**, a Dublin medical student who will go to South Africa and win a **Victoria Cross** in the Boer War.

A team of Protestant professional men from two cities, winning a British championship in Belfast, six years before an Irish nationalist revival will begin describing their sport as a foreign game.

That is the whole of the Irish rugby question in one afternoon, and this generation is where every part of it is set.

---

### The four provinces

The structural decision of the era is the quiet one.

The IRFU of 1879 had branches in Ulster, Leinster and Munster. In **1886** a fourth was created for **Connacht**, and that "helped to seal the quadripartite nature of the IRFU."

The significance is in what the four are. Irish rugby organised itself on the island's **cultural provinces** — Ulster, Leinster, Munster, Connacht — "rather than the country's administrative areas, like the 26 counties in the Republic and six counties in Northern Ireland." Those units are medieval. They long predate the state, and two of them straddle the line that will be drawn across the island in 1921: Ulster the province has nine counties, Northern Ireland has six.

Nobody in 1886 was future-proofing anything. They were adopting the map Irish people already used. But it meant that when a border did arrive, the game's internal geography simply did not acknowledge it — and a governing body whose subdivisions cut across a frontier is very much harder to partition than one whose subdivisions line up with it.

### What kind of game, and for whom

Everywhere else in Ireland the picture is the familiar one from the English and Scottish chapters. Rugby is a game of Protestant professional families, of the universities, and of a defined set of fee-paying schools in Dublin and Belfast. It arrived through those institutions and it stayed inside them.

The historian **Liam O'Callaghan**, whose *Rugby in Munster* is the first book-length treatment of the Irish game, states the rule and its exception in a single line: "The generalization which sees rugby in Ireland as a homogenously middle-class pursuit **save for a degree of proletarian infiltration in Limerick**, is broadly true."

That exception is the most instructive thing in this chapter, and it is instructive because it comes with a control.

### Limerick and Cork

Two cities, one province, one sport, the same decade, the same British-derived origin — and opposite outcomes that have lasted a hundred and forty years.

In **Limerick**, from the **late 1880s**, figures around **Garryowen** — "an improbable trio of men of widely different education, ethos and political inclinations" — organised **Sunday junior rugby** in the city. O'Callaghan's finding is that this "facilitated the expansion of rugby in the parishes and working class areas of Limerick's inner city." Saturday was a working day. A Sunday fixture is the difference between a sport a labouring man can play and one he can only read about. Around those parish teams grew what O'Callaghan calls a masculine and parish identity, and a game "much at odds with the professed ideals of contemporary Muscular Christians and gentleman amateurs."

In **Cork**, forty miles away, none of it happened. And the reason O'Callaghan gives is not geography, or wealth, or religion, or temperament:

> "Sunday rugby could have taken off in Cork too **if there had been Godfathers in the senior clubs to organise it; but there were not**, and a golden opportunity to broaden the game across the social classes of Cork was missed."

So Cork's rugby stayed what it had started as — "mainly played by members of the professions living in the wealthy suburbs of the city" — and Limerick's became a game of the inner city. **Rugby in Limerick was a game of the inner city, while in Cork it had a clear suburban bias**, and the variable was who could be bothered to arrange fixtures on the one day working men were free.

This book has argued from Montevideo onwards that a sport's class character is produced rather than inherited. Munster is the place where that argument can be tested against a near-identical control, and it survives the test. Nothing about the founders differed. The fixture list did.

One further detail, for the England chapter's sake: the culture that grew in Limerick — "characterised by competitiveness and toughness" — was, O'Callaghan notes, "unique in Ireland but could also be observed in **Northern England and France**." The resemblance is not a coincidence of temperament. It is what the game becomes when working men get hold of it.

### The other ban

There is a second institution shaping who played what in Ireland, and it is easy to caricature.

The **Gaelic Athletic Association** barred its members from playing "foreign games," rugby among them. It is tempting to write this as nationalism versus empire, with rugby cast as the garrison sport, and there is enough truth in that to be dangerous. O'Callaghan's account is more careful and more useful: the "attachment of politico-cultural significance to different sports in Ireland was an **evolutionary, contested process**, the success of which was **both regionally and chronologically varied**," and the ban "attained extreme ideological importance only in the decades **after** independence."

Which is to say it was neither total nor uniform, and it hardened later than the story suggests. That is also how a nationalist city like Limerick could be, simultaneously and without much apparent strain, a rugby city.

### 1899, and then nothing

Ireland won the Championship again in **1896** and **1899**. And then the tap closed. There would not be another Triple Crown until **1948** — a gap of **forty-nine years**.

The last stretch of this generation is taken up by a war that the Irish game entered on the same terms as the English one and left in a country that was no longer the same place.

O'Callaghan's study of Irish rugby and the First World War records that when war broke out "the response of the IRFU was much in keeping with that of their English counterpart: fixtures were cancelled and clubs were encouraged to urge enlistment among players and members." But he is careful about what followed: though "rugby players in significant numbers signed up, the motivations for enlistment were complex and contingent upon multiple factors, many of which may not have been rugby-related." Irish rugby's relationship with that war was, in his word, complex — which in an Irish context is not a euphemism but a precise description.

The dead include **Basil Maclear**, killed in May 1915 by a gunshot wound to the neck; **Ernest Deane**, September 1915; **Robbie Smyth**, 1916; **George McAllan**, 1918; **William John Beatty**, 1919. And **Jasper Brett**, who had played in the 1914 match against Wales remembered as the "Battle of Balmoral," survived Gallipoli, and died in Dublin in **February 1917**, by his own hand.

Accounts differ on the total: one gallery names six internationals, another puts the figure at nine dead as a result of the war, seven of them on active service.

### What this generation leaves behind

A game with its shape fully set, and a country about to change underneath it.

By 1920 Irish rugby had four provincial branches drawn on a map older than any border; one Triple Crown, twenty-six years in the past; a governing body run by Protestant professional men from Dublin and Belfast who understood each other perfectly well; a nationalist sporting movement that had declared the game foreign; and, in one city on the Shannon, a thriving working-class version of the sport that fitted none of the categories anybody had prepared.

Within two years the island would be partitioned, and the union would have to decide whether it was one thing or two.
