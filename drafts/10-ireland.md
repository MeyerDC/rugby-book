# Ireland

## Generation 0: Dublin University and the Two Unions (1854–1879)

**15 February 1875. Kennington Oval, London.** The first Irish representative football team in history takes the field against England, and a good many of its players are meeting each other for the first time.

They have been picked from two cities that do not, in any organised sense, yet play the same game as one another. Nine of them come from Trinity College Dublin. Others have travelled down from Belfast. The World Rugby Museum's account of the day is blunt about the arrangement: most of the northerners "had never met or played with their Dublin teammates before."

Three thousand people come to watch. It is twenty a side, as internationals were in those years. England win by two goals and a try to nil.

Ireland has thereby become the third country in the world to play international rugby football — after England and Scotland, and six years before Wales — and it has done so with a team assembled by two rival administrations that had existed, between them, for about eight weeks.

---

The Irish game starts, as so much in this book does, inside a college.

**Dublin University Football Club was founded in 1854**, and it is by some distance the oldest thing in Irish rugby. The club's own history makes the careful claim: that 1854 "gives Trinity a substantial claim to be the oldest rugby club in continuous existence." The qualification is doing real work. **Guy's Hospital FC** in London, founded in **1843** and playing its early football on the Kennington Oval, is older — but it lapsed for some years in the nineteenth century, and Trinity did not.

What they were playing in the 1850s and 1860s was not rugby, or not yet. It had no written laws at all. One member recalled a club that "had no rules, written or unwritten. They just played and ran with the ball, no touch line, no goal lines." Against visiting military sides Trinity would, out of courtesy to their guests, play by **Eton** rules instead. This is the same primordial condition the England chapter found in the 1840s: dozens of local games sharing a name and agreeing on nothing, so that any two clubs meeting had first to negotiate what the afternoon would consist of.

The man who ended it has a decent claim on the title the club gives him — **the father of Irish rugby**.

**Charles Burton Barrington** captained Dublin University for three years, from **1867 to 1870**, and early in **1868**, in a secretary's rooms in the college's Botany Bay, he and a colleague named **Wall** sat down to write the club's laws. They took Rugby School as their model, which was less straightforward than it sounds, because — as the account drily notes — "Rugby [School] itself though had no written rules! They were traditional, like the British Constitution or the Secrets of Free Masonry."

So Trinity wrote down a tradition that had never been written down, and then did the thing that actually mattered. At a club meeting on **17 October 1868** it was recorded that "we have forwarded to the principal leading clubs the rules by which we play, in the hope that for the future we may be able to secure players thoroughly acquainted with the science of the game."

That is an act of institution-building, not of sport. A college club posted a rulebook around the country so that it would have somebody to play. Everything Irish rugby became grew from the network that answered.

### The clubs, and the two capitals

Answer it did, and quickly, in a pattern that would shape the next century and a half.

**North of Ireland Football Club** was formed in Belfast in **1868**. **Wanderers** followed in Dublin at the end of the decade, founded by former Dublin University men; **Queen's University** in Belfast in **1869**; **Lansdowne** and **Dungannon** in **1873**. The game spread through colleges, royal schools and the professional classes of two cities — a game of institutions with gates on them, which is the condition this book has found nearly everywhere and which Ireland would eventually complicate more interestingly than most.

But note the shape. Rugby in Ireland did not radiate outward from one centre. It grew in **Dublin and Belfast at the same time**, in parallel, among people who did not necessarily know each other. And by the early 1870s those two centres had to decide whether they constituted one game or two.

They decided, at first, that they were two.

### Two unions, and the reason

On **14 December 1874** the **Irish Football Union** was founded in Dublin. Its jurisdiction ran over the clubs of Leinster, Munster and parts of Ulster.

Its founding meeting is more interesting for who was missing than for who attended. Dublin University, Wanderers, Lansdowne, Bray, the Engineers, Portora Royal School, Dungannon Royal School and Monaghan were all represented. **North of Ireland FC — the most prominent club in Belfast — was not.**

Within weeks the Belfast clubs had a union of their own: the **Northern Football Union of Ireland**, controlling the Belfast area. And the World Rugby Museum is explicit about why it was created — to "make sure that northern clubs be proper represented in the side."

That is worth sitting with, because it establishes the terms of everything that follows. The first serious division in Irish rugby was not about religion, and it was not about the national question, neither of which was short of energy in the 1870s. It was about **selection** — about whether a body meeting in Dublin could be trusted to pick Belfast players for an Ireland team. The north's answer was to build an institution that would make ignoring it impossible.

And the remedy it chose is the one that matters. Belfast did not form a rival *country*. It formed a rival *union*, in order to be properly represented in a single Irish side. The dispute was over the terms of inclusion, and both parties took the existence of one Ireland team as the thing worth arguing about.

Five years later, in **1879**, the two unions agreed to amalgamate. The **Irish Rugby Football Union** was constituted with branches in **Ulster, Leinster and Munster** — a governing body for the whole island, built out of a quarrel about who got picked.

### What this generation leaves behind

A single union, seven years older than the border it would one day have to survive.

By 1879 Irish rugby had a written code that a college had drafted and mailed out; clubs in both of the island's principal cities; a Test record consisting mostly of defeats; and one governing body covering all thirty-two counties, arrived at not by consensus but by a northern revolt and a southern climbdown.

It also had a habit that would prove more durable than any of its results. Twice in five years — in the assembling of that first side at the Oval, and in the settlement of 1879 — Irish rugby had been faced with a straightforward division and had chosen, both times, to build a structure that contained it rather than to split along it.

Nothing in the 1870s suggests anyone thought this was a principle. It was administrative convenience, arrived at by men who mostly shared a religion, a class and a set of schools, and who found a single Ireland team obviously preferable to two weak ones.

But the machinery they built would be tested, within fifty years, by a question incomparably harder than which Belfast forward deserved a cap. And when it came, the machinery held.
