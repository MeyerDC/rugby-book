# Wales

## The Generations of Welsh Rugby

Researched. And Wales is the answer to England — the **single most important counter-example in the sport.**

Because here's the thing: **Welsh rugby started exactly like English rugby.** Introduced at **Lampeter College** via Rugby School rules. Spread by ex-pupils of Welsh colleges and students coming home from English universities. Middle-class, educated, respectable. Same seed.

And then it hit **the coalfield** — and something happened that happened nowhere else in Britain. The game **crossed the class divide and kept going**, into the pits and the steelworks and the chapel towns, and it became the **national identity of a people**, and Wales *stayed in rugby union.*

They did not walk out like Huddersfield. **They stayed inside the gentlemen's church and beat the gentlemen at their own game** — while the very same amateurism that England exported was **bleeding their best players north for a hundred years.**

That's the tension in every single generation: **a working-class sport, governed by an amateur rulebook written for men who didn't need the money.**

---

**Gen 0 — Lampeter and the Colleges (1850s–1880)**
Rugby arrives at **Lampeter College** in the mid-19th century, under Rugby School rules — carried by **English public school influence** and by students returning from English universities into the booming industrial hubs of South Wales. **Neath RFC** (1871) is widely recognised as the first Welsh club. In **September 1875** the **South Wales Football Union** is founded at **Brecon**, explicitly to play the principal clubs of the West of England. Middle-class, collegiate, and — at this point — indistinguishable from England.

**Gen 1 — The Union and the Coalfield (1881–1899)**
**19 February 1881, Blackheath:** Newport secretary **Richard Mullock** scratches together the first Welsh team to play England. They lose by seven goals, a drop goal and six tries to nil — **82–0** in modern scoring. Three weeks later, on **12 March 1881**, eleven clubs meet at the **Castle Hotel, Neath**, and found the **Welsh Rugby Union.** And then the coalfield arrives: miners, steelworkers, whole valley communities pour in, and the game **crosses the class divide** in a way it never did in England. First **Triple Crown in 1893.** Crowds over 30,000. And in **1895, two hundred miles north, Huddersfield happens** — and the Northern Union immediately starts hunting in Wales.

**Gen 2 — The First Golden Era, and the Bleeding (1900–1914)**
Wales **dominates world rugby.** Four Triple Crowns, an eleven-match unbeaten run, and the **first Grand Slam ever won by anybody, in 1908** — then 1909 and 1911. They invent the **four three-quarter system**, and the entire rugby world copies it. And on **16 December 1905, at Cardiff Arms Park**, a try by **Teddy Morgan** beats Dave Gallaher's **All Blacks 3–0** — the **only match the Originals lost on the entire tour**, and arguably the single fixture that made rugby a global sport. Meanwhile: the **Northern Union is offering Welsh miners jobs, wages and six shillings a match**, and the best players in the valleys start going north. On **New Year's Day 1908, in Aberdare**, Wales plays the All Golds — the **world's first official rugby league international.**

**Gen 3 — The Long Winter (1919–1968)**
**Coal collapses.** The post-war demand vanishes, mass unemployment hits the valleys, and **the people emigrate.** The industry that built Welsh rugby dies, and Welsh rugby dies with it — decades of failure, and a haemorrhage of players north that never stops. A brief revival in the **1950 and 1952 Grand Slams**, and Cliff Morgan, but the structure is broken.

**Gen 4 — The Second Golden Era (1969–1979)**
**Gareth Edwards. Barry John. JPR Williams. Gerald Davies. Phil Bennett. Mervyn Davies.** Grand Slams in **1971, 1976 and 1978.** The **1971 Lions**, with **eleven Welshmen in the Test side**, remain the **only Lions team ever to win a series in New Zealand.** By common consent one of the greatest teams the sport has ever produced — and a national mythology that Wales has been living inside ever since.

**Gen 5 — The Pits Close (1980–2002)**
**Thatcher. The 1984–85 miners' strike. The valleys are deindustrialised.** The economic base that produced the greatest rugby team on earth is dismantled — and Welsh rugby falls off a cliff. The exodus to league becomes a flood (**Jonathan Davies** to Wigan). Then professionalism arrives in **1995**, and Wales has no money to compete. **South Africa 96, Wales 13.** Graham Henry arrives as "the Great Redeemer."

**Gen 6 — The Regions (2003–2019)**
The WRU **abolishes the professional club game** and replaces it with **regions** — killing the elite status of Cardiff, Swansea, Llanelli, Newport, Neath and Pontypool, and creating a wound that has never closed. And yet: **Grand Slams in 2005, 2008, 2012 and 2019**, World Cup semi-finals in **2011 and 2019**, and the **Warren Gatland** era.

**Gen 7 — The Crisis (2020–2026)**
Financial collapse, a governing body engulfed in scandal, players threatening to **strike before a Six Nations match**, regions running out of money — and a national team that fell into **the worst losing run in its history** and out of the world's top ten.

---

Say the word and we start at Gen 0.

## Generation 0: The Vicar, the Village Game, and the Colleges (1603–1880)

Before there was rugby in Wales, there was **cnapan.**

And I'm starting there, because Wales — like Georgia, and like nowhere else in this entire conversation — had a **folk ancestor.** A game that was already in the blood before any Englishman arrived with a rulebook.

---

### Cnapan

A wooden ball, boiled in tallow to make it slippery. Two parishes — sometimes **hundreds, even thousands of men**, some on foot, some **on horseback** — and open country between two villages. The object: get the ball back to your own parish church. No pitch. No time limit. No referee.

It was played in **west Wales** — Pembrokeshire, Carmarthenshire, Ceredigion — and our earliest full account comes from **George Owen of Henllys**, in his *Description of Pembrokeshire*, written in **1603**, who recorded that it had been hugely popular in the county since, in his lovely phrase, *"greate antiquitie."*

Great antiquity. **In 1603.** The game was already ancient when Shakespeare was alive.

It was one of the traditional Shrovetide and Eastertide ball games of the British Isles — the same family of festival mob-football that, in England, the public schools would eventually tame and codify into rugby and soccer.

And there's a detail in the record that you'll recognise instantly from a very different mountain range: cnapan was **suppressed by English authorities in the 17th century for inciting "riotous assemblies"** — and it functioned, the historians argue, as a form of **Welsh cultural resistance to Tudor assimilation.**

*(Remember the 1890 rugby match in Buenos Aires where President Juárez Celman's police arrested both teams and all 2,500 spectators because they thought the match was a political meeting in disguise? Same instinct. Three centuries earlier. **A mass gathering of the common people, playing a violent folk game, reads to a nervous state as sedition.** Every time.)*

So hold this, because it's the deep foundation: **Wales, like Georgia with lelo burti, had a violent, communal, parish-against-parish mass ball game centuries before codified rugby existed.** The bodies were already trained. The instinct — *my village against yours, everyone plays, carry the thing home* — was already there.

The difference from Georgia is that in Wales, the folk game and the codified game never formally fused. Cnapan faded. But when rugby arrived in the industrial valleys two centuries later and every town and village immediately formed a team to fight the next town and village — **that was cnapan's ghost, wearing a jersey.**

---

### The vicar

Now the codified game, and it arrives — as it arrived in Montevideo and Grahamstown and Bucharest — **in the luggage of an educated man.**

**The Reverend Rowland Williams.** An eminent theologian, appointed in **1850** as Vice-Principal and Professor of Theology at **St David's College, Lampeter** — a small Anglican institution in deepest Ceredigion, founded in 1822 to train young Welshmen for ordination.

Williams had been a student at **King's College, Cambridge** — and the story goes that he'd acquired his knowledge of the Rugby School game from **Arthur Pell**, an Old Rugbeian who had attempted to introduce the school's laws at **Cambridge in 1839**, with Williams watching.

*(Pause on that lineage. **Rugby School → Pell → Cambridge, 1839 → Williams → Lampeter, 1850.** One man watching another man try to teach a schoolboys' game, and carrying the memory west for a decade. This is exactly how the game travelled everywhere — not as a rulebook but as a **memory in one head.** The same way it got to Buenos Aires and Valparaíso.)*

When Williams arrived at Lampeter, he was dismayed by the lack of physical activity among the ordinands. So — in perfect keeping with **Dr Arnold's "Muscular Christianity"**, the healthy-mind-healthy-body doctrine of the English public schools — he introduced **cricket, croquet, Eton Fives, and rugby.**

A theology professor, teaching trainee vicars to maul, as a form of **spiritual hygiene.**

And the honest scholarly caveat, which I'll give you rather than the tidy legend: historians like Prescott question whether Williams — who at best had *briefly witnessed* a restricted version of the game ten years earlier at Cambridge — really "introduced rugby" in any meaningful sense. What's beyond doubt is that he **built the sporting environment in which it flourished.** The earliest games were internal house matches, and matches of an *"undefined football nature under local rules"* against local schools.

Which — once again — is the Rugby School story, and the Buenos Aires story, and the Green Point story. **There was no settled game to introduce. There was a culture of playing, out of which a game slowly condensed.**

Lampeter's claim: **the oldest rugby team in Wales, from around 1850.**

---

### 1866: the first match

The Lampeter students had a problem that will sound familiar from every remote rugby outpost in this conversation: **nobody to play.**

The solution arrived when other Welsh colleges took up the game — above all **Llandovery College**, founded 1848.

And so, in **1866**, the **first recorded rugby match in Wales**: **Lampeter College versus Llandovery College**, at Lampeter.

**The score is not known.**

I love that. The founding fixture of Welsh rugby — the national sport of the nation, the game of Gareth Edwards and the Arms Park and eighty thousand voices singing hymns — and nobody wrote down the score. *(Some accounts push inter-school games back to 1856; either way, the paper trail starts long after the ball, as it always does.)*

---

### The graduates come home

Through the **1870s**, the mechanism you've seen on four continents did its work: **ex-pupils of the Welsh colleges, and students returning from English colleges and universities, settled in the larger industrial hubs of South Wales** — and founded clubs.

- **Neath, 1871** — widely recognised as the first Welsh club, founded by a consortium of ten enthusiasts. Their captain, **T.P. Whittington**, would win an international cap in 1873 — **for Scotland.** *(The first great Welsh club's first captain was a Scottish international. Nothing in this sport is ever tidy.)*
- **Swansea and Llanelli, 1872.** The **first recorded club match in Wales: Swansea v Neath, 7 February 1872.**
- **Brecon, 1874. Newport, 1874. Mountain Ash, 1875.**
- **Cardiff and Merthyr, 1876** — Cardiff itself formed from a merger of three clubs: Glamorgan, Tredegarville and Wanderers.
- **Pontypool**, whose origins reach back to 1868.

So far, so English. Colleges, graduates, respectable young men. If the story stopped here, Wales would be Chile — a pleasant collegiate habit in a few towns.

It didn't stop here. Because look **where** those clubs were planted.

**Neath. Merthyr. Mountain Ash. Pontypool.** Steel towns. Coal towns. The graduates didn't settle in cathedral closes — **they settled where the work was**, because industrial South Wales in the 1870s was one of the great economic engines of the world, and everything and everyone was being pulled into the coalfield.

**The game was planted directly into the blast furnace.** Not in a suburb the workers couldn't reach — in the very towns where the workers lived. That accident of geography is the whole reason Wales turned out different from England, and it happens right here, in Gen 0, before a single international is played.

And underneath the famous clubs, the scale of what was coming is visible in one glorious list. In the 1880s, the town of **Llanelli alone** hosted not just Llanelli RFC but: Gower Road, Seasiders, Morfa Rangers, Prospect Place Rovers, **Wern Foundry**, Cooper Mills Rangers, New Dock Strollers, Vauxhall Juniors, Moonlight Rovers and Gilbert Street Rovers.

**Wern Foundry.** A works team. **Moonlight Rovers.** A pub team. Scratch sides forming and dissolving in every street of a tinplate town.

*(That is Brașov's aircraft factory. That is Tucumán's sugar mills. That is the Wanderers of Valparaíso. Except in Wales it's happening in **every town simultaneously**, fifteen years after the game arrived.)*

---

### 1875–1880: the first institutions

**September 1875, Brecon:** the **South Wales Football Club** is founded — with the stated intention of playing matches against the principal clubs of the **West of England**, and with an explicit declaration: *the Rugby rules will be the adopted code.*

Note what that founding document is: **a choice.** Wales chose rugby, formally, in writing, in 1875 — at a moment when association football was conquering the north of England. It could have gone the other way. It went this way.

**December 1875, Castle Green, Hereford:** the SWFC plays a Hereford XV — the **first representative fixture ever played by a Welsh team.** They wore **black and white**, and won by a single try, scored by Swansea's **A.H. Richardson.**

*(The WRU struck a commemorative black jersey for its 125th anniversary in 2005 — worn when Wales beat Australia at the Millennium Stadium. The first Welsh representative colours were not red.)*

**1878:** the SWFC evolves into the **South Wales Football Union**, taking over competition and representative selection. And crucially, it runs the **South Wales Challenge Cup** — a knockout competition.

And you know, from Yorkshire in 1877, exactly what a **cup competition** does. It makes winning matter. It fills the ground. It drags in the best players a town possesses regardless of what their fathers do. The Yorkshire Cup let the working man in and detonated English rugby within twenty years.

**Wales had its own cup by the late 1870s** — and it did the same thing, with one enormous difference we'll see in Gen 1: when the crisis over money came, **Wales handled it in precisely the opposite way.**

---

### What Gen 0 leaves behind

**A folk ancestor** — cnapan, parish against parish, suppressed as sedition, ancient in 1603 — which meant the communal, everyone-plays, my-village-against-yours instinct predated the codified game by centuries.

**A vicar** — Rowland Williams, carrying a ten-year-old memory of Cambridge into a theological college in 1850, teaching trainee priests to play as an act of Muscular Christianity.

**A first match with no recorded score** (1866), and a first generation of clubs (1871–1876) founded by returning graduates — **but planted, by economic accident, directly in the coal and steel towns** rather than in gentlemen's suburbs.

**A formal choice of code** (1875, Brecon, in writing), a representative team in black and white, and a cup competition already pulling the working man onto the field.

And a gathering pressure. Because by 1880 the informal structure was creaking — a meeting of clubs at the **Tenby Hotel, Swansea, in March 1880** had already begun sketching something bigger — and in London, the RFU was watching this Welsh explosion with a mixture of interest and disdain.

What forced the issue was a catastrophe.

**19 February 1881. Mr Richardson's Field, Blackheath.** A Newport businessman named **Richard Mullock** — a man with no official mandate whatsoever — takes it upon himself to select the first ever Welsh national team, and sends them to play an England side with a full decade of international experience.

Wales lose by **seven goals, one drop goal and six tries to nil.** In modern scoring: roughly **82–0.**

*(Two of the selected players didn't turn up, and the team was largely Oxbridge graduates — the local press savaged the selection as unrepresentative of Welsh strength.)*

Three weeks later, on **12 March 1881**, eleven clubs walk into the **Castle Hotel, Neath**, and found the Welsh Rugby Union.

**The WRU was born out of a humiliation.** Which, given everything that follows, is about the most Welsh origin imaginable.

That's Gen 1. Say the word.

## Generation 1: The Union, the Coalfield, and the Gould Affair (1881–1899)

Wales's international rugby history begins with a team that had **never played together**, many of whom had **never even met before that day.**

**19 February 1881. Mr Richardson's Field, Blackheath.**

The man responsible was **Richard Mullock**, secretary of the Newport Athletic Club — Welsh rugby's "Mr Fixit," a man with his fingers in every sporting pie in the country. He'd helped found Newport Athletic in 1874; he'd been in the room at Oxford in 1880 when the Amateur Athletic Association was formed. And when the South Wales Football Union failed to arrange any international fixtures, **Mullock simply organised one himself.** No mandate. No authority. He picked a Welsh team **off his own back** and sent them to play England.

And he picked them — this is the damning part — on **geography and academic background as much as ability.** The side was stuffed with Oxbridge graduates, captained by an **Australian-born Cambridge undergraduate**, James Bevan. Two selected players didn't turn up. They played out of position. They had no cohesion.

England — who had been playing internationals for a full decade and lost only twice in seventeen Tests — won by **seven goals, one dropped goal and six tries to nil.** In modern scoring: **82–0.**

The local press savaged the selection. The word the WRU's own history uses is *humiliation*.

And here is the sentence that tells you everything about what Wales would become. The response to the hammering **was not embarrassment and retreat. It was organisation.**

**Twenty-one days later — 12 March 1881, the Castle Hotel, Neath** — eleven clubs formed the **Welsh Football Union**: Swansea, Lampeter, Llandeilo, Cardiff, Newport, Llanelli, Merthyr, Llandovery, Brecon, Pontypool and Bangor. Cyril Chambers of Swansea, president. Mullock — the man whose freelance disaster had forced the issue — Honorary Secretary and Treasurer, a post he held until 1892.

*(Two lovely footnotes. The meeting happened on the same day as the fourth **South Wales Challenge Cup final**, in the same town — Cardiff beat Llanelli. The founding of the union was, literally, a side-meeting at a cup final. And **Neath — the oldest club in Wales, in whose town the meeting took place — was not recorded as present.** One theory: the president and secretary of the old South Wales Football Union were both Neath men, and attending would have meant voting their own body, and their own influence, out of existence. Institutional self-interest at the founding moment. It took until the 125th anniversary in 2006 for the WRU to formally acknowledge Neath had almost certainly been in the room.)*

---

### What made Wales different

Now the heart of it — the reason this generation matters more than any Welsh Grand Slam.

The same game, from the same source, planted in the same decade as everywhere else, did something in Wales it did **nowhere else in Britain**: it **crossed the class divide and kept going.**

In England, as we've seen, the game belonged to Old Rugbeians and the professions, and when mill workers flooded in through the Yorkshire Cup, the establishment eventually **amputated them.** In Wales, the clubs proliferated straight into the **coal-mining valleys and working-class communities** — and the middle-class men who'd founded the union simply... kept playing alongside them.

Why? The honest answer is a convergence of accidents:

**Geography.** The clubs of Gen 0 were planted in Neath, Merthyr, Pontypool, Mountain Ash — *in* the industrial towns, not in suburbs apart from them. There was no spatial separation to defend.

**Timing.** The game arrived *simultaneously* with the coal boom. South Wales in the 1870s–90s was filling with migrant workers at extraordinary speed, and rugby was the new thing everyone was doing — it never had time to ossify into a gentleman's possession before the crowd arrived.

**Nation.** And this is the deepest one. The WRU was founded in the same era as Wales's other national institutions — the sources draw the parallel with the National Library. For a small, stateless nation inside a larger one, the rugby team became **the institution through which Welshness was performed.** And a national institution, unlike a class institution, *needs* everybody. By the 1890s, matches were drawing **30,000 to 40,000 spectators** — crowds dominated by miners and steelworkers.

The result, in the sources' own framing: unlike England, where the sport aligned with public schools and aristocracy, Welsh rugby became a game of **resilience, egalitarianism and communal solidarity** — uniting east and west, north and south, Welsh-speaking and English-speaking, across every divide the country possessed.

*(And there's cnapan's ghost again: parish against parish, everyone plays. The valleys didn't learn communal rugby from England. They remembered it.)*

---

### The rise

On the field, it took nine years of losing before the breakthrough.

**1890, Crown Flatt, Dewsbury:** Wales beat England for the first time, at the seventh attempt — a try, then worth **one point**, by **Buller Stadden.**

*(Savour the venue. Dewsbury — a Yorkshire mill town, one of the twenty-two clubs that would walk out at the George Hotel five years later. Wales's first win over England was achieved in the heartland of the coming rebellion.)*

**1893: Wales win the Triple Crown** — beating England, Scotland and Ireland in one season, champions for the first time. The captain was **Arthur Gould** of Newport — remember that name; he's about to become the most important man in this story.

The engine was the **"Big Four"**: Cardiff, Newport, Swansea, Llanelli. Newport lost only **seven matches between 1891 and 1895.** And a generation of genuine stars emerged — Gould, Billy Bancroft, Gwyn Nicholls, Dick Hellings — the first household names of Welsh rugby, playing an innovative game built on the **four three-quarter system** that Wales pioneered and the world would copy.

By the mid-1890s, this team was being described — by *English* reporters — as a side whose combination had never been more perfect.

---

### And then 1895 happened next door

You know what happened in Huddersfield. Now watch what it did to Wales, because this is the hinge of the whole Welsh story.

The RFU's amateurism was, in Wales, a **sociological absurdity.** Welsh rugby's playing base was **miners and labourers who could not afford unpaid absences from work** — the exact class of man the broken-time rules were designed to exclude. The class tension that split England ran **straight through the middle of every Welsh club.**

And the Northern Union knew it. From the moment of the split, the professional clubs of the North began **aggressively poaching in Wales** — offering jobs, wages, and broken-time money, initially six shillings a match. The **James brothers of Swansea** — David and Evan, both Welsh internationals — had already been suspended by the union in **March 1893** for professionalism after joining Broughton Rovers. They were the first drops of what would become a hundred-year haemorrhage.

So Wales faced the same question as Yorkshire: **stay in the gentlemen's church, or walk out with the workers?**

And Wales did **neither.** Wales did something far more cunning. Which brings us to the affair that nearly ended everything.

---

### The Gould Affair

**Arthur Gould.** Newport's centre, Wales's captain, the Triple Crown hero of 1893 — arguably **the greatest player of the Victorian age**, and without question the first superstar of Welsh rugby. A national idol in a way no English player was.

In **1896–97**, a Welsh newspaper launched a **testimonial fund** for him — a public subscription, a thank-you from a grateful nation. The proceeds bought him **a house.**

Under the RFU's amateurism laws, this was **flagrantly, unambiguously professional.** A player materially rewarded for playing rugby. Under the rules that had just torn England in half, Gould should have been banned for life and Wales cast out.

**Scotland and Ireland refused to play Wales over it.** The 1897 Championship was disrupted. The crisis was total: Wales stood on the edge of exactly the schism that had split England — except this time it would split the *international* game.

And then — the decisive moment in Welsh rugby history — **the RFU blinked.**

In **1897, the RFU compromised.** They accepted the testimonial. Wales stayed in the fold. And the historian **Tony Collins** describes the settlement with an honesty no official history will give you: from that day forward, **Welsh rugby union pretended to be amateur, and the RFU pretended to believe them.**

**For the next century.**

That's the deal. That's the whole Welsh dispensation, struck in 1897 and never written down. Wales would keep the forms of amateurism — the language, the committees, the pieties — and underneath, in the valleys, there would be boot money and jobs found for props and testimonials and a thousand quiet accommodations, and **everyone would agree not to look.**

Why did the RFU fold for Gould when it had purged Yorkshire two years earlier? Because it could count. Losing twenty-two northern clubs had been survivable. Losing an entire **nation** — the most passionate rugby country on earth, with 40,000-strong crowds — would have handed the Northern Union half of Britain. The gentlemen enforced their principles **exactly as far as their power reached, and not one inch further.**

*(Which is precisely what they'd done for Andrew Stoddart in 1888, and what they'd do for celebrated internationals throughout the shamateur century. Same rule. Different men. Always.)*

---

### What Gen 1 leaves behind

**A union born of humiliation** — 82–0, then organisation within three weeks. The founding reflex of Welsh rugby: when beaten, build.

**The only place in Britain where the game crossed class and held** — planted in the coal towns by accident, nationalised by 40,000-strong crowds, welded to Welsh identity in the same generation that built the nation's other institutions.

**A first Triple Crown (1893)**, a tactical revolution in the four three-quarter system, and the game's first Welsh superstars.

**A haemorrhage already begun** — the James brothers gone north in 1893, six shillings a match on offer, and every talented miner in the valleys now a target.

**And the Gould compromise of 1897** — the unwritten treaty under which Wales stayed amateur in name only, and the RFU agreed to believe the lie.

That compromise bought Wales something extraordinary. Because with the fiction in place — with the workers *in* the game rather than expelled from it — Wales was about to become **the best rugby nation on the planet.**

**Gen 2** is the First Golden Era: four Triple Crowns, the first Grand Slam ever won by anybody, and the sixteenth of December 1905 — Teddy Morgan, Cardiff Arms Park, and the only match Dave Gallaher's All Blacks ever lost.

Say the word.

## Generation 2: The First Golden Era — and the Song (1900–1914)

**16 December 1905. Cardiff Arms Park.** Forty-seven thousand people.

The All Blacks — the **Originals**, the first southern hemisphere team ever to tour the British Isles — have played twenty-seven matches in three months. They have scored **801 points and conceded 22.** In their last six hundred minutes of rugby, they have not conceded **a single point.** They have already beaten England, Ireland and Scotland. On the morning of the match, *The Times* dismissed Wales entirely and predicted their inevitable defeat.

The Welsh Regiment's band plays *Men of Harlech* as the teams walk out.

The All Blacks perform the **haka.**

And then — in a **pre-planned move**, reputedly instigated by the winger **Teddy Morgan** — the Welsh players, with no musical accompaniment at all, begin to sing ***Hen Wlad Fy Nhadau.***

The crowd joins them. Forty-seven thousand voices.

It is the **first recorded instance of a national anthem being sung before an international sporting event.** Anywhere. In any sport. Ever.

Every anthem you have ever heard before kick-off — Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika at Ellis Park, the Marseillaise at the Stade de France, the whole global ritual of it — descends from **Welsh players answering the haka with a song**, on a December afternoon in 1905.

And think about *which* song. An anthem barely fifty years old, composed in 1856 by a father and son from **Pontypridd** — a valleys mill town. Not an ancient royal hymn. A **people's song**, from the coalfield, sung by a team of the coalfield, at a game the coalfield had made its own.

*(This is what the Gould compromise bought. A team that actually represented its nation — workers included — standing on a field being a nation, out loud. England could never have produced that moment. England's team didn't represent a people; it represented a class.)*

---

### The match

The All Blacks were billed as facing the **"Match of the Century"** — the first meeting of the world's two best teams. Wales hadn't lost at home since 1899.

The Welsh try was not an accident. It was a **rehearsed trick play**, invented by the scrum-half **Dicky Owen** — the "pocket Hercules," a rough, fearless tactical genius from Swansea — and drilled in training beforehand. Owen feinted to the blindside, where Bush, Nicholls and Llewellyn had lined up as decoys, dragging the New Zealand defence with them — then switched and threw a long pass the *other* way. **Cliff Pritchard** scooped it off his bootlaces, drew his man, fed **Rhys Gabe**, who drew his, and released **Teddy Morgan** — who outstripped the covering fullback and dived into the corner.

Three points. Unconverted. The only score of the match.

And then, late on, the moment that has been argued about for a hundred and twenty years.

**Bob Deans**, the big Canterbury centre, took an inside pass from Billy Wallace and burst for the line, with Gabe sprinting across to hit him at full speed. In the mist, from the stands, nobody could see whether he'd made it. The referee, **John Dallas** — a Scot, refereeing in a collar, tie and jacket — arrived after making up thirty yards and ruled Deans **six to twelve inches short.** Scrum to Wales.

**Wales 3, New Zealand 0.** The only defeat of the entire 35-match tour. The first international defeat in New Zealand's history.

Deans telegraphed the *Daily Mail* that same evening insisting he had grounded the ball over the line and been dragged back before the referee arrived. He maintained it for the rest of his short life — **reasserting it from his deathbed in 1908**, aged just twenty-four, struck down after appendicitis. The New Zealand tour manager called it an absolutely fair try. Even **Teddy Morgan himself**, and several other Welsh players, later admitted they thought the claim was credible. Gabe — the man who made the tackle — went to *his* grave insisting Deans was inches short and had made an illegal double movement.

Nobody will ever know. And it doesn't matter, because **Dave Gallaher**, the All Blacks captain, walked into the Welsh changing room afterwards, congratulated them, exchanged jerseys — and said simply that **the best team won.**

*(Two codas. Gallaher died at Passchendaele in 1917, and the Deans grievance became the founding grudge of the greatest rivalry in the sport — Wales beat New Zealand again in 1935 and 1953, and **have not beaten them since.** And in 2015, the jersey Morgan wore that day sold at auction in Cardiff for **£180,000** — bought, of all people, by **Nigel Wray**, the Saracens owner, outbidding five separate New Zealand attempts to take it home. The man later at the centre of the salary-cap scandal owns the most sacred shirt in Welsh rugby. Nothing in this conversation ever stops connecting.)*

---

### The era around the match

The 1905 win was the peak of something much bigger. Between **1900 and 1911, Wales won 35 of 43 internationals** and took the Triple Crown in **1900, 1902, 1905, 1908, 1909 and 1911.**

When France entered the championship, Wales won the first ever **Grand Slam in 1908** — the first by any nation — and then did it again in **1909**, and again in **1911** after the Five Nations was formalised. Three of the first four Slams in history. Between March 1907 and January 1910 they won **eleven consecutive internationals** — a record that stood for decades.

The engine was the **four three-quarter system** — seven backs, eight forwards — the Welsh invention that the entire rugby world copied, built on fluid passing and backline exploitation at a time when everyone else was still kicking and shoving. And the selectors had grasped something genuinely modern: with specialists like the hooker **George Travers**, they stopped picking the fifteen "best players" and started picking men **for specific jobs** — arguably the birth of positional selection.

And the team itself was the Gould compromise made flesh. **Gwyn Nicholls** — the "Prince of Centres" — ran a laundry business. Teddy Morgan became a **doctor.** And in the pack, men like **Dai "Tarw" Jones** — "the Bull" — a collier from **Treherbert** in the Rhondda, picked precisely for strength and aggression, the rock on which 1905 was built. Doctors and colliers, laundrymen and steelworkers, in the same fifteen, at the same time, beating the world.

**That team could not have existed in England.** The 1895 purge had made it structurally impossible — England's colliers were all playing in the Northern Union by then.

*(The one defeat that matters from this era: **1906**, when the first Springboks came to Swansea, and 40,000 at St Helen's watched South Africa's forwards grind Wales down 11–0. The Boks announced themselves against the best team in the world — one year after Paul Roos's tourists had named themselves the Springbokken. Your two threads, crossing again.)*

---

### And underneath the gold, the bleeding

Because here is the thing the anthem drowned out.

**All through the golden era, the Northern Union was strip-mining Wales.**

The maths never changed. A Rhondda collier playing union got nothing — officially. A Northern Union club offered him a **job, wages, and six shillings a match**, rising steadily. For a man whose family lived on the edge, the "choice" was no choice at all. The James brothers had gone in 1893; through the 1900s the trickle became a procession, internationals among them.

And on **New Year's Day 1908, at Aberdare** — deep in the coalfield — the professional **New Zealand All Golds**, returning from the tour that founded rugby league in Australasia, played Wales in the **world's first official rugby league international.** Wales won 9–8.

Sit with that. In the very season Wales won union's first ever Grand Slam, there was already enough professional rugby *inside Wales* to field a Test team — in Aberdare, in the valleys, in the heart of the union heartland. Merthyr, Ebbw Vale, Aberdare, Barry — Welsh league clubs sprang up. Most died within a few years; the union's grip on Welsh identity was too strong, and the fiction of 1897 gave the workers just enough reason to stay.

But the northern scouts never left. They would keep coming back — through the golden era, through the twenties, through the Depression, through to Jonathan Davies in 1989 — and every time the Welsh economy sagged, they harvested.

**Wales's golden era was played on a wound that never closed.**

---

### What Gen 2 leaves behind

**The anthem tradition** — invented by Teddy Morgan and 47,000 Welshmen as an answer to the haka, and now the property of every sport on earth.

**The greatest single result in the history of the game to that point** — 3–0, the Originals' only defeat, a rehearsed trick play, and a dead man's disputed try that New Zealand has never forgiven or forgotten.

**Three of the first four Grand Slams ever won**, eleven straight wins, and a tactical revolution — the four three-quarter line — that the whole world adopted.

**A team of doctors and colliers together** — the living proof of the Gould compromise, and of everything the 282 in London had made impossible in England.

**And a dependency nobody had noticed.** Welsh rugby's greatness rested entirely on the coalfield — its players, its crowds, its towns, its money. Coal *was* Welsh rugby.

In 1913, the South Wales coalfield produced its all-time record tonnage. The valleys were full, the Arms Park was full, Wales were champions of the world in all but name.

Then came the war. And after the war, the coal markets collapsed — and **between 1921 and 1938, four hundred and forty thousand people left Wales.**

Rugby league scouts met the misery at the pithead with contracts in hand. **Thirty-seven Welsh players, internationals among them, went north in the twenties alone.** Wales were whitewashed three years running. Clubs folded.

The golden era didn't fade. **It was carried away with the coal.**

That's Gen 3 — the Long Winter. Say the word.

## Generation 3: The Long Winter (1919–1968)

**14 October 1913. Senghenydd, in the Aber Valley.**

An explosion rips through the Universal Colliery. **Four hundred and thirty-nine men and boys die** — the worst mining disaster in British history.

Among the dead: **five members of the Senghenydd rugby team.**

I'm starting there because it's the fact that tells you what Welsh rugby actually *was*. This was not a sport that drew on the industrial communities. It was a sport made **of** them — so completely that when a pit exploded, it took a chunk of a rugby club with it. The sources call it the "slow drip" — the men lost, year on year, to the industries that made the game possible.

The golden era was already fraying. England's win at Cardiff in 1913 was Wales's first home defeat to a Home Nation since 1899. Then the war came, and rugby was suspended, and Welsh internationals — Charlie Pritchard, Johnny Williams — died in it, alongside the men from every valley club.

But the war didn't kill Welsh rugby. Every home nation lost its young men in equal measure.

**What killed Welsh rugby was coal.**

---

### The collapse

The First World War had created an artificially enormous demand for Welsh coal. When it ended, the demand collapsed — and with it, the entire economy of the valleys. Mass unemployment swept the coalfield. The knock-on hit the ports, Cardiff and Newport, whose whole business was shipping the stuff.

And then the people left.

**Around half a million people emigrated from Wales during the depression years** — the figure the sources give for 1921–1938 is around 440,000. Whole villages emptied. This is one of the great forgotten depopulations of modern Europe: a country of under three million losing half a million people.

Now — remember the thesis we've carried across two continents. **Welsh rugby's greatness rested entirely on the coalfield: its players, its crowds, its towns, its money.**

Take the coalfield away, and watch what happens to each pillar in turn.

**The crowds.** In homes where the man was the only earner and the pit was shut, the choice was stark: rugby tickets or food and rent. The gates collapsed.

**The clubs.** This is the part that genuinely hurts to read. **Loughor** — which had produced *five internationals in the 1920s* — was, by 1929, **begging door to door for old kit.** Haverfordwest disbanded entirely from 1926 to 1929. Pembroke Dock Quins were down to **five members** by 1927. In the Rhondda, **Treherbert** — the club of Dai "Tarw" Jones, the rock on which 1905 was built — **vanished before 1930**, along with Llwynypia and Nantyffyllon. Even mighty **Pontypool**, who in 1927 were beating the touring Waratahs and Maoris, were **£2,000 in debt and facing bankruptcy by 1930.**

**The union itself.** The WRU was the only home union that didn't own a ground. The Arms Park was leased from the Marquess of Bute's family; St Helen's was on loan. The governing body of the national sport of Wales was a tenant.

**And the players.** Here it comes.

---

### The harvest

The Northern Union — rugby league — had been picking at Wales since the James brothers in 1893. Now, with the valleys starving, the picking became a **harvest.**

The maths was unanswerable. A Welsh union player could claim nothing — officially. A league club offered a signing fee, wages, and a job, in the industrial North of England where there *was* still work. For an unemployed collier with a family, this was not a temptation. It was a **lifeline.**

**Between 1919 and 1939, forty-eight capped Welsh internationals went north.**

Forty-eight. **The equivalent of three full national squads.** And that's just the capped men — beneath them went an uncounted mass of trialists, club players, and promising boys who never got the chance to be capped at all.

*(This is the Welsh version of the wound you've seen everywhere: Argentina losing Noriega to the Wallabies, Georgia's squad in the Top 14, Romania bleeding to France. Except Wales's version ran for a **century**, and it was driven not by ambition but by **hunger.** Every Welsh league signing was, at bottom, an indictment of the 1893 vote at the Westminster Palace Hotel — proof that amateurism was a tax the poor could not pay.)*

And on the field, the results mirrored the coal price with eerie precision. **Of 42 matches in the 1920s, Wales won 17.** Between 1923 and 1928 they managed **seven wins — five of them against France.** In 1928, even France beat Wales for the first time.

The selectors thrashed about hopelessly. In **1924** they picked **thirty-five different players across four matches, with a different captain for each game.** Only two men played in all four. That is not selection. That is panic.

---

### The strange revival of the 1930s

And then, slowly, it turned — and the reasons are instructive.

The economy began recovering from about 1937. The **Welsh Secondary Schools Rugby Union** was feeding exciting young backs through the school system — note that: when the *clubs* were dying, the **schools** kept the pipeline alive. North Wales finally embraced the game. And the national team started winning again.

**1931:** a first championship in nine years, sealed in a bruising win over Ireland at Ravenhill. **1933:** the moment every Welshman of that generation remembered — **Wales win at Twickenham for the first time**, at the twenty-third year of trying, captained by Watcyn Thomas. **1935:** Wales beat the **All Blacks again, 13–12** at Cardiff, with the young Haydn Tanner making his debut.

Two wins over New Zealand, 1905 and 1935 — both by the width of a hand, both at the Arms Park. The mythology was compounding even as the country starved.

Then the Second World War shut everything down again. Only **four players** bridged the seven-year gap — Tanner among them.

---

### The Indian summer: 1950–1953

After the war, the coal industry was nationalised, the valleys steadied, and Welsh rugby produced one last, glorious flare of the old fire.

**1950: the Grand Slam** — the first since 1911, **thirty-nine years** after the last, captained by **John Gwilliam**, crowned with a 21–0 win over France in Cardiff. It came off the back of a wooden spoon in 1949, which is about as Welsh as recoveries get.

And then the tragedy that sits inside that triumph. Wales won the Triple Crown in Belfast — and a **chartered flight bringing supporters home crashed at Llandow.** **Seventy-five Welsh fans and five crew died.** At the time, it was **the world's worst civil air disaster.** The golden day and the darkest day, on the same weekend, in the same story.

**1952: another Grand Slam** — England beaten at Twickenham, France in Paris, Gwilliam again.

And **19 December 1953, Cardiff Arms Park:** Wales beat **New Zealand 13–8**, with Ken Jones's famous late try, Bleddyn Williams captaining despite injury.

Bleddyn Williams. Jack Matthews. Ken Jones. Roy John. Clem Thomas. And above them all, **Cliff Morgan** — the fly-half from the Rhondda, named the greatest Welsh player of the fifties, one of the great crowd-pullers in the history of the game, captain of the 1955 Lions in South Africa.

*(Morgan's great friend was a quiet, cerebral fly-half from Cefneithin named **Carwyn James**, who won two caps in 1958 and would go on to coach the 1971 Lions to the only series win over New Zealand ever achieved, and the 1972 Llanelli side that beat the All Blacks, and the 1973 Barbarians — and who was **never once asked to coach Wales.** Hold that name for Gen 4; he's the ghost in its machine.)*

**Wales have not beaten New Zealand since that afternoon in 1953.** Seventy-three years, as of this conversation, and counting.

---

### The quiet revolution

The rest of the fifties and early sixties sagged — a shared title in 1956, then nothing until 1964. In **1954** St Helen's hosted its last international and the **Arms Park became Wales's sole home**; the WRU had finally, gradually, wrestled control of its own ground from the Bute estate.

And then came the defeat that changed everything.

**1964, Durban: South Africa 24, Wales 3.** A record hammering, on Wales's first overseas tour.

The response — and by now you know the Welsh reflex: *when beaten, build* — was a **coaching revolution.** The WRU's Coaching Committee was given real teeth. In **January 1967, Ray Williams was appointed Coaching Organiser** — one of the first professional coaching administrators in world rugby. **David Nash** became Wales's first national coach; **Clive Rowlands**, "Top Cat," the tactician who'd captained the 1965 Triple Crown side, took over in 1969.

While England was still refusing to allow league tables in its own club game — remember, no proper English leagues until 1987 — **Wales was building a national coaching structure in the 1960s.** The little nation professionalised its *thinking* thirty years before the game professionalised its wages.

On **3 December 1966**, against Australia, two young men made their debuts: **Gerald Davies and Barry John.** On **1 April 1967**, in Paris, a scrum-half from Gwaun-cae-Gurwen won his first cap: **Gareth Edwards.** By 1968, at twenty, Edwards was the youngest captain in Welsh history. In 1969, Rowlands's Wales won the Triple Crown.

The pieces were all on the board.

---

### What Gen 3 leaves behind

**Proof of the thesis, in the negative.** Everything we established about what *made* Welsh rugby — coal, community, crowds — Gen 3 proves by subtraction. The coal market collapsed, and within a decade the greatest rugby nation on earth was begging door to door for kit. **Welsh rugby was never an institution that sat on top of the coalfield. It was the coalfield, at play.**

**Three squads' worth of internationals lost to the north** — the amateurism tax, paid in full, by the people the 1897 Gould compromise was supposed to protect.

**Two more wins over the All Blacks** (1935, 1953) — and the beginning of the longest wait in the sport.

**Seventy-five dead at Llandow**, folded forever into the memory of the 1950 Slam.

**And a coaching revolution born of humiliation in Durban** — Ray Williams, Clive Rowlands, and a generation of boys named Edwards, John, Davies and Williams arriving in the side just as the structure to use them was being built.

The coal industry had one last generation of full employment left in it. The grammar schools of the valleys were stuffed with talent. The Arms Park was being rebuilt.

Everything was loaded.

**Gen 4** is what happened when it fired: 1971, the greatest side ever to wear the jersey; the only Lions ever to win in New Zealand, with eleven Welshmen in the Test team; *that* try in 1973; and a decade in which a small, wet, post-industrial country of 2.7 million people was, simply, the best rugby nation on the face of the earth.

Say the word.

## Generation 4: The Second Golden Era (1969–1979)

Start with a village.

**Cefneithin.** A mining village in Carmarthenshire, a scatter of houses around a pit. Population: a few hundred.

That one village produced **Barry John** — the fly-half they called The King — and **Carwyn James**, the man who coached the only Lions team ever to win a series in New Zealand.

The King and the Kingmaker. Same village. Same street-level rugby, same chapel, same coal.

That is the seventies in miniature: a tiny, wet, post-industrial nation of 2.7 million people producing, from its villages and grammar schools and pit towns, the greatest concentration of rugby talent the world had ever seen — and, for one decade, being simply **the best rugby nation on the face of the earth.**

---

### Where they came from

Because before the matches, look at the men — since the *sociology* of this team is the whole point of your thesis.

**Gareth Edwards** — a miner's son from **Gwaun-cae-Gurwen**, spotted at grammar school. **Barry John** — Cefneithin. **Phil Bennett** — **Felinfoel**, the brewery village outside Llanelli. **Gerald Davies** — the son of a collier from Llansaint. **JPR Williams** — a doctor's son from Bridgend who became a surgeon himself. **Mervyn Davies** — a schoolteacher from Swansea. **John Dawes** — a chemistry teacher.

And then the front row. **The Pontypool Front Row** — "the Viet Gwent": **Charlie Faulkner**, a steelworker with a judo black belt whose real age was a state secret; **Bobby Windsor**, who worked in the steel mill at Llanwern; **Graham Price**, a collier's son who studied civil engineering and chased down a kick in Paris in 1975 to score from **75 metres on his debut** — a tighthead prop, in the mud, in the Parc des Princes.

Doctors and steelworkers. Teachers and miners' sons. **The Gould compromise of 1897, still paying out eighty years later** — the whole social range of a nation in one changing room, which England's 1895 purge had made structurally impossible on the other side of the Severn.

They were all, officially, amateurs. Windsor clocked into the steelworks on Monday morning after Tests. And the boot money flowed quietly, as it had since 1897, and everyone agreed not to look.

---

### The machine behind them

This didn't happen by magic, and it's important to say so, because the myth of the seventies is all flair and no structure — and the myth is wrong.

Remember Durban 1964, the 24–3 humiliation, and the Welsh reflex: *when beaten, build.* The WRU built. **Ray Williams**, appointed Coaching Organiser in 1967, constructed a national coaching system years before anyone else in the northern hemisphere took coaching seriously — Scotland didn't even *appoint* a coach until 1971, still half-believing the very idea violated amateurism. **Clive Rowlands** — "Top Cat" — became national coach in 1969 and won a Triple Crown immediately.

Wales in 1970 had what almost nobody else had: **a system.** Grammar schools feeding clubs, clubs feeding a fierce unofficial championship — Pontypool, Llanelli, Cardiff, Swansea, Neath, all within an hour of each other, playing murderous midweek fixtures — and a national coaching structure on top. It was, in everything but the pay packet, a professional environment.

*(You've seen this before: Woodward's marginal gains, Rassie's roadmap, Lemoine's 6am sessions. Wales did the culture-and-structure revolution in the 1960s, under amateurism, and harvested it for a decade.)*

---

### 1971

**The Grand Slam** — the first since 1952, won with a squad so settled they used only **sixteen players in four matches.**

The moment everyone remembers: **Murrayfield.** Wales trailing, minutes left, the Slam dying. Skipper John Dawes calms everyone down; **Delme Thomas** wins the lineout; the ball flies down the line; JPR makes the space; **Gerald Davies** squeezes into the corner to cut the gap to one. And then **John Taylor** — the flanker, kicking left-footed from the touchline — lands what is still called the greatest conversion since St Paul. **19–18.**

The Slam was sealed in Paris, 9–5, Edwards and John scoring everything between them.

And then the summer that changed British rugby forever. **The 1971 Lions in New Zealand** — coached by **Carwyn James** of Cefneithin, with **eleven Welshmen in the Test side** — won the series. They remain, to this day, **the only Lions team ever to win a series in New Zealand.**

Barry John kicked and glided through that tour with such unearthly calm that the New Zealand press crowned him **The King**. Edwards wrote later of his marvellous easiness of mind — reducing every problem to its simplest form, backing his talent, radiating a cool superiority that spread through the whole side.

*(And Carwyn — the intellectual, the Welsh-language nationalist, the man who out-thought New Zealand in New Zealand — was **never once asked to coach Wales.** He coached Llanelli. He coached the Lions. He coached the Barbarians. The WRU's committee men found him too clever, too political, too *other*. The greatest coaching mind Wales ever produced, and his country never used him. Hold that alongside everything you know about committees in this sport.)*

**The King abdicated in 1972**, retiring at twenty-seven — driven out, in part, by a suffocating fame no amateur sportsman had ever experienced. A woman once **curtsied** to him. He found it terrifying.

---

### The two matches everyone on earth has seen

**31 October 1972, Stradey Park.** Carwyn James's **Llanelli beat the All Blacks 9–3.** Roy Bergiers scored from his own charge-down; Delme Thomas was carried off on the crowd's shoulders; and the pubs of South Wales famously **ran dry by six o'clock.** There's a folk song about it. Of course there is.

And then **27 January 1973, the Arms Park.** Barbarians v All Blacks — the 1971 Lions reassembled in all but name, Carwyn's spirit presiding.

Third minute. Phil Bennett fields a kick deep in his own 22, and instead of hooshing it into the stands, throws **three sidesteps** at the chasing All Blacks. JPR takes it, then Pullin, then Dawes with the great dummy, Tom David to the halfway line, the juggled Quinnell pass — and **Gareth Edwards**, arriving from nowhere at full sprint, takes it on his chest and dives into the corner through the drizzle.

Cliff Morgan — the Rhondda fly-half of 1953, now in the commentary box — carried it into legend: *Brilliant! Oh, that's brilliant!… What a score!*

It is, by broad consent, **the greatest try ever scored**, and the fact that the commentary was Welsh, the scorer Welsh, the architect Welsh and the coach Welsh tells you who owned rugby in 1973.

---

### The relentless years

**1976: Grand Slam.** Again sixteen players in four matches. Sealed 19–13 against France in Cardiff, and remembered for one image: **JPR Williams** — the surgeon, the man who regarded a rugby field as a place of honest violence — hitting the flying **Jean-François Gourdon** with a shoulder charge feet from the line that saved the Slam and would today earn a red card and a tribunal. Nobody in Wales has ever apologised for it.

*(The price of that spring: captain **Mervyn Davies**, the great number eight, suffered a brain haemorrhage during a cup semi-final weeks later. He survived; his career didn't. The golden era buried one of its own kings standing up.)*

**1977: Triple Crown.** **1978: the Grand Slam again** — sealed 16–7 over France, with a hobbling **Phil Bennett**, now captain, scoring two tries through the pain. Wales had become **the first nation ever to win three consecutive Triple Crowns**, a run they stretched to **four by 1979.**

The decade's ledger: **three Grand Slams (1971, 1976, 1978), five Triple Crowns, six championships.** Against England across the seventies: **nine wins in ten.** The 1972 team went unbeaten but couldn't complete a Slam because Wales and Scotland refused to travel to Dublin at the height of the Troubles after threats purportedly from the IRA — so the greatest era might, with one plane journey, have had a fourth Slam.

On **18 March 1978**, against France at the Arms Park, **Gareth Edwards played his 53rd consecutive Test and walked off a Grand Slam champion.** Fifty-three caps, never dropped, never injured out, not once in eleven years. He and Bennett retired the same afternoon. In 2003, a poll of international players in *Rugby World* named Edwards **the greatest rugby player of all time** — and Will Carling, ranking him first in 2007, wrote that if he played now, he would still be the best.

---

### The asterisk, and the omen

Two shadows, and they matter.

**First: New Zealand.** For all the glory, Wales never beat the All Blacks in the decade — and the 1978 match at the Arms Park is the wound that never healed. Wales led late, until All Black locks **Andy Haden and Frank Oliver theatrically dived out of a lineout** to con a penalty, and Brian McKechnie kicked New Zealand to a 13–12 win. Haden essentially admitted the dive years later. Deans 1905, Haden 1978 — the two nations have spent a century convinced the other robbed them, and both are right.

**Second — and this is the one that matters for what comes next.** Look again at where every one of those players came from. The pit. The steelworks. The grammar school. The chapel. The valley club with its murderous midweek fixture.

**Every single pillar of that machine was about to be demolished.**

The grammar schools were already being reorganised away. The coal industry had one decade left. In 1979, Margaret Thatcher entered Downing Street; in 1984 the miners struck, and lost, and the pits closed, and the communities that had produced Edwards and Bennett and the Viet Gwent were economically gutted within a generation.

Welsh rugby's second golden era, like its first, was **the coalfield at play.** And this time, when the coal went, there would be no 1950s revival waiting — because this time the industry wasn't cyclically depressed. It was **abolished.**

---

### What Gen 4 leaves behind

**The greatest team of the amateur age**, and a fair claim to the greatest ever: three Slams, the 1971 Lions, Stradey '72, *that* try in '73.

**A pantheon** — Edwards, John, Bennett, JPR, Gerald, Mervyn, the Viet Gwent — drawn from miners' cottages and doctors' houses alike: the full, living proof that Wales was the one place in Britain where this game belonged to everybody.

**A coaching genius exiled by his own union** — Carwyn James, the ghost at every Welsh feast since.

**A mythology so heavy** that every Welsh team for the next fifty years would be measured against it and found wanting — the seventies became not an inspiration but a **haunting.**

**And a foundation made entirely of things that were about to be destroyed.**

**Gen 5** is the destruction: Thatcher, the strike, the pits closing; the exodus north becoming a flood — Jonathan Davies to Widnes in 1989; **South Africa 96, Wales 13**; professionalism arriving in 1995 to a nation with no money; and the arrival, from Auckland, of a man the Western Mail would christen **The Great Redeemer.**

Say the word.

## Generation 5: The Pits Close (1980–2002)

**3 March 1985.** The miners of South Wales march back to work behind their lodge banners and their brass bands, heads up, beaten.

The strike is over. The state has won. And over the following decade, the coal industry of South Wales — the industry that had *been* Welsh rugby since the 1880s, that had produced Dai Tarw Jones and Gareth Edwards and the Viet Gwent, that had filled the Arms Park and paid for the jerseys and given the whole thing its meaning — was **shut down. Not cyclically depressed, as in the twenties. Abolished.**

You already know the theorem from Gen 3: *Welsh rugby was never an institution sitting on top of the coalfield. It was the coalfield, at play.*

Gen 5 is the second running of the experiment. Take away the coal — permanently this time — and watch.

---

### The eighties: holding the line

The decade didn't start as a collapse. It started as a **transition nobody managed.**

The seventies gods retired almost as one — Edwards and Bennett in 1978, JPR's last Arms Park Test in 1981 (his eleventh win in eleven against England, naturally). Into their boots stepped a genuinely gifted generation — **Terry Holmes**, **Paul Thorburn**, and above all a slight, electric fly-half from Trimsaran named **Jonathan Davies** — who were asked to carry the heaviest burden in the sport: *be the seventies again.*

And they were carrying it through a country in shock. The pits were closing, the steelworks shedding thousands, whole valley towns losing their reason to exist — and, as the accounts of the decade put it, the national game became one of the last remaining sources of pride for communities that were losing everything else. The team wasn't just underperforming against a mythology. It was being asked to *console a nation* while the world overtook Wales in investment, planning and player support.

And "up north," the rugby league scouts — who had never really left — watched the chaos in the valleys and, in the phrase of one retrospective, licked their lips.

There were real highs. Wales went to the inaugural **1987 World Cup** and finished **third** — beating Australia in the play-off, still their best ever World Cup finish. **1988** brought a Triple Crown and Jonathan Davies at his shimmering peak.

And then the bill arrived.

---

### Going north, again

That summer of 1988, Wales toured New Zealand and were annihilated — 52–3 and 54–9. And in the aftermath, the eighties exodus became a stampede.

**January 1989: Jonathan Davies — the captain of Wales, the best fly-half in the northern hemisphere — signed for Widnes rugby league.**

The reason was the same reason it had been in 1893 and 1926: **money, in a country that had none.** Union paid nothing, officially. The valleys were on the dole. League clubs paid signing fees that could buy a house. Davies went, and after him went a generation: **Scott Gibbs**, Allan Bateman, Scott Quinnell, John Devereux, Paul Moriarty, David Young — internationals, captains, the spine of what should have been the nineties team.

*(This is the third harvest — 1890s, 1920s, 1980s — and every one of them tracks a Welsh economic collapse. The amateurism tax, levied on the poor, collected by the north, for a hundred years. And note the bitter symmetry the Tribune piece captures: the northern league towns being gutted by the same deindustrialisation were themselves about to be told by Rupert Murdoch's money that their historic clubs should merge — and they revolted, with one protester saying the sport had always belonged to the working man and now belonged to businessmen. Both rugby cultures of the British working class were being dismantled by the same decade.)*

On the field, Wales fell off a cliff. A first-ever home defeat to Romania in 1988 — *Romania, the Oaks, mid-golden-era, exactly as you'd now expect.* A first Five Nations whitewash in 1990. At the **1991 World Cup**, Wales lost at home to **Western Samoa** — cue the oldest joke in Welsh rugby: *thank God we weren't playing all of Samoa* — and went out at the pool stage. In **1994** they won a last Five Nations title, a false dawn; by **1995** they'd been dumped out of another World Cup at the pool stage by Ireland, and — uniquely humiliating for a Five Nations country — had been forced to **qualify** for it, through ties against Portugal and Spain.

---

### 1995: the trap springs

And then, in August 1995, the game went professional — declared open in Paris by, of all people, a Welshman: **Vernon Pugh QC**, the former WRU chairman, chairing the IRB.

For Wales, professionalism was simultaneously salvation and catastrophe.

**Salvation**, because it ended the century-long haemorrhage overnight. If union could pay, there was no reason to go north. The league exodus stopped dead, and the exiles started coming *home* — Scott Gibbs, Scott Quinnell, Allan Bateman, and eventually Jonathan Davies himself, returning to Cardiff in 1996 to finish where he'd started.

**Catastrophe**, because Wales had to pay professional wages **out of a post-industrial economy.** England had London money, corporate owners, Sir John Hall and Nigel Wray. France had its town-hall-and-industry club network. Wales had nine or ten proud Victorian clubs — Cardiff, Swansea, Llanelli, Neath, Pontypridd, Newport — in an economy that had just lost its industrial base, all now expected to pay full squads.

And here the sources record the strategic blunder that Welsh rugby people still argue about in pubs: **Wales missed the chance to integrate its clubs into the English league structure** — the big-market, big-TV competition next door — and instead lurched between formats, coaches and rows. The nineties WRU became a byword for knee-jerk governance: lose, sack the coach, reshuffle, repeat.

The clubs spent money they didn't have chasing a market they couldn't win. The national team kept losing. And it all bottomed out on one afternoon.

**27 June 1998. Pretoria. South Africa 96, Wales 13.**

Ninety-six. To a Wales side, admittedly stripped of players, but still — **the worst defeat in the history of one of the game's founding nations.** Fifteen tries. The nation that had beaten the 1905 All Blacks, that had owned the 1970s, conceding nearly a hundred points on the Highveld.

*(You'll notice the family resemblance: England 134–0 over Romania in 2001, New Zealand 96–13 over... no — that scoreline belongs to Wales alone. Every fallen rugby power has its number. Romania's is 134. Wales's is 96.)*

---

### The Redeemer

Six weeks later, the WRU did something desperate and clever: they hired a headmaster from Auckland.

**Graham Henry** arrived in the autumn of 1998 on the biggest coaching salary in world rugby, into a job nobody sane wanted. And what followed was one of the great short-term transformations the game has seen.

Wales won **ten consecutive Tests** — a national record run. They went to **Paris and won** for the first time in years. And on **26 June 1999**, in the first match ever played at the brand-new **Millennium Stadium** — built on the bones of the Arms Park for the coming World Cup — Wales beat **South Africa 29–19.**

The **world champions.** The team that had put ninety-six points on them **twelve months earlier.** Beaten, in Cardiff, for the first time in the entire history of the fixture.

The *Western Mail* christened Henry **"The Great Redeemer"** — and if you want to understand Wales, understand that the biggest-selling newspaper in the country reached for **chapel language** to describe a rugby coach. The messiah reflex — the belief that one anointed man can substitute for a broken system — enters Welsh rugby *here*, and it never leaves. Every coach since has been hired as a saviour and sacked as a heretic.

Wales hosted the **1999 World Cup** in the new stadium, beat Argentina nervily, and reached their first quarter-final since 1987 — where **Australia**, the eventual champions, beat them 24–9. The exiles were home: Gibbs was in the centre, Quinnell at eight, Jonathan Davies watching from the box. The circle, at least, had closed.

But read the fine print, because the sources do. The Millennium Stadium — glorious, roofed, the best rugby ground on earth — was built on **debt**, and the burden of that borrowing would hang around the WRU's neck for the next two decades, shaping every funding row to come. The Redeemer's run ended as they all do: results faded, the Grand Slam-shaped hole remained, and Henry left in 2002, worn down, en route — eventually — to winning a World Cup with the All Blacks.

And underneath it all, nothing structural had been fixed. Nine professional clubs, one small broke country, a union in debt, and a domestic game visibly unable to pay for itself.

Somebody was going to have to do something drastic.

---

### What Gen 5 leaves behind

**The second proof of the theorem.** Coal collapsed in the twenties and Welsh rugby collapsed with it; coal was *abolished* in the eighties and Welsh rugby broke in a deeper, more permanent way — because this time the economic base was never coming back.

**The last great harvest** — Jonathan Davies, Gibbs, Quinnell and a generation gone north — and then the 1995 watershed that ended the century-long bleed at the precise moment Wales could least afford the cure.

**96–13** — the number that ended the pretence.

**A stadium and a debt**, bound together forever.

**And the messiah reflex** — the Great Redeemer, the first of the saviour-coaches, establishing the pattern Wales still lives in: no system, only prophets.

**Gen 6** is the drastic thing: 2003, David Moffett, and the WRU **abolishing its own historic professional clubs** — Cardiff, Swansea, Llanelli, Newport, Pontypridd, Neath, a century and a quarter of identity — and replacing them with five, then four, invented **regions.** A wound that has never closed. And then, impossibly, on top of that wound: **Grand Slams in 2005, 2008 and 2012, Warren Gatland, and two World Cup semi-finals.**

Say the word.

## Generation 6: The Bargain (2003–2019)

**Two dates, eighteen months apart.**

**1 April 2003.** David Moffett walks out and announces that Cardiff, Swansea, Llanelli, Newport, Neath, Pontypridd, Bridgend — clubs older than the Union that governs them, some older than the game's written laws — will no longer exist as professional entities. In their place: five invented **regions**, drawn on a map, some of them stitched together from ancient rivals who had spent a century trying to beat each other. Stradey Park and the Arms Park, as top-flight professional homes, are finished.

**19 March 2005.** In front of a full Millennium Stadium, Wales beat Ireland 32–20 and complete the **Grand Slam** — their first since 1978, the first of the Six Nations era, the end of a **twenty-seven-year wait.** Gavin Henson, Shane Williams, Martyn Williams, a nation weeping into its scarves.

That's the whole generation in two dates. **Welsh rugby made a bargain:** demolish the club game your grandfathers built, centralise everything on the national fifteen, and in exchange take a golden age. And the bargain **worked** — four Grand Slams, two World Cup semi-finals, a fortnight ranked the best team on earth. It worked so well that nobody looked at what it was quietly costing underneath, which is exactly how the best Faustian bargains are supposed to feel.

You already know the theorem from Gen 5: *the messiah reflex — no system, only prophets.* Gen 6 is what happens when the prophets deliver. For a while, spectacularly.

---

### The cull (2003–2004)

Understand what Moffett walked into. The WRU was roughly **£55 million in debt** — the Millennium Stadium, that gleaming debt-financed cathedral from the end of Gen 5, now a millstone. Nine proud professional clubs were all going broke at once in a country of under three million. Something had to break.

Moffett's answer was the Irish answer: **provinces.** Ireland had four (Leinster, Munster, Ulster, Connacht), centrally contracted, feeding one national team — and it was working. *(Watch this comparison across the whole generation, because it's the hinge: Ireland and Wales copied the same blueprint at the same time. Ireland did it with money and patience and won everything for two decades. Wales did it broke and in a hurry, and the difference is the entire story.)*

On **24 February 2003**, at an Extraordinary General Meeting, all 239 member clubs voted. The historic clubs lost. Five regions were confirmed: **Cardiff Blues, Newport Gwent Dragons, Neath-Swansea Ospreys, Llanelli Scarlets** — and **Celtic Warriors,** a shotgun marriage of Pontypridd and Bridgend, two valleys clubs who despised each other.

The Warriors lasted **one season.**

They had the smallest crowd base, the most debt, and an owner — Leighton Samuel — who ended up selling his stake to the WRU, which then simply **dissolved the region** in the summer of 2004. A professional rugby team, representing the heart of the coalfield valleys — Pontypridd, the club of the *Viet Gwent* from your Gen 3 — **abolished after a single year of existence.** Five became four. It has never gone back up.

*(This is the coalfield thesis one more time, in its final and cruellest form. In the 1920s the valleys lost their pits. In the 1980s they lost them permanently. In 2004 they lost their professional rugby team — the last institution standing — killed not by coal prices but by a spreadsheet. The three western/eastern coastal regions survived; the valleys, the actual birthplace of Welsh forward play, were left without an elite side. They still are.)*

Moffett refinanced the stadium debt with Barclays, streamlined the blazers, and left in 2005 having done the necessary, unforgivable thing. The wound he opened has never closed. And it was followed, almost immediately, by glory.

---

### The Slam nobody saw coming (2005)

Nobody rated the 2005 Wales. Mike Ruddock — the WRU's *third* choice, hired only after Gareth Jenkins was overlooked yet again — inherited Steve Hansen's young side and a skills coach, Scott Johnson, and something clicked that no one has fully explained since.

**5 February 2005:** England, the reigning world champions, at a heaving Millennium Stadium. Deep into the game, from nearly fifty metres, **Gavin Henson** — fake tan, silver boots, the most divisive man in Welsh sport — drills a penalty through the posts. **Wales 11, England 9.** First win over England in twelve years.

Then Italy away. Then **Paris,** 15–6 down at the break, roaring back to win 24–18. Then Scotland run off their feet at Murrayfield. And finally Ireland, and the Slam, and twenty-seven years of grief let go at once. It was thrilling, ragged, romantic rugby — the last Slam won purely on instinct before the age of systems arrived.

And then, being Welsh rugby, it **imploded within a year.**

Henson wrote a book — *My Grand Slam Year* — slagging off teammates. The squad, led by captain Gareth Thomas, boycotted a press conference in solidarity against the ghostwriter. Ruddock, contract still unsigned, lost the dressing room, and on **14 February 2006 — mid-Six Nations —** he **resigned.** Grand Slam to gone in eleven months. *(The messiah reflex, inverted: the anointed man discarded the instant the anointing wears off.)*

---

### Enter the actual messiah (2007–2008)

Scott Johnson caretook. Gareth Jenkins finally got the job he'd been denied twice — and led Wales to a **catastrophic 2007 World Cup,** knocked out at the pool stage by **Fiji,** 38–34, in one of the great tournament shocks. Jenkins was sacked in the car park, essentially, that same night. *(1987, third in the world. 2007, out to Fiji. The graph from Gen 5, still falling.)*

Into the crater, in **November 2007,** walked a New Zealander who would define the entire generation: **Warren Gatland.**

His first game in charge, February 2008: **England at Twickenham.** Wales hadn't won there since 1988 — since *Jonathan Davies*, the last golden day before the great exodus north. England led by thirteen. Wales won **26–19.** And they didn't stop: a clean sweep, a second **Grand Slam** in four seasons, sealed 29–12 over France. Gatland's defence coach **Shaun Edwards** built a wall that conceded **two tries in the entire championship.**

The Ruddock Slam was jazz. The Gatland Slam was engineering. That was the difference, and it lasted twelve years.

---

### Warrenball, and the near-miss decade (2009–2013)

Gatland's Wales became big, physical, drilled — carriers running hard lines, a ferocious kick-and-chase, Jamie Roberts smashing holes, George North on the wing. The press called it **"Warrenball,"** usually as an insult; Gatland cashed the trophies and didn't care.

**2011 World Cup, New Zealand.** The best Welsh side in a generation reaches the **semi-final** — first since 1987. Against France, in the eighteenth minute, captain **Sam Warburton** is **red-carded** for a tip-tackle. Fourteen men for over an hour. Wales lose **9–8,** missing kickable penalties, to a French side they were plainly better than. It remains one of the great gutting near-misses in the sport. *(De Allende and Pollard aren't here yet, but South Africa is coming for Wales at exactly these moments — hold that thought.)*

**2012:** revenge on France, a third **Grand Slam** in eight years, 16–9. Then, absurdly, **eight straight defeats** — the messiah reflex twitching again — before **2013** delivered the era's most violent statement: needing to beat England by seven to steal the title, Wales won **30–3,** a record margin, denying England the Slam on the final day and **retaining the championship** for the first time since the 1970s.

Underneath the Test-match glory, meanwhile, something familiar was happening to the players.

---

### The 1893 tax, mutated (2013–2018)

Here is the thread from Georgia and Romania and the James brothers, running straight through the golden age.

The regions were **broke.** Four professional teams in a small post-industrial economy could not pay what the **English Premiership** and the **French Top 14** could pay — clubs backed by billionaires and TV money. So Welsh players started leaving, exactly as they'd left for the rugby league north a century earlier. *Same economics, new border.* Not Widnes and Leeds now, but Toulon, Bath, Racing, Northampton.

In **2013–14** it nearly blew the whole thing apart: the four regions, starved of funding and furious over a European-competition row, threatened to **quit and join an Anglo-French league,** breaking from the WRU entirely. It was open war, conducted in the newspapers, for the better part of a year. *(Ireland never had this war. Ireland funded its provinces. That's the whole difference, again.)*

The peace deal invented the machinery Welsh rugby still runs on: the **Rugby Services Agreement** and **National Dual Contracts** — the WRU paying 60% of a star's wages, the region 40%, to keep the best players home. And **"Gatland's Law":** if you played your club rugby outside Wales, you needed **sixty caps** to be picked for Wales at all. A tariff wall against your own exodus.

It half-worked. It brought some players home. It also confirmed the structural truth: **the national team was a Ferrari bolted onto a chassis that couldn't afford tyres.** Everything flowed to the Test side, because the Test side won, and winning paid the bills — turnover pushed past £100 million. The regions were left to bleed as "problem children." *(You mapped this exact fork in Gen 5: Georgia turned exile into strategy, Romania just bled. Wales in the golden age was doing both at once — winning at Test level while its club game slowly haemorrhaged out.)*

And amid it all, the answer to Gen 5's defining number. **96–13** had been the abyss. Between **2014 and 2018,** Gatland's Wales beat **South Africa five times in six meetings** — including, in Nelspruit in June 2014, a **first-ever win on South African soil.** The nation that conceded ninety-six was now, routinely, beating the Springboks. The wound of the nineties, finally scarred over.

---

### 2015: the injuries, and the kick that wasn't taken

Home-nation World Cup, and Wales landed in the **"pool of death"** with England and Australia. What followed was the most Welsh victory imaginable: at **Twickenham,** against the hosts, with the treatment room **overflowing** — Halfpenny and Rhys Webb gone before the tournament, then Scott Williams, Liam Williams and Hallam Amos all breaking down *during the match* — Wales won **28–25.** England, needing three points to draw late, spurned the kick, chased a lineout for the win, and got **shoved into touch.** England went out of their own World Cup at the pool stage. Wales went through on courage and strapping tape.

The **quarter-final** was the cruelty: **South Africa 23, Wales 19,** decided by a late Fourie du Preez try, Wales undone by soft penalties. *(South Africa. Again. 2011 was France; 2015 and 2019 would both be the Boks. Wales's ceiling, three World Cups running, wore green and gold.)*

---

### The annus mirabilis, and the sunset (2019)

Then, at the very end, the peak.

Wales won **fourteen consecutive Tests** — the longest winning run in their history — sweeping through a **2019 Grand Slam** (sealed against Ireland on the final day) and, on **19 August 2019,** for the first time in the entire history of the world rankings, becoming the **number one team on the planet.** Number one. The nation of the closed pits and the dissolved clubs, top of the world.

They carried it to the **2019 World Cup semi-final** in Japan — and there, for the third World Cup in a row, the ceiling. **South Africa 19, Wales 16,** Handré Pollard's boot in the seventy-sixth minute, an arm-wrestle of eighty-one kicks that Wales lost by three. The Boks went on to lift the cup. Wales lost the third-place game to the All Blacks, and **Warren Gatland walked away** after **twelve years** — the longest, most successful reign any foreign coach has ever had over a Test nation.

Alun Wyn Jones, veteran of the 2011 and 2019 semis both, said it best: Gatland's legacy was bigger than one game. It was. He'd won four... no — he'd won **three** of the four Grand Slams himself (2008, 2012, 2019), plus a title, plus two semis, plus No. 1 in the world. The great redeemer of Gen 5 had a *name*; Gatland had a *reign.*

---

### What Gen 6 leaves behind

**The bargain, honoured — and its price, deferred.** Wales sacrificed its historic club game in 2003 and got a golden age in return: four Grand Slams (2005, 2008, 2012, 2019), a title, two World Cup semis, a fortnight as the best team alive. On the pitch, it is the second-greatest era in Welsh history after the 1970s. Nobody can take that away.

**But the base was hollow the entire time.** Four broke regions, one glorious national team, the whole structure balanced on the results of fifteen men — and on the **Millennium Stadium debt** still ticking away underneath, the way it has since Gen 5. When the national team won, the model looked like genius. The model was never genius. It was a **Ferrari with no engine factory.**

**The exodus, in modern dress** — the 1893 amateurism tax reborn as the Top 14 and the Premiership, patched over with Dual Contracts and Gatland's Law rather than solved.

**And the messiah reflex, vindicated for once** — Gatland held the prophet's role for twelve years and actually delivered, which taught Welsh rugby precisely the wrong lesson: that you don't need a system, you just need the right man. Ruddock lasted a year. Gatland lasted twelve. The reflex looked like wisdom.

**Then Gatland left.** The prophet walked out, No. 1 in the world, and handed over a team at its peak sitting on a foundation that was **quietly rotting** — regions in the red, Covid about to add £5 million of debt to each of them at 11% interest, a governance culture about to be exposed, and no messiah left in the building.

**Gen 7 is the bill for the bargain.** Everything Gen 6 deferred, arriving at once: Pivac, the collapse, the near-strike of 2023, the scandal that engulfed the WRU, Gatland *summoned back* to save it and failing, and a losing run that would eventually become the **longest in the history of any tier-one nation.** The current crisis — the deepest valley yet, deeper than the eighties, deeper than the twenties.

The prophets are gone. Say the word.

## Generation 7: The Deepest Valley (2020–present)

**19 August 2019.** Wales are the number one rugby team on planet Earth. Fourteen straight wins, a Grand Slam, Gatland about to lead them to a World Cup semi-final. The summit.

**7 July 2025.** Wales are ranked **fourteenth in the world** — their lowest position in the history of the rankings — having just lost their **eighteenth consecutive Test match**, a streak that stretched **644 days** and equalled the longest losing run by any tier-one nation in the history of the sport. The record they matched was set by **France between 1911 and 1920** — a run that ran *through the First World War.* Wales matched it in peacetime, fully professional, with a century of infrastructure behind them.

Six years. Summit to abyss. That is the shape of Gen 7, and you have been waiting for it since the very first thing I told you about Wales, because this is the generation where the bill for the whole bargain — Gen 6's Faustian deal, the national Ferrari bolted to a chassis with no engine factory — **finally, entirely, came due.**

You know the theorem. *Welsh rugby was never an institution on top of the coalfield. It was the coalfield at play.* And you know the corollary from Gen 6: they hollowed out the club game, centralised everything on fifteen men, and it worked right up until the moment it didn't. Gen 7 is the moment it didn't. The base was gone. The prophets ran out. And there was nothing underneath to catch the fall.

*(A rugby writer with forty-six years on the Welsh beat was asked, in 2025, whether the game had ever been in a deeper valley. His answer was no. Not the eighties. Not the twenties. Here. Now. This is the bottom of the whole story.)*

---

### The false dawn (2020–2022)

Wayne Pivac inherited the No.1 team and was supposed to evolve it. For one spring it looked like he had: in **2021**, Wales won the **Six Nations title** — a Triple Crown, a 40–24 hammering of England — and came within one phase of a Grand Slam, denied only by a last-play French try in Paris.

It was a mirage. Look at the fixtures either side and the truth shows: thumped 54–16 by the All Blacks, well beaten by South Africa, and then the two results that told the real story. In **March 2022**, **Italy beat Wales in Cardiff** — Italy, who hadn't won a Six Nations match in seven years, winning on the last play at the Principality. And in **November 2022**, Wales lost **13–12 to Georgia.**

*Georgia.* Sit with that, because Georgia has been a thread through this entire book — the exile-strategy nation, the tier-two power hammering on the locked door of the Six Nations, the team told for two decades it wasn't good enough for the top table. In November 2022, Georgia walked into Cardiff and beat a founding nation. **The line between tier one and tier two — the line this whole book has been arguing about — didn't blur that day. It moved. And Wales was on the wrong side of it.**

Pivac won 13 of 34. He was sacked in December 2022.

---

### The prophet, recalled (December 2022)

And Welsh rugby did the only thing its instincts know how to do. It reached for a messiah.

**Warren Gatland came back.** The man who had delivered three Grand Slams and No.1 in the world, summoned from the past to save the present — the messiah reflex from Gen 5 in its purest, most desperate form. If the system is broken, find the prophet who last made it sing.

He said, on arriving, that he *wished he'd known* what he was walking into. Because within weeks, the crisis stopped being about rugby at all.

---

### The rot in the walls (January 2023)

On **23 January 2023**, BBC Wales aired a documentary, and the floor fell out of the institution itself.

Former staff described a **toxic culture** at the Welsh Rugby Union — allegations of **sexism, racism, homophobia and misogyny.** The former head of the women's game said she had been left in tears; that a colleague had, in front of others, joked about wanting to rape her. Another staffer said she had been left suicidal. Within a week, chief executive **Steve Phillips had resigned.** The interim CEO, former Olympic hurdler Nigel Walker, used a phrase that hung over everything that followed: Welsh rugby, he said, was facing an **"existential crisis."**

This is the part of Gen 7 that makes it different from every valley before it. In the 1920s and the 1980s, the *game* collapsed because the *economy* collapsed — the rot came from outside, from the pits closing. In 2023, the rot was **inside the building.** The governing body that had held Welsh rugby together through every previous catastrophe was itself revealed as broken, and the catastrophe was partly *moral.* You cannot deindustrialise your way out of that. It was self-inflicted.

---

### The players down tools (February 2023)

Three weeks later, it nearly stopped entirely.

Ahead of the England match — for many the most important eighty minutes of any Welsh year — the players **threatened to strike.** Not over the culture scandal directly, but over money and contracts: the regions and the WRU had failed for months to agree a funding framework, players were out of contract with no idea if they'd have jobs, and their fury boiled over into a genuine refusal to play. Gatland couldn't even name a team.

The game went ahead only after an eleventh-hour deal: player representation on the board, contract flexibility, and the loosening of **"Gatland's Law"** — the 60-cap rule that barred overseas-based players — down to **25 caps.** Ken Owens, the captain, said Welsh rugby had become the **"laughingstock."** Alun Wyn Jones, the most-capped player in the history of the sport, said the quiet part aloud: *treat people badly for long enough, and you end up here.*

*(And notice what this was, underneath. The 1893 amateurism tax — the argument about who pays the working man, running through this book from the James brothers to Jonathan Davies going north — had finally turned inward. It wasn't Wales losing players to Widnes or Toulon any more. It was Wales's own players, refusing to take the field against England, because the union couldn't tell them whether they'd be paid. The hundred-year-old wound, reopened from the inside.)*

---

### One last flare (2023 World Cup)

And then — because Welsh rugby is never allowed a clean narrative — the **2023 World Cup went well.**

Out of the chaos, Gatland's patched-up, written-off squad **topped their pool unbeaten**, including their **biggest-ever victory over Australia** (40–6), a result that helped bounce the Wallabies out of the tournament altogether. They reached the quarter-final and lost, narrowly and painfully, to **Argentina** — another old thread from this book, the Pumas, professionalised and rising while Wales fell.

For a moment it looked like the bottom had been a false floor. It hadn't. It was the last flare before the dark.

---

### The floor gives way (2024–2025)

After the Georgia game in 2023, Wales **beat no international side for a year and a half.**

**2024:** a Six Nations **whitewash** — including *another* home defeat to Italy — and then, in November, a **first-ever home defeat to Fiji** at the Principality. The proud fixtures fell one by one.

**2025** is where the story hits the rock. Gatland — the prophet, the man who once stood atop the world — oversaw defeat after defeat until, on **11 February 2025, mid-Six Nations, he walked away.** The most successful coach in Welsh history, resigning in-tournament, his second reign a ruin: six wins in twenty-six, a 23% return, the exact inverse of everything he'd been recalled to be. *The messiah reflex, disproven on its own founder.*

Cardiff's Matt Sherratt took interim charge and the losses kept coming. The nadir arrived on the final day of that Six Nations: **England 68, Wales 14** — Wales's **heaviest-ever home defeat to England**, the seventeenth loss in a row, a third... a *second* straight wooden spoon confirmed. Then the streak rolled to **eighteen** in Japan, matching France's WWI record, before Wales finally, *finally* won — **31–22 in Kobe on 12 July 2025**, ending 644 days of losing.

And tucked inside 2025, the single most damning statistic in this entire book: when the British & Irish Lions ran out for their opening Test that summer, **there was not one Welsh player in the matchday squad — the first time that had happened since the 1890s.** Since before Gwyn Nicholls. Since before the First Golden Era of Gen 2. A hundred and thirty years of Welsh rugby supplying the Lions' beating heart, ended in a single team-sheet.

*(Someone wrote, that season, that Max Boyce's mythical Welsh "fly-half factory" in the valleys had drawn its shutters — like every other factory in the region. That is the coalfield thesis, delivered as a joke, landing as an autopsy. The pits closed, then the works closed, then — last of all — the thing the closures had somehow always spared: the outside-half production line, the deepest magic in Welsh rugby, from Bennett to Jonathan Davies. Gone quiet too. The base was finally, fully gone.)*

---

### The surgery, again (2025–2026)

And so, twenty-two years after David Moffett cut nine clubs into five regions and then four (Gen 6), Welsh rugby reached for the knife one more time.

In August 2025 the WRU floated cutting the four regions to **two.** After consultation, in October 2025 the board **unanimously agreed to go to three** — one in Cardiff, one in the east, one in the west — to be implemented from the 2028/29 season. It is Moffett's amputation, run in reverse and again: five to four to, soon, three. Another historic region will die, and the valleys — Pontypridd's Celtic Warriors killed in 2004, never replaced — remain without an elite team.

The regions themselves are drowning. **Covid loaded roughly £5 million of debt onto each of them, at 11% interest.** The Scarlets — in their **150th-anniversary season** — just posted a **third straight £2 million annual loss.** Cardiff fell into administration in 2025 and had to be taken over by the WRU. A proposed takeover of Cardiff by the Ospreys' owners collapsed in April 2026. And only **this month**, in July 2026, did the Scarlets become the last of the four to sign the new Professional Rugby Agreement — a deal that funds all four until 2028 and then **ends with one of them gone.** Four clubs have now signed a contract whose final clause is the death of one of them. That is the state of the professional game in Wales as we speak.

Overseeing it: a new chief executive, **Abi Tierney** — the first woman to run the WRU, brought in explicitly to clean out the culture the 2023 documentary exposed — and a new chair, and a new director of rugby, and the language of "transformational systemic change." Whether it's strong medicine or, as that veteran writer put it, **death by a thousand cuts,** nobody yet knows.

---

### Where it stands — July 2026

And here, for the first time in this whole book, the story catches up to the present and stops being history.

**Steve Tandy** — a Welshman, an ex-Ospreys man, poached back from Scotland's coaching staff — took permanent charge for the **2026 Six Nations.** Wales finished bottom. A **third successive wooden spoon.** Thrashed by England and France.

But — and Welsh rugby always has a *but* — they were **competitive against Scotland and Ireland**, they produced flashes of the old fire, and on **14 March 2026** they **beat Italy 31–17 in Cardiff** to end a fifteen-match, three-year Six Nations losing streak. Not a revival. A pulse. The faint, unmistakable sense of a floor being found.

---

### What Gen 7 leaves behind — and the whole story, closed

**The thesis, proven a final and total time.** Three times before, Welsh rugby collapsed when its industrial base collapsed — the 1920s, the 1980s, the 1990s. Gen 7 is the fourth collapse, and the deepest, because it is the one with **no base left to lose.** The coal is a museum. The works are car parks. The fly-half factory has drawn its shutters. Welsh rugby spent Gen 6 living off the accumulated genius of a coalfield that no longer existed, and in Gen 7 that inheritance simply ran out. There was nothing underneath. There hasn't been for a while. Everyone just looked away while the national team was winning.

**The messiah reflex, exhausted.** Gen 5 invented the saviour-coach. Gen 6 vindicated him. Gen 7 recalled him — and watched the greatest prophet in Welsh history fail, because no single man can substitute for a system, and Wales has spent forty years refusing to build one.

**The institution itself, broken and being rebuilt** — the 2023 scandal forced Welsh rugby to admit that the rot wasn't only economic, and the slow, unglamorous work of governance reform is the least romantic and most necessary thing in this entire narrative.

**And the open question the whole book has circled from the very first page in Uruguay:** *can a small, poor, post-industrial nation sustain elite rugby at all?* For Georgia, the answer was *maybe, through exile.* For Romania, it was *no.* Wales spent a century proving the answer was *yes, gloriously* — and Gen 7 is Wales asking the question again, from the bottom of the deepest valley, with the honest answer no longer certain.

But end on this, because it's true and the record demands it. **Wales has been in valleys before and climbed out.** The 1930s dole queues gave way to the 1950 Grand Slam. **96–13 in Pretoria gave way, twelve months later, to beating the world champions and, twenty years on, to No.1 in the world.** This nation has buried its rugby and resurrected it more times than any country in the sport. The valley is deeper now than it has ever been. That is not the same as saying there's no way up.

The 2027 World Cup is the next test. The three-region future is the next gamble. Steve Tandy and a young side that beat Italy are the next, fragile hope.

For the first time in this whole telling, there is no next generation to name — because we've arrived at *now,* and now is still being written. **Gen 8 is unwritten. Wales is standing in the deepest valley in its history, looking up.**

Whether it climbs is the only part of the story I can't tell you.

That's Wales. Where to next — back to the northern giants, or somewhere new on the map?
