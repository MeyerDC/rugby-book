# Wales

England invented a game and then invented a rule to decide who was allowed to be good at it. Two hundred miles west, a country took the same game, the same rulebook, the same word — *amateur* — and produced the opposite outcome. Where England's amateurism drove its northern working men out of the union code entirely, Wales's working men stayed inside it and, for a generation at a time, were the best in the world at it. That is the fact this chapter has to explain: not a class thesis argued in advance, but a hundred-year sequence of matches, pits, chapels, unions and departures that made the same seed grow into a different tree.

The engine of the difference was coal. When rugby reached South Wales in the 1870s it was carried, as everywhere, by educated men — college graduates, returning students, a theology professor teaching trainee vicars to play. But it was planted in the coalfield, in the very towns where the work was, and the collier picked it up. From that accident of geography comes everything: the crowds, the golden eras, the singing, and also the long wound — because the game the collier loved was governed by a rulebook written by men who never needed to be paid to play, and for a century that rulebook kept handing his best players to the professional code in the north of England.

This chapter is told the way the others in this book are now told: from inside each room, with no hero people and no villain people. The collier who played on Saturday and was back underground on Monday; the club committee counting the gate; the amateur official at the union who believed, from inside his own world, that he was protecting the game; the player who took the train to Wigan because his family needed the money, and was written out of Welsh rugby for it; and, in the end, the modern administrators who tried to save a system and the ones who nearly broke it. The facts are carried by named sources, and where the record is thin or the historians disagree, the draft says so rather than smoothing it over. It runs in eight generations, from a folk game older than Shakespeare to the deepest crisis the Welsh game has known.

## Generation 0: The Vicar, the Village Game, and the Colleges (1603–1880)

**1603, Pembrokeshire.** A Welsh gentleman-scholar named George Owen of Henllys sits down to write a description of his home county, and among the customs he records is a game. Men gather in open country between two parishes — sometimes a few dozen, sometimes, he says, many hundreds, some on foot and some on horseback — and they contest a small wooden ball boiled in tallow to make it slippery, the object being to carry it home to your own parish church, across fields and streams and whatever bodies stand in the way. There is no pitch, no referee, no agreed hour to stop. Owen calls the game *cnapan*, and records that it had been played in the county since, in his phrase, "great antiquity." He was writing while Shakespeare was still alive, and the game was already old.

This is where a history of Welsh rugby honestly has to begin, because Wales — like Georgia with its folk game *lelo burti*, and unlike England, whose mob-football the public schools eventually captured and codified — carried a violent communal ball game in its own country for centuries before any Englishman arrived with a rulebook.

### Cnapan

Cnapan belonged to the family of Shrovetide and Eastertide festival games played across the British Isles: parish against parish, everyone who could stand allowed to play, the ball driven cross-country by main force. In Wales it ran through Pembrokeshire, Carmarthenshire and Ceredigion — the west, not the coalfield of the later story — and George Owen's 1603 account remains the fullest description anyone has. What that account says plainly is that this was mass participation, communal, and rough.

The temptation is to draw a straight line from cnapan to the game of Gareth Edwards, and the honest historians resist it. Dai Smith and Gareth Williams, in *Fields of Praise*, the official history of the Welsh Rugby Union, treat the folk game as part of the cultural soil rather than a direct ancestor: cnapan was suppressed and faded, and codified rugby arrived generations later by an entirely separate route, out of the English schools. Where the game was pushed down by authority — and the record here is thinner than the retelling usually admits — it was pushed down as a public-order problem, a crowd of common men gathering to do something violent, which a nervous state has always been inclined to read as something more than sport. That instinct recurs across this book. But the link between the tallow ball and the leather one is best kept as what it is: not a bloodline, but a disposition. When rugby did finally reach the industrial valleys and every town at once raised a team to fight the next town, the *shape* of the thing — my parish against yours, everyone plays, carry it home — was already familiar in the Welsh imagination.

### The vicar and the college

The codified game arrived the way it arrived in Montevideo and Bucharest and Grahamstown: in the luggage of an educated man. In **1850** the **Reverend Rowland Williams**, a theologian trained at King's College, Cambridge, was appointed Vice-Principal and Professor of Theology at **St David's College, Lampeter**, a small Anglican foundation in rural Ceredigion that existed to train young Welshmen for the priesthood. Dismayed by how little his ordinands did with their bodies, Williams introduced games — cricket, fives, and a form of football — in the spirit of the Muscular Christianity then spreading out of Thomas Arnold's Rugby: the healthy body as the vessel of the healthy soul. A professor of divinity teaching trainee vicars to tackle, as a form of spiritual hygiene.

The tidy legend has Williams carrying the Rugby School game west himself, having watched the Old Rugbeian Arthur Pell try to introduce its laws at Cambridge in 1839. It is a good story, and it is the sort of story this book has learned to handle carefully. Historians such as Prescott have questioned whether Williams "introduced" rugby in any real sense — the chain from Pell to Lampeter is a transmitted account, not an archived one, and what Williams demonstrably did was build the *sporting environment* in which a game could condense, not import a finished rulebook. The earliest fixtures at Lampeter were internal matches of an undefined footballing kind under local rules. Lampeter's own claim to be the oldest rugby side in Wales, from around 1850, sits on that ground: institutionally it came first, but there is no record of a match until well over a decade later, and no properly constituted club for years after that. What can be said without embroidery is that the game entered Wales through a theological college, under a clergyman, as an instrument of moral formation — and that detail matters, because the same establishment origin gave England its class weapon, and Wales would do something else with it entirely.

### The first match

The Lampeter students had the problem of every remote rugby outpost in this book: no one to play. The answer came when **Llandovery College**, founded 1848, took up the game, and in **1866** the two colleges met — the **first recorded rugby match in Wales.** The score was not written down. The founding fixture of what would become a national religion went unrecorded, which is fitting; the paper trail in every rugby country begins long after the ball. Some accounts push school games back to the 1850s, but 1866 is where the documented history starts, and the rest is tradition.

### The graduates and the coalfield

Through the **1870s** the familiar mechanism did its work: former pupils of the Welsh colleges, and Welshmen returning from English universities, settled and founded clubs. **Neath**, in **1871**, is widely recognised as the first — a detail that captures how tangled these origins are, since Neath's early captain, T. P. Whittington, would win an international cap not for Wales but, in 1873, for Scotland. **Swansea** and **Llanelli** followed in **1872**; the first recorded club match, Swansea against Neath, is dated to **7 February 1872.** Then **Brecon** and **Newport** in 1874, **Mountain Ash** in 1875, **Cardiff** and **Merthyr** in 1876 — Cardiff itself stitched together from three existing clubs — with **Pontypool**'s origins reaching back, approximately, to 1868.

So far this is the English pattern exactly: colleges, graduates, respectable young men. What breaks the pattern is not who founded the clubs but *where.* Neath, Merthyr, Mountain Ash, Pontypool — these were not cathedral towns or leafy suburbs. They were coal and iron and tinplate towns, and the graduates settled in them because that is where the work and the money and the people were. Industrial South Wales in the 1870s was one of the furnaces of the world economy, and it pulled everything and everyone toward the coalfield. The game was planted, by economic accident rather than design, directly among the men who dug and smelted — not in a district they could never reach. Smith and Williams make this geography the hinge of the whole Welsh story, and it turns here, in the founding decade, before a single international was played. Beneath the famous clubs the scale of what was coming is visible in the works and street sides that sprang up around them: a tinplate town like Llanelli would soon carry, on top of its senior club, a swarm of foundry and pub and colliery teams. The instinct of cnapan — every place fields a side against the next place — was reasserting itself in industrial clothes.

### The choice of code, and the cup

In **September 1875**, at Brecon, the clubs of the south formed the **South Wales Football Club**, with a stated purpose: to play the principal clubs of the West of England, and — set down in writing — to adopt the Rugby code. That was a decision, not a drift. At the very moment association football was sweeping the north of England, South Wales committed itself, on paper, to the handling game. In **December 1875**, at Castle Green in Hereford, that side played its first representative fixture, a Hereford XV, and won by a single try scored by Swansea's A. H. Richardson. They wore **black and white** — the first representative colours of Welsh rugby were not red, a fact the WRU itself acknowledged more than a century later.

By **1878** the club had become the **South Wales Football Union** and was running the **South Wales Challenge Cup.** What a cup competition does to a game is by now a settled theme of this book: it makes winning matter, it fills the ground, and it draws in the best player a town possesses regardless of what his father does for a living. In Yorkshire that logic let the working man onto the field and, within twenty years, detonated English rugby into two hostile codes. Wales acquired the same detonator in the 1870s. What it did with the explosion, when the argument over money finally came, is the whole of the next generation — and it did the opposite of what England did.

### What Generation 0 leaves behind

By 1880 Wales had assembled, without quite intending to, every ingredient of a distinctive rugby culture. A folk memory of communal, everyone-plays, parish-against-parish football, ancient in 1603. A clerical, collegiate point of entry — a Cambridge-trained vicar and a theological college — that made the game respectable at birth. A first generation of clubs founded by graduates but planted, crucially, in the coal and steel towns rather than the suburbs. A deliberate, written choice of the Rugby code over association football. And a cup competition already pulling colliers and tinplate men onto the same field as the men who had brought the game home from university.

What forced all of this into a single institution was a humiliation. On **19 February 1881**, at Blackheath, a Newport official named **Richard Mullock** — acting with no official mandate — assembled the first Welsh national side and sent it against an England team with a decade of international experience. Two of his selected players never arrived; the side he did field was largely drawn from Oxbridge men, and the Welsh press savaged it as unrepresentative of the country's real strength. Wales lost by seven goals, a drop goal and six tries to nil — something close to **82–0** in modern scoring. Three weeks later, on **12 March 1881**, at the Castle Hotel in Neath, the clubs of Wales founded the **Welsh Rugby Union**, with Mullock installed as its secretary. The detailed roll of who was in the room that day, and who presided, is recorded more confidently in local retellings than the evidence strictly allows — and, in a small mystery no one has ever quite explained, the host town's own club, Neath, appears to have been absent from the meeting held in its own hotel.

A national union, then, born out of a thrashing, in a coalfield, from a game a vicar had taught to priests. Everything that made Welsh rugby unlike English rugby was already present. The coalfield would now decide what to do with it.

## Generation 1: The Union, the Coalfield, and the Gould Affair (1881–1899)

**The night of 6 January 1893, Cardiff Arms Park.** A hard frost had turned the pitch to stone — "hard as a brickbat," the reports said — and the following afternoon Wales were due to play England. So through the freezing night the ground staff fought the weather with the one thing South Wales had in limitless supply. By the Cardiff Rugby Museum's account they set out some five hundred *fire-devils* — coal-filled buckets, holes punched in their sides, stood up on bricks — and kept them burning across the turf for the better part of twenty-seven hours. A rugby ground thawed by coal fires, so that a coalfield's team could play. It is almost too neat a picture of what this generation was, and it happens to be true.

### The thing that happened in the valleys

Between the founding of the union in 1881 and the end of the century, Welsh rugby did the thing that no other rugby culture in Britain managed, and the reason was underground. The South Wales Coalfield in these decades was one of the great industrial engines of the world, and it pulled people into the valleys by the hundred thousand — from rural Wales, from the English west country, from Ireland and beyond. They packed into the terraced pit and tinplate towns, and on Saturdays, in their thousands, they played and watched rugby.

This is the heart of the Welsh story, and it is worth being precise about who is telling it. In *Fields of Praise*, their official history of the Welsh Rugby Union, Dai Smith and Gareth Williams made the coalfield the engine of the whole national game — the argument, in its strong form, that the fortunes of Welsh rugby would rise and fall with the fortunes of coal, because the game drew its players and its crowds and its money from the same seams. What it produced in the 1880s and 1890s was something England had just spent the same decades making impossible: a first-class game in which the collier who was back down the pit on Monday stood on the same field, in the same jersey, as the doctor and the schoolmaster. The game did not stay respectable and exclude him, as it did across the border. It absorbed him, and he made it better.

That single difference — the same seed, the same rulebook, the same governing word *amateur*, and the opposite social outcome — is the fact the whole chapter exists to explain. It was not a theory anyone argued in Cardiff in 1885. It was just what happened when you planted a gentlemen's game in a coalfield and let the coalfield play.

### Out-thinking the game: the four three-quarters

The valleys gave Wales players. Cardiff gave it an idea. In the mid-1880s Cardiff RFC solved a selection problem — how to fit a gifted newcomer, the Somerset-born **Frank Hancock**, into a back line that already had its centres — by simply playing an extra man behind the scrum: four three-quarters instead of three, seven backs instead of six, and a game built on handling and passing rather than forward smother. Cardiff's official history records Hancock first playing in the reshaped line in 1884–85, and captaining the club in 1885–86 to a season of almost absurd dominance: something like 131 tries scored and four conceded.

The idea travelled awkwardly. Hancock captained Wales in an early experiment with the system against Scotland in January 1886, and it went badly enough that the side abandoned it during the match. But the principle was too good to lose, and over the following seasons Wales made the four three-quarter game its own — a national style, taught downward through the clubs, prizing speed and width and the pass. By the great season of 1893 it was simply how Wales played. A country that had been humiliated 82–0 a dozen years earlier had, in the space of one generation, started telling the rest of the rugby world how the game might be played. (Much later the four three-quarter formation would be described as a Welsh invention of the golden 1900s; the record puts its birth here, in Cardiff, in the mid-1880s.)

### The Crown

The two things met — the coalfield's players and Cardiff's idea — in **1893**, and Wales won its first **Triple Crown**, beating all three of the other Home nations in a season for the first time. It opened on that thawed ground at Cardiff Arms Park, where Wales edged England **12–11** in a match made genuinely confusing by the fact that the scoring values had only just been changed — England's Howard Marshall crossed for three tries and still finished on the losing side. As the story has always been told, the game was settled by the full-back **Billy Bancroft**, who took the decisive kick as a dropped goal against the express wishes of his captain, **Arthur Gould**, who had wanted it placed — and put it over. Wales then beat Scotland at Raeburn Place and, in the decider, Ireland at Stradey Park in Llanelli, where Gould's younger brother Bert scored the try that clinched the Crown. It was Wales's first, and its only one of the nineteenth century.

Around Gould and Bancroft a recognisable Welsh game was forming: the "Big Four" clubs of Cardiff, Newport, Swansea and Llanelli; Bancroft at full-back, who would win thirty-three caps in an unbroken run to 1901; and, arriving in 1896, the Cardiff centre **Gwyn Nicholls**, the "Prince of centres," who would carry the four three-quarter game into the next decade and captain the side that beat the All Blacks. Wales had a style, a set of heroes, and full houses. What it did not have was any settlement with the two forces now pulling at it from outside — one from the north, and one from the game's own rulebook.

### The pull north

The first pull came before the great schism itself. In **1892**, three years before the game formally tore in two, the Swansea half-back brothers **David and Evan James** — a dazzling pair the crowds called "the curly-haired marmosets" — turned professional and went north to **Broughton Rangers**. The Welsh authorities suspended them in March 1893; they were reinstated only in 1896, came home to Swansea, and played for Wales again. But the direction of travel had been shown, and it pointed the same way it would point for the next hundred years.

When England's northern clubs broke away in August 1895 to form the Northern Union — the split that became rugby league, told in full in the England chapter — the new professional code did not stay in Lancashire and Yorkshire. It came recruiting in the Welsh valleys, and it found exactly what it was looking for: brilliant working-class players governed by a code that forbade paying them, in communities where a wage and a job in the north could change a family's life. The historian Tony Collins, in *Rugby's Great Split*, traced how systematically the northern game drew on Wales; Robert Gate's *Gone North* later counted 148 Welsh union internationals who went to league between 1895 and 1986. The exact inducements of these early years are harder to pin down than the retellings suggest — a much-quoted "six shillings a match" cannot be reliably sourced to this period and is best left aside — but the shape is not in doubt. Wales had built a working-class first-class game and then handed its rulebook to men who believed, sincerely, that a player must never be paid. The bleed north was the price, and it never fully stopped.

### The Gould Affair

The second pull came from inside, and it very nearly broke Wales's place in the game altogether.

**Arthur Gould** was the first superstar Welsh rugby produced — Newport's three-quarter, captain of the 1893 Crown, a national figure of a kind the sport had not seen before. As his career neared its end, an admiring public raised a testimonial for him by subscription, something over £700, and the money went into a house on Llanthewy Road in Newport, later known as *Thornbury*. On Easter Monday **1897**, at a dinner in the town's Drill Hall, the WRU's own president, **Sir John Talbot Dillwyn Llewelyn**, presented Gould with the deeds.

To the men who filled that hall, this was a nation thanking its greatest player. To the International Rugby Football Board — the guardians of amateurism sitting in England, Scotland and Ireland — it was something with a precise and damning name: a player being paid, in property, for playing rugby. And here the two moral worlds of British rugby, which this book keeps setting side by side, collided head on. The Board's officials were not inventing a grievance to punish Wales; they genuinely believed that the amateur principle was the thing that kept the game clean, and that a house bought by public subscription for a footballer was professionalism whatever you called it. The Welsh believed, just as genuinely, that a poor country's honouring of its hero was nobody's business but its own. Both sides were defending something real, and the result was a rupture.

Scotland and Ireland refused to play Wales; the WRU, rather than submit, briefly **resigned from the International Board** early in 1897. For a season and more, the country that had just shown the rest of Britain how to play was locked out of playing them at all. A compromise was patched together later that year once the English union shifted its ground, but the fixtures did not all knit back together at once — Scotland did not resume playing Wales until 1899 — and the affair was resolved as much by Gould's own retirement from international rugby as by any principle. He kept the house, and lived in it until his death in 1919.

### What Generation 1 leaves behind

By the end of the century Wales had become, in less than twenty years, a genuine rugby power and a genuine rugby problem. It had crossed the class divide that England had spent the same period fortifying, and built a first-class game on the shoulders of colliers and tinplate workers. It had out-thought the sport with the four three-quarter system. It had won a Triple Crown on a ground thawed by coal fires.

And it had discovered the two costs of being what it was. From the north, the professional code would keep taking its best working men, because Wales insisted on remaining amateur. From within amateurism itself, the Gould Affair had shown how close the country's whole conception of rugby — the people's game, the people's hero, the people's gift — sat to the line the English establishment had drawn, and how ready that establishment was to shut Wales out for crossing it.

Neither problem was solved. Both were simply carried forward into the years when Welsh rugby stopped merely being good and became, for a decade and a half, the best in the world — the years of the Triple Crowns, the first Grand Slam anyone ever won, an afternoon in December 1905 that made the game global, and a song.

## Generation 2: The First Golden Era, and the Song (1900–1914)

**16 December 1905, Cardiff Arms Park.** Before the match a touring side in black lined up and performed its war dance — the haka — as it had before every fixture of a tour on which it had so far beaten everyone it met. What happened next had, as far as anyone can document, never happened at a sporting ground before. On the suggestion of the Welsh selector Tom Williams, the Welsh players answered. Led, the accounts say, by the wing Teddy Morgan, they began to sing *Hen Wlad Fy Nhadau* — "Land of My Fathers" — and some forty thousand Welsh voices took it up until the whole ground was singing a hymn back at the war dance. It is the first recorded instance of a crowd singing a national anthem at an international sporting fixture, and every anthem sung before every match since traces to that afternoon. The song itself was Welsh to the bone: written in Pontypridd in 1856, words by Evan James and melody by his son James James, first sung under the title "Glan Rhondda."

Then they played, and Wales won.

### The match that made the game global

The 1905 New Zealanders — the "Originals," the first side to be called the All Blacks — arrived in Britain and dismantled everything in front of them, winning thirty-four of thirty-five matches on tour. The one they lost was this one, **3–0**, and it was no fluke. Under the captaincy of Gwyn Nicholls, Wales met New Zealand's forward power with a plan: a seven-man scrum to unsettle the tourists' pack, and a set of pre-rehearsed moves among the backs — the four three-quarter game, by now grooved to perfection. The single try came from exactly that. The scrum-half Dickie Owen, the tactical brain of the side, worked a planned switch that sent the ball through Rhys Gabe to Teddy Morgan, who went over in the corner. Three points, and Wales defended them for the rest of the game.

They very nearly did not hold. Late on, the New Zealand centre **Bob Deans** went for the corner, was hauled down near the line, and to his dying day insisted he had grounded the ball for a try before being dragged back. The Scottish referee, John Dallas, arriving behind the play, ruled no try. Deans believed the decision robbed him; he died three years later, at twenty-one, still saying so, and New Zealand has never entirely let the grievance go — Teddy Morgan himself reportedly thought Deans had scored. This book has no verdict to offer a hundred and twenty years late. What matters is that both sides were, in their own honest recollection, right, and that the disputed few inches at Cardiff Arms Park became one of the founding arguments of world rugby. The match is often called the moment rugby became a global game with a global rivalry; it certainly made Wales, for a while, the centre of it.

### The best in the world

The 1905 win sat inside the strongest sustained run any team had produced. In the first dozen years of the century Wales took **six Triple Crowns** — 1900, 1902, 1905, 1908, 1909 and 1911 — beating the other Home nations almost at will. Two of those seasons were more than Triple Crowns. In **1908**, with France now on the fixture list, Wales beat England, Scotland, Ireland and France in a single season — the first side ever to beat all four, and so the **first Grand Slam** in the sport's history. Wales did it again in 1909. Because France did not become an official member of the Championship until 1910, the purists reserve the title of first *official* Grand Slam for Wales's **1911** clean sweep — but by either reckoning, the Grand Slam was a Welsh invention, first achieved in the golden years.

Around Nicholls and Owen stood a side that Wales has mythologised ever since: Willie Llewellyn and Teddy Morgan on the wings, Rhys Gabe in the centre, a back division playing the fastest, widest rugby in the world. It was, on the surface, everything the game's founders in England could have wished for — brilliant, amateur, gentlemanly. But the men who made it were not, most of them, gentlemen, and underneath the gold the coalfield was doing the two things the coalfield always did: it was supplying the muscle, and it was being bled.

### The collier in the pack

The backs got the songs. The forwards came from the pits. The clearest single figure of it is **Dai "Tarw" Jones** — "Tarw" is Welsh for "bull" — a giant of a man, a **coal miner from Aberaman** in the Cynon Valley, and the forward the WRU itself would later call the rock on which the 1905 side was built. He won Triple Crowns in 1902 and 1905; he packed down in the pit on the days he was not packing down for Wales. He and the rest of the working-class Rhondda and valley forwards were the physical foundation of the golden game, and Dai Jones is also, exactly, where the golden game's contradiction becomes a person.

Because in **1907** Dai Jones was suspended by the union on a charge of professionalism — of having, in some way, been paid — and, suspended, he did what a working man with a family and a talent could do: he went to rugby league. The professional code had by now planted itself in the valleys. Between roughly 1907 and 1908 Northern Union clubs sprang up at Merthyr Tydfil, Ebbw Vale, Aberdare, Barry, Mid-Rhondda and Treherbert, right in the coalfield, offering the collier the one thing the amateur union forbade: to be paid for the game he was best at. On **New Year's Day 1908**, at the Athletic Ground in Aberdare, a Welsh rugby league side beat the touring New Zealand "All Golds" **9–8** in front of some twenty thousand people in freezing cold — the first rugby league international ever played — and the man who scored the winning try was Dai "Tarw" Jones, the rock of 1905, now a professional because his own union had cast him out. The same body that built the golden era was still, at the height of it, handing its own heroes to the north.

### The base beneath the game

None of this was separable from coal, and the historians who know the ground best insist on the connection. Dai Smith — himself a son of the Rhondda — and Gareth Williams, in *Fields of Praise* and in Williams's essays in *1905 and All That*, made the South Wales Coalfield the foundation of the whole edifice: the crowds, the money, the players all came up out of the same valleys. And in these very years the coalfield reached its absolute peak. By **1913** the South Wales pits employed something like **233,000 men** — close to a quarter of a million, roughly a fifth of all Britain's miners — and cut around fifty-six million tons of coal a year; Barry had become the largest coal-exporting port on earth, with Cardiff close behind. The golden era of Welsh rugby and the maximum extent of Welsh coal are the same moment. That is not a coincidence the coalfield thesis has to strain to explain; it is the thesis.

Which carried a warning inside it that nobody in a full Arms Park in 1913 wanted to hear. If the game rose with coal, it would fall with coal. And 1913 was the peak. There is only one direction from a peak.

### What Generation 2 leaves behind

By 1914 Wales had been, for over a decade, the best rugby nation in the world — six Triple Crowns, the first Grand Slams ever won, the conquerors of the first All Blacks, the country that taught the world to sing before a match. It had done it with a fusion no other rugby culture achieved: the educated backs' running game welded to a pack hewn out of the pits.

And it left that inheritance walking into two catastrophes at once. The nearer one was the war. When it came in 1914 the young men of the valleys enlisted in their thousands, and Welsh rugby paid the toll the whole of Britain paid: at least thirteen Welsh internationals were killed, among them Richard Garnons Williams, who had played in the very first Welsh international back in 1881 and died at Loos in 1915 at fifty-nine, and the great wing Johnny Williams, killed on the Somme. The golden generation did not fade; much of it was buried in France.

The further catastrophe was slower and, in the end, larger. The coal that had made everything had just crested. What happens to a rugby nation when the industry it was built on begins, seam by seam and valley by valley, to die — that is the long, grey story of the next fifty years.

## Generation 3: The Long Winter (1919–1968)

**June 1921.** A sixteen-year-old from Cardiff named **Jim Sullivan** — already, everyone could see, the finest young full-back in Wales — signed for the Northern Union club Wigan for a fee of around £750, a record for the professional game. He would play 922 matches over the next quarter-century, score six thousand points, and be remembered in the north of England as one of the greatest players who ever lived. He never won a cap for Wales, because Wales did not pay and Wigan did. He is the first sentence of this generation because he is the whole of it in one man: the best of Welsh rugby, leaving before it had properly begun, carried out of the valleys by an economy that was about to fall off a cliff.

### The coalfield goes into reverse

The thesis that explains the golden era explains the winter too, and it does it by running backwards. If Welsh rugby rose with coal, it would fall with coal — and after the brief post-war boom, coal fell, and fell, and kept falling. The export markets that had made South Wales rich did not come back. Through the 1920s and 1930s the coalfield that had employed something like a quarter of a million men in 1913 was gutted: by the standard economic accounts, **241 South Wales mines closed between 1921 and 1936**, and the number of miners was roughly halved, from about 270,000 to 130,000. Unemployment in the coalfield ran at around 40 per cent — twice the British rate — and reached nearly 43 per cent among miners by 1932. In 1926 the miners were locked out for six months and beaten, and after that the only real growth industry in the valleys was the road out of them.

And people took it. Between 1921 and 1938 something on the order of **440,000 people left Wales**, the overwhelming majority from the coalfield valleys — Merthyr Tydfil alone lost more than 26,000. This is the single most important fact about Welsh rugby for half a century, and it is not, on its face, a rugby fact at all. A rugby nation is its population of young men. When close to half a million people walk out of your heartland looking for work, they take the second rows and the wing-forwards with them, and the crowds that would have watched them, and the subscriptions that would have kept the clubs alive. The historians of the game, Dai Smith and Gareth Williams above all, have never treated the interwar collapse of Welsh rugby as a puzzle of tactics or temperament. It was a puzzle of coal, and the answer was written on the emigration returns.

### The flood north

What emigration did to the population, rugby league did to the talent that stayed. In the golden era the loss of players north had been a steady bleed; in the Depression it became a flood, for the simplest and least mysterious reason in this book. A gifted collier with no pit to go to, and a family to feed, could be offered a signing fee and a job in Lancashire that amounted to more money than he would otherwise see in years — and all he had to give up was the right to play, unpaid, for a country that could not employ him. Dozens of Welsh internationals made that journey between the wars; Robert Gate's later reckoning put the full century's toll at 148 union caps lost to league. They were not traitors and they did not think of themselves as traitors, whatever the amateur establishment implied. They were workers taking the best offer available in a ruined economy, exactly as their neighbours were doing by boarding trains to Slough and Coventry and Dagenham. The difference was that when a fitter left for the Midlands, Wales lost a fitter; when Jim Sullivan left for Wigan, Wales lost a full-back it would mythologise for a century.

Here the two moral worlds of British rugby, which this book keeps holding up against each other, show their cost most plainly. The men who ran the union genuinely believed amateurism was a principle worth defending. But the principle, in a starving coalfield, functioned as a rule that a poor man must give up either his best sport or his best wage. England had used amateurism to keep working men out. Wales had let them in — and then, unable to pay them, watched the professional code buy them away. Both unions kept the same word. It broke Wales's heart with it.

### The strange revivals

And yet the game did not die, because in Wales it never quite can. Out of the grey decades came sudden, inexplicable flares of the old brilliance. In **1931** Wales won the Championship outright for the first time in nine years. On **21 January 1933**, at Twickenham, Wales finally won on the ground for the first time ever — twenty-three years and many humiliations after the stadium opened — Cardiff's **Ronnie Boon** scoring a try and dropping a goal to beat England 7–3 in front of 64,000 people, with a young three-quarter named **Wilf Wooller** in the side. Wales took the title again in 1936.

And on **21 December 1935**, at Cardiff, they beat New Zealand for the second time, **13–12**, in one of the most famous finishes in the country's history. With the match in the balance, Wooller kicked ahead toward the New Zealand line, and the Oxford wing **Geoffrey Rees-Jones** chased it down for the winning try — a try Wooller himself, in the WRU's own account years later, admitted he never saw: Rees-Jones, he said, followed the ball "like a faithful spaniel… apparently, for I never saw it — to catch the ball." It was a day of good bounces and high courage, and it cost the hooker Don Tarr a broken neck, from which he recovered. These flares mattered enormously precisely because the background was so dark. A country being emptied of its people held onto its rugby team the way it held onto its choirs and its chapels — as proof that it was still, whatever the coal owners and the labour exchange said, a nation.

### The Indian summer

After the second war came the loveliest of the flares, and the last for a long time. Around a fly-half of genius, **Cliff Morgan**, and the elegant centre **Bleddyn Williams**, the "Prince of Welsh centres," Wales built a side that took the **Grand Slam in 1950 and again in 1952**, captained both times by the schoolmaster **John Gwilliam**. On the wing was **Ken Jones**, fast enough to have won an Olympic relay silver for Great Britain in 1948. And on **19 December 1953**, at Cardiff, this side beat the All Blacks **13–8**, the winning try coming when the flanker **Clem Thomas**, with nothing else on, improvised a cross-kick that Ken Jones ran onto and finished.

That match should be remembered for its quality. It is remembered, instead, for what came after it — which is nothing. **Wales has not beaten New Zealand since that afternoon in December 1953.** As of 2026, across some thirty-eight Tests since 1905, Wales has beaten the All Blacks three times — 1905, 1935, 1953 — and lost the rest; the most recent meeting, in the autumn of 2025, finished 52–26 to New Zealand. The 1953 win, meant to be a beginning, turned out to be the far edge of the golden past. Even in this best of the winter's seasons the old wound stayed open: the teenage prodigy **Lewis Jones**, who had made his debut at eighteen, went north to Leeds in 1952 for a record fee, in the middle of the Grand Slam years. The Indian summer warmed the valleys and then, like the coal, it ran out.

### The quiet revolution

By the early 1960s Welsh rugby had run out of both coal and inspiration, and in 1964 it received the humiliation it needed. On tour in South Africa, Wales were taken apart by the Springboks **24–0** at Durban — a nothing performance that finally shamed the Welsh Rugby Union into asking whether a great rugby nation could really keep selecting talented individuals on a Thursday and hoping. The answer, at last, was no. The WRU set up an inquiry into coaching, and out of it came the most consequential appointments in the modern history of the Welsh game — unglamorous, administrative, and decisive.

In **1967** the union appointed **Ray Williams** as its national coaching organiser, reportedly the first full-time paid coaching post anywhere in world rugby: a man whose entire job was to build, from the schools upward, a system rather than a hope. That same year **David Nash** became the first man to coach the national side, and though his tenure was short and unhappy — the committee, in a very Welsh act of self-sabotage, refused to send him on the 1968 tour to Argentina — the principle held, and **Clive Rowlands** inherited it. Wales was, for the first time in its history, deciding that success might be manufactured deliberately rather than merely prayed for.

### What Generation 3 leaves behind

Half a century of winter. The coal that built the game collapsed, the people left, the best players were bought away to the north, and Welsh rugby spent the decades between 1919 and the mid-1960s mostly losing, lit only by the occasional impossible afternoon — a first win at Twickenham, two victories over the All Blacks, two post-war Grand Slams — that kept the faith alive without ever restoring the throne.

But the winter ended not with a flare but with a plan. For the first time Wales had a coaching system being built on purpose, from the ground up. And it was being built at the exact moment a group of young men were coming through who would need it: a scrum-half from Gwaun-cae-Gurwen named **Gareth Edwards**, capped in 1967 at nineteen and captain of his country at twenty; a fly-half from Cefneithin, **Barry John**, who had joined him by 1967 and whom Wales would simply call "the King"; and, arriving in 1969, a full-back named **JPR Williams** who would tackle like a wing-forward. The system and the talent met at the turn of the decade. What they made together was the most beloved team the country has ever produced, and the last great harvest of the coalfield before it closed for good.

## Generation 4: The Second Golden Era (1969–1979)

**27 January 1973, Cardiff Arms Park, four minutes into the match.** A New Zealand kick drops behind the Barbarians' line, and a small dark-haired fly-half named **Phil Bennett** turns to field it almost on his own goal-line, with the whole of the All Blacks bearing down. What a sane player does here is kick to touch. What Bennett does is sidestep — once, twice, three times, off both feet, past a cluster of black jerseys — and suddenly the ball is moving: to **JPR Williams**, to the hooker **John Pullin**, to the captain **John Dawes** with a dummy, to **Tom David**, to **Derek Quinnell**, and at last, at full tilt onto Quinnell's one-handed pass, to **Gareth Edwards**, who dives into the left corner as Cliff Morgan's voice climbs into the sentence every Welsh person of a certain age can still recite — "*this is Gareth Edwards… a dramatic start… what a score!*"

It was, strictly, a Barbarians try, scored in an invitational match by a scratch British and Irish side. But five of the seven men who touched the ball were Welsh, the move began in a Welshman's instinct not to kick, and the world has always understood it for what it was: the purest four seconds of the way Wales played rugby in the 1970s. This is the generation the country has never stopped grieving for, because it was the best, and because it was the last of its kind.

### The best team in the world, again

The honours are almost tedious to list, which is the point. Between 1969 and 1979 Wales won **three Grand Slams** — 1971, 1976, 1978 — and **six Triple Crowns**, including four in unbroken succession from 1976 to 1979, a feat only England has since matched. For most of a decade Cardiff Arms Park was simply not a ground visiting teams expected to win at; the home record in the Championship ran unbeaten for twenty-seven matches before Scotland finally broke it in 1982. And the names have passed into a kind of scripture: Edwards at scrum-half, the most complete player the game had produced; **Barry John** at outside-half, so languidly untouchable on the 1971 Lions tour that the New Zealand press crowned him "the King"; Bennett, who succeeded him; JPR Williams thundering up from full-back into the line like an extra flanker; **Gerald Davies** and **JJ Williams** on the wings; **Mervyn Davies**, "Merv the Swerve," at the back of the scrum; and in front of them the **Pontypool front row** — Charlie Faulkner, Bobby Windsor and Graham Price — the first entire front row a country ever picked as a single club unit, and the subject of a Max Boyce song.

### The proof: New Zealand, 1971

The measure of how good they were is what they did in a red jersey that was not Wales's. In 1971 the **British and Irish Lions** toured New Zealand and did the thing no Lions side had managed before or has managed since: they **won the Test series**, two to one with a draw. The side was built around Wales — the coach was the Welshman **Carwyn James**, the captain the Welshman **John Dawes**, thirteen of the thirty tourists were Welsh — and its spine was the Welsh back division, with Barry John scoring a tour-record 188 points and JPR Williams dropping the goal that sealed the drawn final Test. More than half a century later it remains the only Lions series victory in New Zealand. Wales had spent its golden years of the 1900s teaching the world to play; in the 1970s it went to the far side of the earth and beat the best there was, on their own ground, at their own game.

### The machine, and the clubs

It is tempting to explain all this as pure genius arriving at once, and the men themselves are the last people who would let you. Underneath the golden side was the thing Wales had never had before: a system, built deliberately in the grey years. **Ray Williams**, the coaching organiser appointed in 1967, had by the early 1970s produced hundreds of qualified coaches and a grassroots structure that the rest of the world copied — a template so effective that the same union which employed him would not, at first, let him near the national team, so he attended in a red tracksuit and helped unofficially. The talent was coal-country talent; the polish was Ray Williams's.

And beneath the national side stood the strongest club game in the world. The clubs were not feeders; they were powers, and the proof came on **31 October 1972** at Stradey Park, when **Llanelli beat New Zealand 9–3** — Roy Bergiers charging down a clearance for the try, Phil Bennett and Andy Hill kicking the points, Carwyn James coaching and Delme Thomas leading — and the town emptied every barrel it had. The poet Max Boyce turned it into the line the day is still known by: *the day the pubs ran dry.* No Welsh club has beaten the All Blacks since. The valleys in the 1970s were still, for a last decade, working valleys — the pits and steelworks still open, the communities still whole — and they poured their young men into the clubs, and the clubs poured them upward into the best team on earth.

### What the game was carrying

Something else ran underneath, and it is the reason a chapter about class cannot let this decade be only a highlight reel. To the men in the valleys, this team was never merely a team. It was the argument that a poor, exploited, deindustrialising country could take the English at the English game and humiliate them — and the most famous expression of that feeling is a speech that Welsh rugby has repeated for fifty years and cannot actually verify. Before Wales played England in March 1977, Phil Bennett is supposed to have told his players to look at the men across the tunnel: *they've taken our coal, our water, our steel; they buy our houses and live in them a fortnight a year; we've been exploited and controlled and punished by the English, and that's who you're playing this afternoon.* Whether Bennett said those exact words, or anything like them, is genuinely disputed — the best sources flag it as very possibly apocryphal, a sentiment the nation put in its hero's mouth. Which is, in its way, the truer fact. A country does not invent that speech and attach it to that match unless the grievance it expresses is real. Welsh rugby in the 1970s carried a hundred years of coal and colliery and being told it did not matter, and it discharged all of it, eighty minutes at a time, against England.

### The omen

The golden side had one blind spot, and it pointed at the future. Even at their peak they could not beat New Zealand. In the very year the run began, 1969, Wales toured New Zealand and were taken apart — 19–0 at Christchurch, 33–12 at Auckland, the All Blacks' Fergie McCormick alone kicking a then-world-record twenty-four points. At home the golden generation were kings; away, against the one team that measured them honestly, they were found out. The three defeats of New Zealand that Welsh people cherish still all belonged to the past — 1905, 1935, 1953 — and this best of all Welsh sides never added a fourth.

The larger omen was economic, and the historians who wrote the coalfield thesis saw it plainly. This team was the last great harvest of an order that was about to be destroyed. The clubs that fed it were fed, in turn, by working pits and working steelworks and the tight chapel-and-colliery communities around them — and all of that was standing, in the 1970s, on ground that the next decade would blast away. Dai Smith and Gareth Williams's *Fields of Praise* appeared in 1981, at the very hinge, an official history that was also, unmistakably, an elegy: it understood that the game it was celebrating and the society that produced it were the same thing, and that the society was ending.

### What Generation 4 leaves behind

The most loved team in the country's history, and a mythology the nation has been living inside ever since. Three Grand Slams, four straight Triple Crowns, the only Lions series ever won in New Zealand, and four seconds at the Arms Park in January 1973 that are still shown wherever the game is played.

And an ending that arrived faster than anyone believed possible. Mervyn Davies was gone by 1976, his career stopped by a brain haemorrhage on a Cardiff pitch. Gareth Edwards and Gerald Davies and Phil Bennett all walked away in 1978; JPR held on to 1981. When they went, the system that had produced them was still there — but the country underneath the system was about to change beyond recognition. It would be **twenty-seven years** before Wales won another Grand Slam. The men who made the golden era were, though almost none of them knew it, the last generation to be carried up out of a coalfield that still existed. In 1979 the pits were open. Within a decade most of them would be gone, and Welsh rugby would go down with them.

## Generation 5: The Pits Close (1980–2002)

**January 1989.** The best fly-half in Britain signed for a rugby league club. **Jonathan Davies** of Llanelli and Wales — quick, brilliant, the nearest thing his generation had to Barry John — went north to Widnes for a world-record fee of around £230,000, and Welsh rugby reacted as though a death had occurred, which in a sense it had. He was not the first and would be nowhere near the last. But he was the emblem, and the shape of his going was exactly the shape of Jim Sullivan's going seventy years before: the finest player Wales could produce, leaving a country that would not pay him for a code that would, at the precise moment the economy that might have kept him home was being destroyed. The valleys had been here before. They were about to find out how much worse it could get.

### The pits close

The coalfield thesis has a terrible final chapter, and this is it. In the 1980s the thing that had made Welsh rugby — the coal, and the tight colliery-and-chapel communities built on it — was not merely declining, as it had declined between the wars. It was deliberately dismantled. The **miners' strike of 1984–85**, solid almost to a man across South Wales, ended in defeat in March 1985, and after it the pit-closure programme ran through the valleys until there was almost nothing left. The last deep mine in South Wales, **Tower Colliery** at Hirwaun, survived only because in 1995 its own miners bought it — 239 of them putting in £8,000 each to keep it open — and even Tower finally closed, its reserves exhausted, in 2008. A coalfield that had employed a quarter of a million men in 1913 came, within a lifetime, to nothing.

What that did to the communities is the real story, and it is not a rugby story until you remember where Welsh rugby had always come from. The clubs of the valleys were the colliery, at play. When the colliery went, the work went, and then the young men went, and the crowds and the subscriptions and the whole dense social fabric that had turned a collier into a wing-forward on Saturday went with them. Dai Smith and Gareth Williams had understood, writing *Fields of Praise* at the very start of the decade, that the game and the society were one thing. In the 1980s the society was taken apart, and the coalfield thesis predicted, with grim precision, what would happen to the game.

### The flood, one more time

What happened first was the oldest thing in this book, at a scale never seen before. With union still amateur and the valleys emptying of work, rugby league came shopping, and Wales sold its team. Jonathan Davies to Widnes was the famous one, but he went in a crowd: **Terry Holmes** to Bradford Northern in 1985, **Stuart Evans** to St Helens in 1987, **Adrian Hadley** to Salford and **David Bishop** to Hull Kingston Rovers in 1988, **Paul Moriarty** and **Jonathan Griffiths** and **John Devereux** north around the same time, **David Young** and **Allan Bateman** and **Rowland Phillips** in 1990, and later **Scott Gibbs** and **Scott Quinnell**, the latter for a record fee for a forward. By the mid-1990s Wales had lost the better part of a first-choice international side to the north.

It was, exactly, the trap that had caught the James brothers in 1892 and Jim Sullivan in 1921, sprung again on a whole generation at once. And it is worth saying plainly, because Welsh rugby did not always say it kindly at the time: these men were not deserters. They were skilled workers accepting good money in a country that had, once again, arranged its affairs so that a poor man had to choose between the sport he loved and the wage his family needed. The union that could not pay them kept the moral high ground and lost the players. The professional code that paid them got the best of Wales, over and over, for a hundred years — and never more than in the decade the pits closed.

### The collapse

On the field the effect was what the thesis said it would be. There were flares, as there always are: at the **1987 World Cup**, a depleted Wales somehow finished **third**, beating Australia 22–21 in the play-off, though the tournament also produced the warning shot of a **49–6** semi-final annihilation by New Zealand. In **1988** Wales won a **Triple Crown**, the first since the golden days — and then went to New Zealand that summer and were beaten **52–3** and **54–9**, kings at home and cannon fodder away, precisely as in 1969. After that the flares mostly stopped. Between 1989 and 1995 Wales finished bottom of the Five Nations five times. And in **October 1991**, at the Arms Park, at a World Cup Wales was co-hosting, the national side lost **16–13 to Western Samoa** and went out in the pool stage, prompting the joke that has followed the fixture ever since — that it was a good job Wales had not been playing the *whole* of Samoa. The country that once taught the world to play could no longer get out of its own group at home.

### The trap springs

Then, in **August 1995**, the thing Welsh rugby had spent a century being punished by simply ended. Under pressure from satellite television, from a threatened breakaway, and from the plain impossibility of pretending that international rugby was a hobby, the International Board declared the game **open**. Professionalism, the sin for which Wales had lost Arthur Gould and Jim Sullivan and Jonathan Davies, was now the rule. Wales could pay its players at last.

And here the trap did the cruellest thing of its long life. It sprang the other way. For a hundred years the problem had been that Wales was too amateur to keep its players; now the problem was that Wales was too *poor* to keep them, because the game had gone professional in a country whose wealth had just been mined out of it, against opponents — the English and French clubs above all — with money Wales could not dream of. The amateur cage opened, and Wales walked out into a marketplace it could not afford. The nadir came on the **1998 tour**, when a Welsh side coached by Kevin Bowring was beaten by South Africa **96–13** at Loftus Versfeld in Pretoria — the heaviest defeat in the country's history — in a year that also brought a 60–26 hammering by England and a 51–0 shut-out by France. Wales did not even have a home to lose in: the Arms Park had been demolished to build the Millennium Stadium for the 1999 World Cup, and for two years the national team played its "home" games at **Wembley**, in England, a borrowed ground for a borrowed era.

### The branches rejoin, and the Redeemer

But professionalism, having sprung the trap, also began — slowly — to heal the oldest wound. Because once union could pay, the exiles came home. **Jonathan Davies** returned to Welsh rugby in 1995; **Allan Bateman**, **Scott Gibbs**, **Scott Quinnell** and **David Young** followed over the next year. The men that amateurism had driven north for a hundred years could, for the first time, come back — and the generation the league clubs had bought became, in union whites, the core of Wales's recovery. It is the same movement this book watched in Uruguay, where the branch that left the trunk in 1900 rejoined it a century on. The thing that had bled Welsh rugby dry was also, in the end, the thing whose ending let it begin to refill.

What Wales did with the returning players was reach, as it always reaches, for a saviour. In 1998 the WRU appointed a New Zealander, **Graham Henry**, and when he won ten Tests in a row the country crowned him with the title it keeps in reserve for the coach who will finally fix everything: **the Great Redeemer.** Under Henry, Wales beat **South Africa for the first time in its history**, 29–19, at the opening of the Millennium Stadium in June 1999. And two months earlier, at Wembley, in the very last match of the old Five Nations, **Scott Gibbs** had cut through the England defence in the final minute and **Neil Jenkins** had kicked the conversion to win it **32–31** and deny England the Grand Slam — one of the sweetest afternoons the modern Welsh game has known, delivered by a man who had spent four years playing rugby league.

The redemption did not last, because it never does. The deeper problem — that Wales had a professional national team and no professional structure worth the name beneath it — did not go away because a charismatic New Zealander had been hired to paper over it. In February 2002, after Ireland beat Wales **54–10**, Henry left by mutual agreement, the Redeemer redeeming nothing structural, and his assistant **Steve Hansen** inherited a side that was about to be torn up and rebuilt from the club upward.

### What Generation 5 leaves behind

The pits closed, and Welsh rugby fell as far as it had fallen in the 1920s — its communities gutted, its best players sold north one last time, its national team beaten by ninety-six points and playing its home games in London. That is the low point of the entire chapter, and it is not a coincidence that it arrived in the exact decade the coalfield was abolished. The thesis held to the bitter end: the game rose with coal, and it fell with coal, and the coal was gone.

But two things had changed by 2002, and both would shape everything after. Professionalism had finally ended the century-long haemorrhage north and even begun to reverse it — the exiles were home. And the WRU had run out of other options. With no coalfield left to produce players by accident, and no money to compete club-for-club with England and France, Welsh rugby was about to make a deliberate, radical and bitterly divisive decision about how a small, poor country could survive in the professional age. It would tear down the historic clubs that had carried the game since the 1870s — Cardiff, Swansea, Llanelli, Newport, Neath, Pontypool — and build, in their place, a handful of **regions.** It would be the most controversial bargain in the history of Welsh rugby, and the argument about whether it was salvation or sacrilege has not ended yet.

## Generation 6: The Bargain (2003–2019)

**Spring 2004.** A rugby team that had existed for less than a year, and had just finished a creditable first season, was taken out and killed. The **Celtic Warriors** — the region cobbled together from Bridgend and Pontypridd, two proud clubs of the coalfield's edge — were wound up by the Welsh Rugby Union barely twelve months after being created, when their backer withdrew and the WRU decided that Wales could afford four professional sides but not five. Supporters who had been told a year earlier that the future was regional now watched the future be abolished by the same people who had promised it. It was a small, brutal illustration of the decade's central word: **bargain.** Wales had made a trade. To field a competitive national team in the professional age, a poor country would sacrifice the club game that had carried its rugby since the 1870s. The Celtic Warriors were the first thing the bargain cost. They were not the last.

### The cull

The architect was **David Moffett**, an Australian administrator brought in to save a bankrupt union, and in April 2003 he announced the end of the old order. The historic first-class clubs — Cardiff, Swansea, Llanelli, Newport, Neath, Pontypool, Bridgend, Pontypridd, names that ran back to the founding of the game in the coal towns — would no longer be the professional face of Welsh rugby. In their place stood five, then four, **regions**: Cardiff Blues, the Ospreys, the Scarlets and the Newport Gwent Dragons.

To understand why this was a wound and not merely a reorganisation, you have to remember what those clubs were. In the England chapter, clubs are the story of a middle-class game. In Wales they were the story of the collier — Pontypool's front row, Llanelli beating the All Blacks, the works and pub sides that had speckled every valley since the 1880s. To tell a town like Neath or Pontypool that its club was now beneath the top tier of the national game was, to that town, to sever the thread between the pit and the jersey that the whole of this chapter has been following. The supporters who never forgave it were not being sentimental. They understood, correctly, that something was being ended that could not be rebuilt.

And yet the case for the bargain was real, and this book will not pretend otherwise. Wales was broke. The old club system could not sustain fifteen or twenty professional squads competing for the same shrinking money; the golden generation was gone and nothing coherent had replaced it; England and France were pulling away. Moffett's regions were an attempt — cold, unloved, and arguably correct — to concentrate scarce resources into a handful of sides strong enough to develop international players. The tragedy of the decade is that the case for the bargain and the grief at its cost were both entirely justified, and that the argument between them has never been settled, because both sides were right.

### The Slam nobody saw coming

What the bargain bought became clear with astonishing speed. In **2005**, only two seasons into the regional era, Wales won the **Grand Slam** — its first since 1978, ending a twenty-seven-year drought that had begun the moment the golden generation retired. It came from nowhere and it was gorgeous: **Gavin Henson**, all fake tan and self-belief, thumping a monstrous late penalty to beat England; **Shane Williams** jinking through defences no one else could see gaps in; **Martyn Williams** the player of the tournament; Gareth Thomas the captain. A country that had spent the pit-closure years being beaten by ninety-six points was, suddenly, champion of the northern hemisphere.

It did not hold together off the field. Within a year the coach, **Mike Ruddock**, had abruptly resigned, in February 2006, and Welsh rugby promptly told itself the most dramatic possible story: a player revolt, senior men forcing out the coach who had just won the Grand Slam. It made a gripping legend, and it is worth noting that the reliable record does not actually support it. Ruddock's departure was officially a matter of contract and family, and the lurid "player power" version — however often it has been repeated — has never been firmly stood up. What is beyond dispute is that Wales had won a Grand Slam and could not keep its coaching arrangements stable for twelve months afterward, which was, in its way, the more worrying fact.

### The actual messiah

Then came the reflex this book has watched Wales perform since the 1960s: when in trouble, find a saviour. This time it worked. After Wales went out of the **2007 World Cup** in the pool stage — beaten **38–34 by Fiji** in a Nantes thriller that cost Gareth Jenkins his job — the WRU appointed a New Zealander named **Warren Gatland**, and paired him with the former rugby league great **Shaun Edwards** as defence coach. Where Graham Henry had been the Redeemer who redeemed nothing structural, Gatland simply won. In his very first Six Nations, **2008**, Wales took the **Grand Slam**, opening by beating England at Twickenham for the first time in twenty years. He would win two more, in 2012 and 2019 — the first coach in history to win three — and stay for twelve years, the longest and most successful reign since the golden age.

The style was direct, physical, built on big centres crashing the gain-line and a suffocating Edwards defence; opponents christened it "Warrenball," a label Gatland always rejected, and around it grew a side that became as familiar as the 1970s greats: **Sam Warburton** and **Alun Wyn Jones** in the back row and second row, **Leigh Halfpenny** at full-back, **Jamie Roberts** and **Jonathan Davies** in the centre, **George North** on the wing, **Dan Biggar** at ten, **Taulupe Faletau** at the back of the scrum. For a decade Wales was, once again, genuinely good.

### The near-miss decade

Genuinely good, and repeatedly, agonisingly short. The Gatland years were defined as much by the matches Wales nearly won as by the ones it did. At the **2011 World Cup**, a young Welsh side reached the semi-final and ran into catastrophe on eighteen minutes, when captain **Sam Warburton** was sent off for a tip-tackle — a red card most of the rugby world thought harsh — and Wales, down to fourteen for over an hour, lost **9–8 to France**, Leigh Halfpenny's long late penalty falling agonisingly under the bar. It was the best chance any Welsh team has had to reach a World Cup final, and it turned on a referee's judgement of a tackle and the fifteen inches by which a kick fell short.

In between the near-misses came real silver: another **Grand Slam in 2012**, and in **2013** a title sealed by dismantling England **30–3** in Cardiff, one of the great modern Welsh performances. This was not a fluke decade. It was a genuinely strong national team, produced — and here is the uncomfortable part — by the very bargain the country still resented. The regions people mourned were also developing the players who filled Gatland's side. The trade was working exactly as designed, at exactly the human cost its critics had warned of.

### The 1893 tax, mutated

Underneath the results, the oldest problem in this book was quietly rewriting itself for the professional age. For a hundred years, Wales had lost its best working men to rugby league because union would not pay them. Now union paid — but the Welsh regions, funded out of a small and shrinking union, could not pay what the rich clubs of England and France could pay. And so the drain simply changed its destination. Where once a Jonathan Davies went north to Widnes, now a **Jamie Roberts** went to Paris and a **Leigh Halfpenny** to Toulon, chasing salaries no Cardiff or Llanelli region could match. It was the exact same tax that had been levied on Welsh rugby since the Gould affair of 1897 — a poor country cannot keep its best players — mutated into a new currency.

Wales fought it with the tools of the professional era. In 2014 the WRU and the regions signed a truce and introduced **National Dual Contracts**, jointly funding a handful of star players to keep them at home. In 2017 came the so-called **"Gatland's Law,"** a rule that a player based outside Wales could not be picked for his country unless he had sixty caps — an attempt to make leaving cost something. These were sensible measures, and they treated a symptom. The disease was structural and it was old: a national game living beyond the means of the economy beneath it, at perpetual war with itself over who controlled the money — the union or the regions. The golden results of the Gatland decade hid that war. They did not end it. And when the results stopped, the war would consume everything.

### 2015, and the annus mirabilis

Two seasons stand at the decade's edges. At the **2015 World Cup**, drawn into a brutal pool with England and Australia, an injury-ravaged Wales — losing frontline players almost weekly — beat **England 28–25 at Twickenham** and knocked the host nation out of its own tournament, in a match remembered for England's fateful late decision to kick for the corner instead of taking the draw. Wales's reward for that heroism was to limp, on emptied reserves, into a quarter-final it lost **23–19** to South Africa, a Fourie du Preez try settling it near the end. It was the near-miss decade in a single fortnight: magnificent, brave, and just short.

And then, in **2019**, everything arrived at once. Wales won a third Grand Slam under Gatland, went to the World Cup in Japan, and in August, for the first and only time in its history, was ranked the **number-one team in the world.** It reached the semi-final and lost, again narrowly, **19–16 to South Africa**, a late Handre Pollard penalty ending it. Gatland then went home to New Zealand, his twelve years over, and handed to his successor **Wayne Pivac** a national side at the summit of the world game.

### What Generation 6 leaves behind

A paradox, and a debt. The bargain worked. The cold, hated decision to scrap the historic clubs and concentrate everything into four regions produced, within two years, the first of three Grand Slams, a world number-one ranking, and a decade in which Wales was again one of the best teams on earth. Anyone who says regionalisation failed has to explain the 2005, 2008, 2012 and 2019 Grand Slams.

But a bargain is a thing you pay for, and Wales had been paying in instalments it preferred not to count. It had severed the thread between the towns and the professional game and never reconnected it. It had built a glittering national team on top of four regions that were chronically underfunded and locked in a cold war with their own union. It had papered the structural cracks with the results of a golden coach who was now gone. In 2019 Wales stood, briefly, at the top of the world. Almost nobody noticed that the platform it stood on was rotten, that the regions beneath the national team were sliding toward insolvency, and that the union governing all of it was one scandal away from open crisis. The bill for the bargain was about to be presented in full — and the decade that followed would be the worst the Welsh game has known since the pits closed.

## Generation 7: The Deepest Valley (2020–2026)

**The last week of February 2023.** For the first time in its history, the Welsh national rugby team met to decide whether to go on strike — whether to refuse to play the Six Nations match against England that Saturday. Their grievance was that the union employing them was in such disarray that some eighty players did not know whether they would have contracts in four months' time. And they were making that decision in the same fortnight that the union itself, the Welsh Rugby Union, stood publicly accused of running a workplace poisoned by sexism, racism and misogyny. On the field, the best team in the world of 2019 was falling apart. Off it, the institution that governed the whole Welsh game was on fire. Both crises arrived in the same week, and together they opened the deepest valley Welsh rugby has descended into since the 1920s.

### The false dawn

It had not looked like this at the start of the decade. Gatland's successor, another New Zealander named **Wayne Pivac**, actually won the **2021 Six Nations** — a genuine championship, if not a Grand Slam, played out in the empty, echoing stadiums of the pandemic. But it was the last good thing for a long time. In **March 2022** Wales lost at home to **Italy** for the first time ever, 22–21, beaten by a try in the final minute against a side that had not won a Six Nations match in seven years. That autumn Wales lost to Georgia, also for the first time. By December 2022 Pivac was gone, and the WRU reached, as it always reaches, for the man who had saved it before: it brought back **Warren Gatland.** The prophet was recalled. This time there would be no redemption.

### The union in the dock

Before Gatland could take a training session that mattered, the ground opened beneath the whole organisation. In **January 2023** a BBC investigation broadcast allegations that the WRU was, as a workplace, institutionally sexist and discriminatory. A former general manager of the women's game, **Charlotte Wathan**, described a culture in which a colleague had joked openly about raping her; an anonymous employee described being driven to the edge of suicide. The chief executive, **Steve Phillips**, resigned within days. An independent review, chaired by the retired Court of Appeal judge **Dame Anne Rafferty**, was commissioned, and when it reported later that year it did not spare the institution: it found the culture of the WRU to have been "unforgiving, even vindictive."

This book has tried throughout to explain its institutions from the inside rather than to hiss at them, and that discipline is worth keeping here, where it is hardest. The men who had run Welsh rugby were not cartoon villains; they were the products of an insular, self-protective, chronically underfunded body that had never modernised its governance and had spent decades believing that the rugby was all that mattered. But explaining is not excusing, and the review's own words stand: real people were harmed, and the harm was not incidental to how the institution worked — it was how the institution worked. Welsh rugby's governing body had to be rebuilt almost from scratch, and it began to be: a new and first female chief executive, **Abi Tierney**, arrived in 2024, and the union's first independent chair with her. But the reckoning consumed the very years the national team most needed steadying.

### The strike

The players, meanwhile, were paying for the structural war that the golden Gatland decade had merely hidden. The regions were running out of money; the contracts of scores of internationals had been frozen while the union and the regions fought over a funding deal; and the players were still bound by the resented sixty-cap rule. In the week before the England game in February 2023 they came within a day of striking, and only a hurried agreement — which among other things cut the sixty-cap threshold to twenty-five — got the match played. It is a remarkable thing for a rugby-obsessed nation to have nearly not fielded a team against its oldest rival because the players had lost faith in the people running the sport. It happened, and it happened because the bargain of the previous generation had never been properly paid for.

### The record

Then the results caught up with the chaos, and they were historic in the worst way. Wales had one last good run in it — at the **2023 World Cup** the team topped its pool, beating Australia 40–6 on the way — before losing the quarter-final **29–17 to Argentina**, after which the last of the great Gatland-era players departed, among them **Alun Wyn Jones**, retiring as the most-capped Test player the sport had ever produced. And with them went the last of the competitiveness. Wales lost every match of the **2024 Six Nations**, taking the wooden spoon, and simply kept losing. The run reached **eighteen consecutive Test defeats** — the worst losing streak any tier-one rugby nation has ever recorded — and stretched over some six hundred and forty days. Along the way came a first-ever home defeat by Fiji. The team that had been ranked number one in the world in 2019 fell to **fourteenth**, its lowest ever.

Gatland could not stop it, and did not stay to try. In **February 2025**, after a fourteenth straight defeat — at home, to Italy — he left by mutual consent, the second coming ending not in a Grand Slam but in the worst run in the country's history. His interim replacement, **Matt Sherratt**, finally halted the bleeding on **12 July 2025**, when Wales beat **Japan 31–22** in Kobe to win a Test match for the first time in six hundred and forty-four days.

### The cull comes for the regions

Underneath all of it, the structure was still failing in the way the Bargain had always risked. The four regions were now, a generation after the historic clubs were sacrificed to create them, themselves close to insolvency — and in 2025 the WRU concluded that even four professional teams were more than Welsh rugby could afford, and set out to reduce the number to **three by the end of the decade.** In April 2025 Cardiff, one of the four, went into administration and had to be taken over outright by the union; a year later a proposed rescue collapsed, and the WRU was left allocating a shrinking number of professional licences by tender, with the Ospreys and the Scarlets openly feared for. The cull that had killed the clubs in 2003 had come, twenty-odd years on, for the regions that replaced them. The severing of the thread between the towns and the top of the game — begun in the 1980s when the pits closed, formalised in 2003 when the clubs were demoted — was reaching its logical end, with the professional game contracting toward a handful of teams in a country that had once fielded a rugby club in every colliery village it had.

### The turn

There is one thing at the bottom of the valley that is not simply despair, and it is small but real. When the WRU appointed a permanent head coach to replace Gatland, it did something it had not done in a long time: it chose a Welshman. **Steve Tandy**, a former collier's-country flanker from the Ospreys who had built his reputation coaching defence for Scotland and the Lions, took charge in September 2025 — the first Welsh-born permanent head coach of Wales since 2007. After sixty years of the messiah reflex, of importing a Redeemer whenever things got dark, a broke and humbled union turned instead to one of its own. Whether that is wisdom or merely the exhaustion of a country that can no longer afford a foreign saviour, only the next generation will show.

### Where things stood, mid-2026

By the middle of 2026, the picture was honest and hard. Under Tandy, Wales had come through the 2026 Six Nations with a single win — a 31–17 defeat of Italy in Cardiff that ended a three-year run of Championship losses — but had finished with the wooden spoon for the third year running, and sat around twelfth in the world, a nation that had been first only seven years earlier. The governance of the game was being rebuilt under new leadership but was not yet rebuilt. The professional tier was being cut from four teams to three, with the losers not yet chosen. And ahead, in 2027, lay a World Cup in Australia that Wales would go to not as a contender but as a country trying to prove it still belonged at the top table at all.

### What the whole chapter leaves behind

Read from the beginning, the Welsh story is the clearest case this book contains of its own thesis, run all the way through to its end. Rugby has no class; institutions do — and in Wales the institution that produced the game's character was not a union or a school but the **coalfield.** The coalfield made Welsh rugby the people's game, crossing the class divide England fortified; the coalfield gave it the golden eras and the song and the greatest team the sport has seen; and the coalfield, dying, took the whole edifice down with it, exactly on schedule, decade by decade, as the seams closed. The amateur ideology bled Wales's best men north for a century; professionalism ended that bleed and opened a new one, to England and France; and the Bargain that was meant to save the modern game hollowed out the base that the coal had once filled for free.

Now the coal is gone — the last deep mine closed in 2008 — and with it the accidental machine that used to turn colliers into wing-forwards. That is the real meaning of the deepest valley. It is not the losing streak, which will end, and has. It is that Welsh rugby must now **build on purpose**, out of a rebuilt union and a shrunken professional game, the thing that coal used to build for it by simply existing. The country that gave the world the four three-quarter system, the singing before the match, and four seconds of Gareth Edwards at the Arms Park has been asked the hardest question a rugby nation can be asked: who are you, when the thing that made you is gone? As of the middle of 2026, with a Welshman at the helm and a World Cup in Australia on the horizon, Wales did not yet have the answer. But it was, at last, being made to look for it in the right place — not in the next saviour, but in itself.
