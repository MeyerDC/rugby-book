# France

## Generation 0: The British in Le Havre and Paris (1872–1892)

**20 March 1892. Bagatelle, in the Bois de Boulogne.** The first French rugby championship is decided in a single match between the two great clubs of Paris, and the referee is **Baron Pierre de Coubertin**.

Racing Club de France beat Stade Français **4–3**. It is close to the end: *le Stade Français rate de peu une pénalité égalisatrice lors de la dernière minute de jeu* — Stade Français narrowly miss an equalising penalty in the final minute.

Two years after this afternoon, the man with the whistle will found the International Olympic Committee. He is, at this point in his life, the most articulate advocate in France of the English public-school games ethic — of athletics as moral training, played by gentlemen, for nothing.

Hold the picture, because the rest of this chapter dismantles it. Within forty years French rugby will have left Paris for the farms and tanneries of the south-west, will be paying its players under the table, and will be expelled from international rugby by the very unions whose amateur creed Coubertin spent his life exporting.

---

Where the game came from is a more interesting question than the standard answer allows.

The standard answer is **Le Havre, 1872**. British merchants — former Oxford and Cambridge men working in the trading houses of the port — had been gathering on open ground by the sea to play; in 1872 the crews of two Royal Navy vessels in the harbour came ashore and played them. The club they belonged to became **Le Havre Athletic Club** in 1884, and is routinely described as the oldest sports club in France.

There are two problems with it, and French scholarship has been blunt about both.

The first is that what they were playing was not rugby. It was **"la combination"** — an English word, kept in English — and it was a deliberate **mixture of rugby and association football**. In the academic treatment of the subject, Pascal Charitas describes it as a game "without pre-defined conditions" as to rules or the dimensions of the field: not one code or the other but a hybrid, and one that lasted at Le Havre for over two decades.

The second problem is why it lasted. Charitas's finding is that the club's own statutes **marginalised rugby and football separately, in favour of the hybrid, from 1872 to 1894** — and that this looks less like confusion than like **policy**. Keeping one combined game stopped the club splitting into a rugby section and a football section, in an era when omnisports clubs were competing for members and prestige and could not afford to divide.

That is worth stating plainly, because it is this book's argument appearing in the very first paragraph of French rugby. **The reason France's oldest club played neither code cleanly for twenty-two years was institutional.** Not a preference of the players, not the character of the port, not anything about the games themselves. A committee found that an undivided club was stronger than a divided one, and so the sport the members played was a compromise between two others.

As for the primacy of Le Havre, Charitas opens by quoting the historian **Jean-Pierre Bodis** flatly: *"rugby arrives in France at Le Havre in 1872. This is false!"* Bodis's own candidates are **Paris in 1877** and **Bordeaux in 1892** — and it is the second of those, as the next generation shows, that turned out to matter.

The honest version, then. Rugby did not arrive in France at a place on a date. It seeped in through British commercial communities in several ports and in the capital across the 1870s and 1880s, in forms that were not yet clearly one game or another, and the tidy founding story was assembled afterwards. This book has watched the same thing happen at Batumi and Poti, at Coronel and Iquique, and in the archival silence of Montevideo in the 1880s.

### The Paris clubs

What is not in doubt is where the game first became organised, and among whom.

**Racing Club de France** and **Stade Français** were founded in the early 1880s by young men of the Parisian bourgeoisie — lycée and university men, the sons of the professional class. They are the French equivalent of Dublin University and Wanderers, or of Blackheath and Richmond: metropolitan, well-connected, and comfortably placed to treat sport as a matter of principle rather than of wages.

They also had an organiser. From **1887** the **Union des Sociétés Françaises de Sports Athlétiques** — the USFSA — gathered French athletic clubs into a single body, and in time it would run rugby along with everything else. It is a specific kind of institution and worth naming as such: not a rugby union but a general sporting federation, of the amateur, improving, Coubertin sort, in which rugby was one activity among many.

So the game that reached its first championship in 1892 was a Parisian, bourgeois, amateur possession, administered by men who believed in athletics as character-building, and refereed on the day by the future founder of the Olympic movement.

### What this generation leaves behind

A capital city's game, and a founding myth that will not survive inspection.

By 1892 French rugby had two strong clubs, a governing body that was not really a rugby governing body, a championship consisting of one match, and an origin story about a port on the Channel that its own historians would later reject.

What it did not yet have was any of the things it would become famous for. There were no crowds to speak of, no money, no violence worth remarking on, and no reason at all to think that this particular English import would end up belonging to the wine-growers and stockmen of the Garonne rather than to the young gentlemen of the Bois de Boulogne.

That happened next, and it happened fast — and the vehicle was the same one that carried the game to Montevideo and Valparaíso. British merchants, in a port, selling something.

In the case of France, the something was wine.

## Generation 1: The Parisian Game (1893–1920)

**1 January 1906. Parc des Princes.** France play their first international, and the opponents are the best team that has ever existed.

The New Zealanders touring Europe that winter are the **Originals** — Dave Gallaher's side, three months at sea and then unbeaten across Britain, playing a game nobody there has seen before. The Bibliothèque nationale's account of them is admiring: *ces joueurs de classe, virevoltent ballon en main, relancent inlassablement et marquent beaucoup d'essais* — players of class, twisting and turning with ball in hand, endlessly counter-attacking, scoring a great many tries. They have won **thirty-one of thirty-two** matches before they reach Paris.

The French press has no illusions. A notice in December 1905: *"Ce match, dans lequel les nôtres n'ont aucune chance de vaincre, est un évènement considérable dans tous les milieux sportifs"* — this match, in which ours have no chance of winning, is a considerable event in all sporting circles.

It is played in the wet. France lose **38–8**, ten tries to two, the French tries scored by **Cessieux** and **Dufourcq** with a conversion from **Pujol**. The captain is **Henri Armand**, the first man to lead a French XV.

And then look at the team sheet, because it is not what a reader of the previous four chapters would expect.

Two of the fifteen are not French at all: **William Crichton**, an English full-back from Le Havre, and **Allan Muhr**, an American second row from Racing. And, in the Bibliothèque nationale's phrasing, *autre particularité de taille* — another notable peculiarity — **la France ce jour-là compte deux joueurs de couleur, André Vergès et le Guyanais Georges Jérôme**: France that day fielded two players of colour, André Vergès and Georges Jérôme, a Guyanese.

In January 1906. Set that beside the chapter this book gives to South Africa, where the state spent the twentieth century using this sport to build a race; or beside the Home Unions, who at that moment were policing their own game to keep working men out of it. France's very first international side contained an Englishman, an American, and two men of colour, and nobody appears to have thought it worth more than a line.

---

The organised French game of this period was, nonetheless, a Parisian and bourgeois affair, and the Bibliothèque nationale de France names its character exactly: **les cols blancs du Racing ou du Stade français** — the white collars of Racing and Stade Français.

Those two clubs and their circle ran the championship, supplied most of the national side, and understood the sport in the terms Coubertin had taught: an amateur pursuit, morally improving, played by men whose living came from elsewhere. The **USFSA**, the general athletics federation that administered rugby along with everything else, held the line on their behalf.

It was already failing.

### The scourge

The money arrived before the crowds did, and it arrived from the clubs.

The BnF's account of the period is blunt about the mechanism: *les clubs français fleurissent pour recruter les meilleurs joueurs contre une rémunération garantie* — French clubs were competing to recruit the best players **against a guaranteed payment**. The USFSA's response was administrative and revealing: it invented **a licence for foreigners**, specifically in order to prevent *le commerce de talents*, the trade in talent.

Hold that phrase against the England chapter. The RFU met the same problem in the 1880s and answered it with a doctrine — an amateur creed, written into the laws, that made payment a moral offence and drove twenty-two clubs out of the union in 1895. The French answer, twenty years later, was a paperwork control on imported players.

The difference matters, because it tells you what each body thought it was defending. The RFU was defending a definition of who ought to play. The USFSA was trying to stop clubs outbidding each other.

### 1920

The war took the generation that would have run French rugby into its twenties, and killed a great many of them — including, on the other side of the world's admiration, Dave Gallaher himself, whose Originals the BnF notes would soon be *rattrapés par un autre devoir : défendre la liberté*.

What emerged afterwards was a governing body of rugby's own. **In October 1920 the Fédération française de rugby emancipated itself from the USFSA** and became a full sporting federation in its own right; *la FFR existe officiellement depuis le 11 octobre 1920*, succeeding the USFSA's rugby committee. Its first leader was **Frantz Reichel**, a Racing man and an experienced administrator, who had left the presidency of the SCUF in 1919 to chair a provisional committee set up to deal with the USFSA's decay.

It began with nothing. *Si elle est sans le sou dans un premier temps, cette toute jeune Fédération cherche à maintenir l'idée de l'amateurisme ; elle doit pourtant lutter contre le « fléau » qui gangrène le rugby des clubs : l'argent.* Penniless at the outset, the young federation set out to maintain the idea of amateurism — and found itself fighting the **scourge** eating into club rugby: **money**.

A federation founded broke, committed to amateurism, and already losing to it. Everything that happens to French rugby in the next twenty years is in that sentence.

### What this generation leaves behind

**241 clubs**, and a centre of gravity about to move four hundred miles south.

That is the figure the BnF gives for the FFR's membership at its creation in 1920. By the 1930s it would be **close to six hundred** — and the growth would not be in Paris. *En dessous de la Loire, le rugby s'implante vigoureusement, chaque village a son club, chaque club a son derby.* **Below the Loire**, rugby took root hard: every village with its club, every club with its derby.

And with it went the ownership of the game. *Le rugby n'est plus seulement l'affaire des cols blancs du Racing ou du Stade français, c'est aussi celui des territoires et des petits entrepreneurs* — rugby was no longer only the business of the white collars of Racing and Stade Français; it belonged now to the regions, and to small businessmen.

The white collars kept the federation. They lost the sport.
