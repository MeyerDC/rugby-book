# France

## Generation 0: The British in Le Havre and Paris (1872–1892)

**20 March 1892. Bagatelle, in the Bois de Boulogne.** The first French rugby championship is decided in a single match between the two great clubs of Paris, and the referee is **Baron Pierre de Coubertin**.

Racing Club de France beat Stade Français **4–3**. It is close to the end: *le Stade Français rate de peu une pénalité égalisatrice lors de la dernière minute de jeu* — Stade Français narrowly miss an equalising penalty in the final minute.

Two years after this afternoon, the man with the whistle will found the International Olympic Committee. He is, at this point in his life, the most articulate advocate in France of the English public-school games ethic — of athletics as moral training, played by gentlemen, for nothing.

Hold the picture, because the rest of this chapter dismantles it. Within forty years French rugby will have left Paris for the farms and tanneries of the south-west, will be paying its players under the table, and will be expelled from international rugby by the very unions whose amateur creed Coubertin spent his life exporting.

---

Where the game came from is a more interesting question than the standard answer allows.

The standard answer is **Le Havre, 1872**. British merchants — former Oxford and Cambridge men working in the trading houses of the port — had been gathering on open ground by the sea to play; in 1872 the crews of two Royal Navy vessels in the harbour came ashore and played them. The club they belonged to became **Le Havre Athletic Club** in 1884, and is routinely described as the oldest sports club in France.

There are two problems with it, and French scholarship has been blunt about both.

The first is that what they were playing was not rugby. It was **"la combination"** — an English word, kept in English — and it was a deliberate **mixture of rugby and association football**. In the academic treatment of the subject, Pascal Charitas describes it as a game "without pre-defined conditions" as to rules or the dimensions of the field: not one code or the other but a hybrid, and one that lasted at Le Havre for over two decades.

The second problem is why it lasted. Charitas's finding is that the club's own statutes **marginalised rugby and football separately, in favour of the hybrid, from 1872 to 1894** — and that this looks less like confusion than like **policy**. Keeping one combined game stopped the club splitting into a rugby section and a football section, in an era when omnisports clubs were competing for members and prestige and could not afford to divide.

That is worth stating plainly, because it is this book's argument appearing in the very first paragraph of French rugby. **The reason France's oldest club played neither code cleanly for twenty-two years was institutional.** Not a preference of the players, not the character of the port, not anything about the games themselves. A committee found that an undivided club was stronger than a divided one, and so the sport the members played was a compromise between two others.

As for the primacy of Le Havre, Charitas opens by quoting the historian **Jean-Pierre Bodis** flatly: *"rugby arrives in France at Le Havre in 1872. This is false!"* Bodis's own candidates are **Paris in 1877** and **Bordeaux in 1892** — and it is the second of those, as the next generation shows, that turned out to matter.

The honest version, then. Rugby did not arrive in France at a place on a date. It seeped in through British commercial communities in several ports and in the capital across the 1870s and 1880s, in forms that were not yet clearly one game or another, and the tidy founding story was assembled afterwards. This book has watched the same thing happen at Batumi and Poti, at Coronel and Iquique, and in the archival silence of Montevideo in the 1880s.

### The Paris clubs

What is not in doubt is where the game first became organised, and among whom.

**Racing Club de France** and **Stade Français** were founded in the early 1880s by young men of the Parisian bourgeoisie — lycée and university men, the sons of the professional class. They are the French equivalent of Dublin University and Wanderers, or of Blackheath and Richmond: metropolitan, well-connected, and comfortably placed to treat sport as a matter of principle rather than of wages.

They also had an organiser. From **1887** the **Union des Sociétés Françaises de Sports Athlétiques** — the USFSA — gathered French athletic clubs into a single body, and in time it would run rugby along with everything else. It is a specific kind of institution and worth naming as such: not a rugby union but a general sporting federation, of the amateur, improving, Coubertin sort, in which rugby was one activity among many.

So the game that reached its first championship in 1892 was a Parisian, bourgeois, amateur possession, administered by men who believed in athletics as character-building, and refereed on the day by the future founder of the Olympic movement.

### What this generation leaves behind

A capital city's game, and a founding myth that will not survive inspection.

By 1892 French rugby had two strong clubs, a governing body that was not really a rugby governing body, a championship consisting of one match, and an origin story about a port on the Channel that its own historians would later reject.

What it did not yet have was any of the things it would become famous for. There were no crowds to speak of, no money, no violence worth remarking on, and no reason at all to think that this particular English import would end up belonging to the wine-growers and stockmen of the Garonne rather than to the young gentlemen of the Bois de Boulogne.

That happened next, and it happened fast — and the vehicle was the same one that carried the game to Montevideo and Valparaíso. British merchants, in a port, selling something.

In the case of France, the something was wine.
