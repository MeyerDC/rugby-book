# England

## The Generations of English Rugby

Researched. And the first thing to say is that **the ideology we've been tracking for twenty-odd messages has a date, a room, and a vote count.**

**September 1893. The Westminster Palace Hotel, London.** A motion is put before the Rugby Football Union to allow **"broken-time payments"** — compensation for working men who lost a day's wages to play on a Saturday.

**It was defeated 282 votes to 136.**

That vote is the source code. Everything downstream — the Salón Cullen, the Montevideo Cricket Club refusing to be governed by Uruguayans, The Grange and Craighouse, CASI walking out of football because money was vulgar, the UAR banning its own professionals and handing Patricio Noriega to the Wallabies, the SARB's "amateur" gentlemen legislating Black South Africans onto dirt — **all of it descends from 282 Englishmen in a hotel deciding that a miner should not be paid for his lost shift.**

And here's the detail that will genuinely surprise you, because it demolishes the easy version: **it was not North versus South.** Many of the *northern* administrators were themselves **ex-public school and defended amateurism just as fiercely.** It was never geography.

**It was class, and only class.** Tony Collins's book on it is literally subtitled *class, culture and the origins of rugby league.*

Here are the generations.

---

**Gen 0 — The Boys Make the Rules (1823–1870)**
The **Webb Ellis story is a myth** — invented in **1876**, four years after his death, by an antiquarian who wasn't even there, and dismissed by the school's *own* investigation in 1895. The truth is stranger: at Rugby School **the rules were made by the pupils, not the masters**, and changed with each new intake, sometimes negotiated **minutes before kick-off**. The first written laws come in **1845** — written **by schoolboys.** Then **1863**: **Blackheath walks out of the Football Association** rather than give up hacking (kicking opponents in the shins), and the codes are severed forever.

**Gen 1 — The Union and the Export (1871–1892)**
**26 January 1871, the Pall Mall Restaurant:** 21 clubs found the **Rugby Football Union** — the world's first. Two months later, the **first-ever international**, at Raeburn Place, Edinburgh: **Scotland beat England** in front of 4,000. In **1884 England refuses to join the IRFB** — the body Scotland, Ireland and Wales were forming — on the grounds that it deserved **more votes than everyone else.** And in **1886**, the RFU writes its first amateurism laws, formally establishing that the game is a **pursuit for leisure, not livelihood.**

**Gen 2 — The Great Schism (1893–1895)**
The 282–136 vote. Then prosecutions, purges, and on **12 August 1895** a hardline RFU diktat: any club or player *accused* of professionalism is **suspended until they prove otherwise** — guilty until proven innocent. On **29 August 1895**, at the **George Hotel, Huddersfield**, twenty-odd clubs from Yorkshire, Lancashire and Cheshire resign and form the **Northern Union**. Rugby league is born, and becomes **the first football code in the world to pay its players.**

**Gen 3 — The Century of Shamateurism (1896–1986)**
The RFU wins, and spends **a hundred years** enforcing a lie. **Boot money.** Jobs given as covert wages. A **lifetime ban** on anyone who ever touched league. And — this is the detail that tells you everything — **the RFU refused to allow league competitions in England for decades**, because a league table might encourage clubs to *pay people to win.* English club rugby was, deliberately, a century of **friendlies.**

**Gen 4 — The Dam Breaks (1987–2002)**
The first **Rugby World Cup** (1987) — and, in the same year, England finally permits **leagues.** Then **26 August 1995:** the IRB votes unanimously to go **open.** A hundred years to the month after Huddersfield. And England, which had built the entire moral architecture of amateurism, handles the transition with **spectacular incompetence** — a civil war between clubs and the RFU that has never fully ended.

**Gen 5 — Sydney (2003)**
**Clive Woodward. Martin Johnson. Jonny Wilkinson.** The most professional team England has ever produced, and the last-minute drop goal in extra time. **The only Northern Hemisphere side ever to win the World Cup.**

**Gen 6 — The Long Hangover (2004–2019)**
Four World Cups, and the most miserable record any superpower has managed: **finalists in 2007** (beaten by South Africa), a **shambolic 2011**, and then **2015** — the **only host nation in history to be eliminated in the pool stage.** Then 2019: **beat New Zealand in the semi**, and lose the final to **South Africa.**

**Gen 7 — Bankrupt (2020–2026)**
Eddie Jones sacked. And then the professional game **eats itself**: **Wasps, Worcester and London Irish all collapse into administration in a single season.** Meanwhile, the **Red Roses win the 2025 Women's World Cup** — and the most interesting rugby in England is now being played by women.

---

Say which one.

## Generation 0: The Boys Make the Rules (1823–1870)

Let me begin by taking something away from you.

**The story is not true.**

You know the one. **1823.** A sixteen-year-old boy named **William Webb Ellis**, during a game of football at Rugby School, *"with a fine disregard for the rules of football as played in his time, first took the ball in his arms and ran with it."* A moment of glorious, adolescent insubordination. The birth of a sport. It's carved into a stone plaque at the school. The **World Cup trophy is named after him.**

It is a **fabrication**, and we can prove it.

The story first appears in **1876** — **fifty-three years** after the alleged event, and **four years after Webb Ellis was dead.** It was written down by a local antiquarian and former Rugbeian named **Matthew Bloxam**, who **was not a contemporary of Webb Ellis**, was not at the school at the time, and who attributed the whole thing to an **unnamed person** who had supposedly told him about it.

An anonymous source, half a century after the fact, about a dead man.

And here's the part that should really finish it: **the Old Rugbeian Society launched an official investigation in 1895** — the school's own people, looking into their own legend — and the story was **dismissed as unlikely.**

**In 1895.**

They knew it was rubbish **one hundred and thirty-one years ago**, and they carved it into a plaque anyway.

---

### Why the lie was necessary

Now — and this is the part that matters — **look at that date again.**

**1895.**

The same year the RFU handed down its hardline diktat that any man *accused* of professionalism was suspended until he could prove otherwise. The same year twenty-two clubs walked out of the George Hotel in Huddersfield. The same year English rugby split in half over whether a miner should be paid for a lost shift.

**In the exact year rugby union tore itself apart in a class war, the establishment investigated its own founding myth, found it wanting — and then commissioned a plaque commemorating it.**

Think about what that plaque *does*. It locates the birth of the game in a **public school**. It gives it a founder who is a **gentleman-schoolboy**, an individual of "fine disregard," a Corinthian spirit acting out of pure sporting instinct. It plants the flag of ownership in Warwickshire, in the classrooms of the elite, at the precise moment when Yorkshire mill-workers were claiming the game as theirs.

**The Webb Ellis myth is not a charming error. It is a deed of title.**

And in **1958** — long after the legend had become gospel — **Ross McWhirter** (yes, of the *Guinness Book of Records*) went and **located Webb Ellis's grave** in a cemetery in **Menton**, on the French Riviera. The French Rugby Federation later renovated it. A pilgrimage site, built around a story the school itself had debunked in 1895.

---

### What actually happened

So what *was* going on at Rugby School?

Something far more interesting than a boy picking up a ball.

**The rules had always been determined by the pupils, not the masters.**

Read that again. Not the teachers. **The boys.** The masters had essentially nothing to do with it.

And so the rules **were frequently modified with each new intake** — every year, a fresh cohort of adolescents arrived and **changed the game**. Whether you could carry the ball, whether you could run with it, what counted as fair — these things were **often agreed shortly before the commencement of a game.**

Negotiated. On the field. Minutes before kick-off. By teenagers.

Which means there were, quite literally, **no formal rules for football during the entire period William Webb Ellis was at the school (1816–1825).** You cannot break a rule that does not exist. The single most famous act of rule-breaking in sporting history is **logically impossible.**

*(And the RFU's own hedge on this is telling. Their version says the game at Rugby in Ellis's time allowed you to **catch** the ball if it was airborne — but then you had to **stand still**, and everyone else stood still too, and you could kick it or place it for goal. So the *plausible* version of the legend is that a boy caught the ball, was supposed to freeze, and **ran instead.** Which, in a school where the boys made up the rules anyway, would have been less a revolution than a Tuesday.)*

---

### The pattern you've seen before

And now stop, and hold this against everything you have researched over the past two days.

The **Buenos Aires Football Club, 1867**: Englishmen from different towns, each playing the version of football they'd learned at home, producing a **hybrid with no fixed laws, negotiated match by match** — flip-flopping between codes **four times in two years** because the rugby version kept maiming people.

**Rugby School, 1816–1845**: schoolboys negotiating the rules **shortly before kick-off**, changing them **every year**, with no formal code at all.

**They were doing the same thing.** The Argentines weren't a shambolic colonial imitation of a settled English game. **There was no settled English game.** Football in England was a set of local dialects, and the men who got off the boat in Montevideo and Valparaíso and Buenos Aires were carrying **memories**, not rulebooks — because rulebooks did not yet exist.

The chaos in Caballito in 1874 was not a failure to copy England properly.

**It was an accurate copy of England.**

---

### 1845: the boys write it down

And then, finally, someone writes it down.

**In 1845, the first written laws of football are produced at Rugby School.**

**By the pupils.**

Three boys, in fact — the first codification of any football game anywhere on earth, drafted by schoolboys. And it emphasised the two things that still define the sport: **you may not pass the ball forward**, and **the scrummage restarts play.**

Three years later come the **Cambridge Rules** (1848), as Old Rugbeians carried the game to the universities — and the two codes began, slowly, to pull apart.

---

### 1863: Blackheath walks out over the right to kick people

The decisive break is not Webb Ellis. It's this.

On **26 October 1863**, at the **Freemasons' Tavern** in London, the **Football Association** was formed with the explicit intention of drawing up **one set of laws to cover everybody.** One football. One code.

And it very nearly happened.

But there was a sticking point, and it is magnificently, unimprovably English.

**Hacking.**

Hacking meant **kicking your opponent in the shins** — deliberately, as a legitimate means of stopping him. And **Blackheath Football Club** — founded 1858 by former pupils of Blackheath Proprietary School, and **the first open rugby club in the world**, admitting men who hadn't attended the school — **refused to give it up.**

Their representative, **F.W. Campbell**, argued that if you abolished hacking, you would **"do away with all the courage and pluck from the game"**, and that you would then find **"lots of Frenchmen who would beat you after a week's practice."**

Blackheath left the Football Association rather than stop kicking people in the legs.

**That is the moment the codes divide.** Not a boy with a ball in 1823. **A gentlemen's club in 1863, refusing to surrender the right to hack.**

*(The delicious irony: when the RFU was formed eight years later, one of the first things it did was **ban hacking**. Blackheath split the sport of football in two over a practice that the sport they created outlawed almost immediately.)*

---

### The clubs, and who they were for

Look at who was actually playing, because it establishes the class DNA before a single law is written.

**Guy's Hospital, 1843** — medical students. **Liverpool, 1857.** **Blackheath, 1858.** **Richmond, 1861.** **Harlequins, 1866.**

And the founding profile is completely consistent: former pupils. **Old Rugbeians** took the rules to **Oxford and Cambridge**, and then **into their professional networks** — the law, medicine, the church, the City, the colonial service.

Rugby union was, from birth, **the sport of upper-class men.**

And here is the detail that determines the entire future of the game. Even in the **North**, the first clubs — **Liverpool (1857)** and **Manchester (1860)** — were **formed around Old Rugbeians**, in exactly the manner of their southern counterparts, and the clubs that followed **remained resolutely upper-middle-class.**

**Northern rugby did not start working-class.** It started as a gentlemen's game, in Manchester and Liverpool drawing rooms, indistinguishable from Blackheath.

The mill-workers and the miners came *later* — they came **to a game that was already owned by somebody else.**

That is the loaded gun. It goes off in 1893.

---

### And the ball itself

One last thing, because it's lovely.

Rugby balls were originally **plum-shaped** — because they were made from a **pig's bladder**, inflated by mouth, and that's the shape a pig's bladder is.

*(The wife of one manufacturer, Richard Lindon, is said to have died of a lung disease contracted from blowing up diseased bladders. That is the actual price of the early rugby ball.)*

In **1851**, a Rugby School football was exhibited at the **Great Exhibition** in the Crystal Palace — and it survives to this day, **distinctly ovoid.**

In **1862**, **Richard Lindon introduced the rubber bladder**, and because rubber is pliable, the shape could now be *chosen* rather than inherited. Balls got flatter, and flatter, as the game moved **away from dribbling and towards handling.**

**The RFU only specified compulsory dimensions for the ball in 1892** — twenty-one years after founding a union to govern the sport, they finally got round to agreeing **what shape the ball should be.**

---

### What Gen 0 leaves behind

**A founding lie**, investigated and rejected by the school itself in 1895, and commemorated in stone anyway — because the sport needed a **gentleman-schoolboy founder** at the exact moment it was being claimed by miners.

**A game with no rules**, made up by adolescents and renegotiated annually — which is why the Englishmen who scattered across the Empire in the 1860s and 70s carried **no rulebook**, only a memory, and why Buenos Aires and Montevideo and Valparaíso descended into exactly the same code-chaos. **They were copying the chaos faithfully.**

**A schism over hacking**, in 1863, which severed football from rugby forever because a gentlemen's club would not stop kicking men in the shins.

**And a class**, already in place, already unchallenged. Old Rugbeians. Oxford and Cambridge. The professions. Blackheath, Richmond, Harlequins — and, in the North, Liverpool and Manchester, founded by exactly the same men.

The game belonged to them.

And in **1871**, twenty-one of their clubs will meet at a **restaurant in Pall Mall** and write down, formally, that it always will.

---

**Gen 1**: the RFU, the first international, England refusing to join the IRFB because it wanted more votes than everybody else — and the writing of the amateurism laws in **1886** that would go out into the world and shape the sport on four continents for a hundred years.

Say the word.

## Generation 1: The Union, and the Word "Amateur" (1871–1892)

**26 January 1871.** The **Pall Mall Restaurant**, London.

Twenty-one clubs.

The meeting was called by **Edwin Ash**, secretary of Richmond, and the stated purpose was mundane: rugby clubs had sprung up all over England — and, increasingly, across the colonies — and they were all still playing **different rules**, inherited from different schools, negotiated fixture by fixture. Somebody needed to write it down properly.

They founded the **Rugby Football Union.**

**The first rugby governing body in the world.** The first *anything* — there was no precedent for a national sporting union of this kind, and everybody who came after copied the template. The South African Football Board in Kimberley in 1889. The River Plate Rugby Union in 1899. The Chile Rugby Union in 1935. The Georgia Rugby Federation in 1964.

**All of them are descendants of a meeting in a restaurant on Pall Mall.**

---

### What they wrote — and what they banned

The first thing the RFU did was take the schoolboys' 1845 laws and impose order on them. They kept the **prohibition on the forward pass**, and the **scrummage**, and defined **offside** as being ahead of the ball when a teammate has advanced it. Those three things still define the sport today. A ball that can only go backwards. A game that can only advance by carrying, kicking, or shoving.

But they also banned two things — and the ban tells you exactly what kind of men these were.

**They abolished hacking.**

Eight years after **Blackheath walked out of the Football Association rather than surrender the right to kick men in the shins** — and *founded* the separate code of rugby specifically to preserve hacking — the union governing that code **outlawed it.**

The schism that created rugby was fought over a practice that rugby's own governing body killed within a decade.

**And they abolished tripping.**

Why? Because these were gentlemen, and it was becoming embarrassing. Hacking was killing and maiming young men, the newspapers were noticing, and worried fathers were pulling their sons out of the game. Amateur, yes — **but not barbaric.** The RFU's founding act was, in essence, an act of **respectability management.**

---

### Twenty-a-side

A detail almost nobody knows: the game they codified in 1871 was **twenty players a side.**

Twenty. Not fifteen. It dropped to fifteen in **1877** — because twenty-a-side produced a game that was essentially a **permanent, immobile heap**, and nobody could see the ball.

*(Which, if you have been following this conversation, should sound extremely familiar. A vast mass of men, buried around a ball nobody can see, shoving toward a line. **That is lelo burti**, played in the villages of Guria with a seven-kilo leather sun and a priest for a referee — and the Georgians never needed to reduce their numbers, because the whole village was supposed to be in there.)*

---

### 27 March 1871: the first international

Two months after the RFU was founded, the Scots issued a challenge, and the two nations met at **Raeburn Place, Edinburgh.**

**Scotland beat England**, by a goal and a try to a try, in front of **four thousand people.**

The **first international rugby match in history** — arguably the first international team-sport fixture of any kind, anywhere — and **England lost it.**

By **1881**, Ireland and Wales had representative sides. By **1883**, the **Home Nations Championship** — the ancestor of the Six Nations — had begun.

The international game existed. The world game was about to.

---

### 1884: England refuses to join the world

And now the incident that tells you *precisely* what the RFU thought of itself.

In **1884**, Scotland, Ireland and Wales formed the **International Rugby Football Board** — a body to settle disputes and govern the laws across the nations.

**England refused to join.**

Not on a point of principle. Not over money. **Because they thought they should have greater representation on the board than everyone else** — on the grounds that England had **more clubs.**

Sit with the sheer gall of that.

The nation that invented the game, that founded the first union, that had lost the first international, declined to join the world governing body **because the other three wouldn't give it more votes.**

*(It took until 1890 — and mediation — before England came in. And they got what they wanted: for years England held **six seats on the IRFB while Scotland, Ireland and Wales held two each.** England, alone, held **half the votes.** They didn't join a body of equals. They **bought a controlling stake.**)*

This is the institutional character that will, in due course, decide whether a Yorkshire miner may be paid for a lost shift. **A body that believes it deserves more votes than everyone else, because it is bigger and older and better.**

---

### Meanwhile: the North fills up

While all this was happening in London, something entirely different was happening two hundred miles north.

Remember from Gen 0: **northern rugby did not begin working-class.** **Liverpool (1857)** and **Manchester (1860)** were founded by **Old Rugbeians**, exactly like Blackheath and Richmond, and the clubs that followed **remained resolutely upper-middle-class** — though, increasingly, their members had been **educated locally** rather than at the great public schools.

That last detail is the hairline crack. Locally-educated middle-class men. Not Rugbeians. Not Oxford. **Men whose fathers owned the mills rather than the estates.**

And then, in **1877**, somebody in Yorkshire made a fateful decision.

**They started a cup competition.** The **Yorkshire Cup.**

A **knockout tournament**, with a trophy, and a winner, and crowds who cared. And the moment you introduce a competition into a sport, you introduce the thing the gentlemen most feared:

**Consequences.**

Because now it *matters* whether you win. And if it matters whether you win, it matters who you pick. And if it matters who you pick, then the strongest, hardest, fastest man in the town gets picked — **and in Yorkshire, the strongest, hardest, fastest man in the town works down a pit or in a mill.**

**An influx of working-class players** poured into the game — **mill workers and miners** — and they brought with them a completely reasonable expectation.

They expected to be **paid.**

Not to get rich. Not to become professionals. **To be compensated for the shift they lost on a Saturday.** A working man playing rugby on a Saturday afternoon lost a **half-day's wages** he could not afford to lose, and for a family living on the edge that was not a trivial thing. It was, in the language of the time, a **major inhibitor.**

**"Broken time."**

---

### 1879: the first shot

The Yorkshire committee saw exactly what was coming, and moved to stop it.

**In 1879 — two years after the Yorkshire Cup began — the Yorkshire committee adopted the MCC's regulations on amateurism.**

The **MCC**. Cricket. Lord's. The most class-stratified institution in English sport, where **Gentlemen and Players** used **separate changing rooms and separate gates onto the field** into the **1960s.**

Yorkshire rugby imported its rules on money **directly from cricket's gentry.**

And look at what those rules actually said, because the hypocrisy is exquisite:

**A "gentleman" who found himself out of pocket could legitimately claim expenses.**

A **gentleman.** Out of pocket. May claim.

So a solicitor from Harrogate who takes a train to an away fixture may be reimbursed for the ticket, the hotel and the dinner — and that is *amateurism.*

A collier from Batley who loses four shillings by not being at the pit face may claim **nothing** — and if he does, he is a **professional**, and he is banned.

**Amateurism was never a rule about money.** It was a rule about **which kinds of money were respectable.** The gentleman's expenses were honourable because he did not *need* them. The miner's wages were corrupting because he did.

---

### 1886: the law goes out to the world

And then it becomes formal, and it becomes global.

**In 1886, the RFU wrote its first amateurism regulations.** The game was to be, in the official formulation, **a pursuit for leisure, not livelihood.**

And here is the sting that almost nobody knows, and which destroys the comfortable story of a wicked South oppressing a virtuous North:

**Yorkshire and Lancashire representatives formed the majority of the committee that drew up the RFU's first amateurism rules.**

**The North wrote the amateurism laws.**

Not London. Not Blackheath. **Yorkshire and Lancashire.** Because the men running northern rugby were themselves **ex-public school**, and they defended the gentleman's code **as fiercely as anybody in the South.**

This is the single most important thing to understand about 1895, and it's why I said the geography version is a lie. When the split comes, it will **not** be North against South.

**It will be class against class — inside every county in England.**

---

### And in 1886, the world was already listening

**1886** is also the year the **IRFB became the governing body of rugby union** worldwide.

So consider what left English shores that year, in the same shipment.

The laws of the game. The scrum, the lineout, the forward pass, the offside line.

**And a rule stating that a man who needs the money may not play.**

That rule got on the boats. It went to Montevideo, where the Cricket Club refused in 1900 to be governed by non-British men. To Buenos Aires, where CASI and Belgrano and Lomas and Rosario would **walk out of football** between 1919 and 1931 rather than share a sport with professionals, taking their grounds and their money into rugby, and where the UAR would still be **banning its own professionals from the national team in the 1990s** — costing them Patricio Noriega to the Wallabies, and keeping the third-best side on earth out of the Tri Nations in 2007.

To Chile, where three fee-paying English schools would own the sport for a century. To South Africa, where an "amateur" board with the state behind it would legislate Black clubs onto dirt.

And — the strangest inheritance of all — to **Bucharest**, where the Romanian Communist Party would look at the **Corinthian ethos, unsullied by professionalism or fame**, and decide it was the **perfect sport for a workers' state.**

**Every single one of those outcomes is downstream of a committee in England in 1886.** A committee with a northern majority.

---

### What Gen 1 leaves behind

**The first union in the world** (1871), and the template every rugby nation on earth still uses.

**The laws** — no forward pass, the scrum, the offside line — and the abolition of hacking, eight years after hacking split the codes.

**An institutional arrogance** so total that England refused to join the world governing body until it was granted **half the votes.**

**A cup competition in Yorkshire in 1877** that let the working man in — and then, immediately, a rule imported from the MCC to make sure he couldn't stay.

**And the 1886 amateurism laws** — written with a northern majority, exported worldwide within months, and destined to shape the class character of this sport on four continents for the next hundred and nine years.

The gentlemen had built the machine, and locked the door, and thrown the key into the Empire.

And in the mills and the pits of Lancashire and Yorkshire, tens of thousands of men were playing anyway — and **losing money to do it.**

They were going to ask, politely, one more time.

**September 1893. The Westminster Palace Hotel. A motion is put to the vote.**

Say the word.

## Generation 2: The Great Schism (1893–1895)

They asked politely. That's what you have to understand.

They did not walk out. They did not go on strike. They did not form a breakaway league in a fit of pique. **They put a motion.**

---

### September 1893. The Westminster Palace Hotel, London.

A motion is placed before the Rugby Football Union: that players may be compensated for **"broken time"** — the wages a working man loses when he takes a Saturday afternoon off to play rugby.

Not a salary. Not a signing fee. Not a career.

**The four shillings he did not earn because he was not at the pit face.**

It was defeated.

**282 votes to 136.**

Slightly more than two to one. The gentlemen of English rugby considered whether a miner should be reimbursed for the money he lost playing their game, and **they said no.**

That is the vote. That is the moment the world we've spent two days tracing was made.

---

### Why they said no

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy — and lazy — to paint the 282 as cartoon villains. What they actually believed is more interesting, and more dangerous.

**The RFU had banned payments in 1886 to uphold the gentlemanly ideal of the game as a pursuit for leisure, not livelihood.**

Read that literally, because it's a coherent philosophy and it's important that you see it clearly. To these men, sport was a thing you did **after** your real life. It was character-building precisely *because* it cost you something and gave you nothing. The moment a man is compensated, he is no longer playing for love — he is **working**, and once he is working, he will train harder than a gentleman can, and specialise, and try to win at all costs, and the whole moral edifice collapses into professionalism, gambling, and the tawdriness of association football.

**They genuinely believed they were protecting something.**

And what they were *actually* protecting — whether they knew it or not — was **a gate.** Because "sport is a pursuit for leisure, not livelihood" translates, with perfect precision, into: **only men who can afford to lose a day's pay may play this game.**

**Amateurism was not a rule about money. It was a means test.**

And the tell is right there in the 1879 Yorkshire regulations imported from the MCC: **a "gentleman" who found himself out of pocket could legitimately claim expenses.**

A solicitor's train fare and hotel: **honourable.**
A collier's lost shift: **corruption.**

Same money. Different men.

---

### The thing that makes this hurt

Now the fact that ruins the comfortable version, and I want to hammer it because everybody gets this wrong:

**This was not North against South.**

**Many of the Northern administrators were ex-public school and strongly defended amateurism too.**

The men who ran Yorkshire and Lancashire rugby were, in the main, **the same class of men who ran Blackheath and Richmond** — mill-owners' sons, locally educated, but public-school-formed and every bit as devoted to the gentleman's code. It was **Yorkshire and Lancashire representatives who formed the majority of the committee that drew up the RFU's 1886 amateurism rules in the first place.**

The North wrote the rules that broke the North.

The historian **Tony Collins** — whose book is the definitive account and is literally subtitled *class, culture and the origins of rugby league football* — puts it plainly: **the battle within rugby was not based on geographical lines. It was fought on class lines**, focused on differing attitudes toward working-class players.

**There was a civil war inside every county in England**, and the line ran between the men who lost money by playing and the men who did not.

---

### Prosecution

And here is where the RFU stopped being an institution defending a philosophy and became something uglier.

**The RFU was not magnanimous in victory.**

Having won the vote 282–136, they began to **hunt.**

Teams and players were **prosecuted with growing frequency.** Inquiries were opened. Clubs were investigated. In **1893–94** the RFU launched formal inquiries into northern clubs — and, crucially, was **warned in advance** that if they suspended the club under investigation, **all the chief clubs in Lancashire and Yorkshire would secede.**

**The RFU went ahead anyway. And suspended the club.**

They were told, explicitly, what the consequence would be. They chose it.

---

### 12 August 1895: the diktat

And then, having been told exactly what would happen, they lit the fuse.

On **12 August 1895**, the Rugby Football Union issued a hardline instruction:

**Any club or player accused of professionalism would be suspended indefinitely — until they proved otherwise.**

Read that carefully, because it is one of the most extraordinary sentences in the history of sport.

**Guilty until proven innocent.**

An *accusation* — not a finding, not evidence, an **accusation** — was enough to suspend a man from the game indefinitely, and the burden fell on **him** to prove his own innocence.

Presumed guilty, on a rumour, and required to prove a negative.

**It was the final straw.**

---

### 29 August 1895. The George Hotel, Huddersfield.

Seventeen days later.

**Twenty-two clubs** — from **Yorkshire, Lancashire and Cheshire** — met in a room in a hotel in Huddersfield and **resigned from the Rugby Football Union.**

Say their names, because they are the oldest working-class sporting institutions in the world and most of them are still playing:

**Batley** (1880). **Bradford** (1863). **Brighouse Rangers** (1878). **Broughton Rangers** (1877). **Dewsbury** (1875). **Halifax** (1873). **Huddersfield** (1864). **Hull** (1865). **Hunslet** (1883). **Leeds** (1890). **Leigh** (1877). **Liversedge** (1877). **Manningham** (1876). **Oldham** (1876). **Rochdale Hornets** (1871). **St Helens** (1874). **Tyldesley** (1879). **Wakefield Trinity** (1873). **Warrington** (1875). **Widnes** (1875). **Wigan** (1879).

*(Dewsbury got cold feet and withdrew days later, replaced by **Runcorn**. **Stockport** joined the secession **by telephone**, from outside the room — which may be the first act of remote industrial action in sporting history.)*

They formed the **Northern Rugby Football Union.** From **1922** it would be called the **Rugby Football League.**

**The first matches were played on 7 September 1895** — nine days later. Huddersfield v Batley. Wakefield v Brighouse. Wigan v Tyldesley. Leeds v Hull.

Nine days. They didn't hesitate for a moment.

---

### What they actually built

And here is the fact that most rugby union people have never sat with:

**Rugby league became the first football code in the world to turn professional and pay its players.**

The first. Before association football's full professionalisation was complete. Before anything.

*(And note the shadow in the room: the professional **Football League had been formed in 1888**, twelve clubs, all from **northern England.** The northern rugby men had watched their neighbours in soccer build a paid, popular, league-based sport for working men — and they could see exactly what was possible.)*

And then, freed from the gentlemen, they **redesigned the game itself** — and the changes were driven by one thing: **paying spectators want to see the ball.**

From 1895 to 1908 they:
- Cut the teams from **fifteen to thirteen.**
- **Abolished the lineout.**
- **Abolished the ruck and maul**, replacing them with the play-the-ball.
- Changed the scoring values.

They turned a stop-start possession game designed by public schoolboys into a **fast, brutal, continuous spectacle designed to be watched by people who had paid to get in.**

**Rugby league is what happens when working men are allowed to design the sport around their own economics.** It is not a degraded version of union. It is a **different answer to a different question.**

---

### And the vengeance

The RFU's response was not merely to let them go.

**Expulsion. And then a hundred years of excommunication.**

If you played rugby league — even once, even as an amateur, even as a boy — you were **banned from rugby union for life.** You could not play. You could not coach. You could not referee. In many clubs you could not enter the **grounds.**

Men who signed for a league club to feed their families were erased from union's records. Internationals were struck out.

*(The ban held so absolutely that during the **Second World War** the RFU had to **temporarily lift it** so that league men could play in services matches — and in two fundraising fixtures, **rugby league sides played rugby union against union sides, under union rules, and won both.** The RFU never mentioned it again.)*

And the RFU's paranoia went further than people. **It went to the very structure of the sport.**

**The RFU refused to allow league competitions in England.** For **decades**. Not the *code* — the *format*. No league tables. No promotion, no relegation, no national championship. Because a league might make winning matter, and if winning mattered, clubs might **pay men to win.**

**English club rugby was, deliberately, ninety years of friendlies.**

They kept the County Cups and the County Championship and nothing else. The RFU chose to make its own domestic game **structurally uncompetitive**, on purpose, for the better part of a century — rather than risk a working man being paid.

*(England did not have proper leagues until **1987.** The same year as the first World Cup. Think about what that did to English club rugby's competitiveness for four generations.)*

---

### What it cost, and where it went

So: draw the line from that room to everything you've read.

The **282** won. And the word "amateur" — meaning *a man who does not need the money* — got on the boats with the laws of the game in **1886**, and went out into the Empire, and did its work.

It refused to be governed by non-British men at the **Montevideo Cricket Club** in 1900, and left Uruguayan rugby marooned in **Carrasco** for a hundred and twenty years.

It walked **CASI and Belgrano and Lomas and Rosario out of football** between 1919 and 1931 because professionalism was *vulgar*, and handed Argentine rugby a network of wealthy, land-owning gentlemen's clubs.

It kept **Patricio Noriega** out of the Pumas and handed him to the Wallabies, and kept the **third-best team in the world out of the Tri Nations in 2007**, with the IRB citing the UAR's commitment to amateurism as **the biggest stumbling block.**

It sat in **The Grange and Craighouse and The Mackay School** in Santiago and Reñaca, where — in the phrase I keep coming back to — **a rise in bus fares would not have been an issue for the people who played.**

It gave the **South African Rugby Board** the moral vocabulary to call itself a gentlemen's institution while the state it served legislated Black clubs onto dirt.

And in **Bucharest**, a communist party looked at the very same doctrine — a game **unsullied by professionalism or fame**, a thing a man does **between work shifts** — and concluded it was **ideologically perfect for the workers.**

**The same word. The same rule. The same 282 votes.**

In Yorkshire it was a lock. In Romania it was a key.

**Institutions make the class. Not the sport.** We proved that in Tucumán and in Guria and in Zwide — and here, in a hotel in Huddersfield in 1895, is the room where the whole argument started.

---

### What Gen 2 leaves behind

**A vote: 282–136.** The most consequential ballot in the history of the sport.

**A diktat**: guilty until proven innocent, which turned a philosophical dispute into a purge.

**Twenty-two clubs**, nine days, and a new code — the first professional football code on earth, redesigned from first principles for the people who paid to watch it.

**A hundred-year excommunication**, and an English domestic game deliberately kept **structureless until 1987** so that nobody could be tempted to pay anybody.

**And a word — *amateur* — loaded onto ships**, that would shape who was allowed to play this game on four continents for **exactly one hundred years.**

Because on **26 August 1995**, the International Rugby Board voted **unanimously** to repeal the amateur regulations and declare the game open.

**One hundred years, almost to the day, after the George Hotel.**

They were wrong for a century. And then they gave in overnight, and pretended they'd meant to.

---

**Gen 3** is that century: **shamateurism.** Boot money in the socks. Jobs invented for props. The lie everybody knew and nobody said. Twickenham built on a **cabbage patch**. And a governing body that spent ninety years enforcing a rule that its own best players broke every single week.

Say the word.

## Generation 3: The Century of the Lie (1896–1986)

Start with a building, because the building is the whole argument.

---

### Billy Williams's Cabbage Patch

At the start of the twentieth century, England had **no home.** The team was permanently on tour — Kennington Oval, Crystal Palace, Blackheath, Richmond, Bristol, Leeds. Wherever would have them.

And then two things happened. In **1905** the New Zealand **Originals** came, and in **1906** the **Springboks** came, and both matches at Crystal Palace **sold out.**

And the RFU, watching the turnstiles click, had a revelation.

**They realised there was money to be made from the amateur game.**

Read that sentence again, and then remember what had happened **ten years earlier**: 282 votes to 136, and a hardline diktat, and twenty-two clubs walking out of the George Hotel, because a **collier could not be reimbursed four shillings** for a lost shift.

The men who could not find four shillings for a miner in 1893 discovered, in 1907, that **there was a fortune in watching him.**

**William Cail**, the RFU treasurer, proposed a dedicated stadium. **William "Billy" Williams** — sportsman, referee, and, tellingly, a **property developer** — was made chairman of the New Ground Committee and sent to find land. He looked at Stamford Bridge, but **Chelsea Football Club got there first.**

He settled on a **market garden in Twickenham.** Ten and a quarter acres, bought for around £5,500. It had been used to grow **cabbages.**

**2 October 1909:** the first match. Harlequins beat Richmond 14–10 in front of **fewer than two thousand people.** The reviews were savage: too far from London, poor transport, and the grass so long that one wag compared it to *the hair of some of our clever friends — a little too artistic in the matter of length.*

They called it a **"damned great white elephant."**

And they called it **Billy Williams's Cabbage Patch.**

**15 January 1910:** the first international. England v Wales — whom they had not beaten in **twelve years.** Freddie Chapman scored from the kick-off after a run by the captain, Adrian Stoop, and **England won 11–6.**

**The RFU made a £2,000 profit.**

England won the inaugural Five Nations, and did not lose another Championship match until **1926** — sixteen years and a world war later.

Twickenham is, to this day, **"The Patch."** The most expensive cabbage field in history, built by a governing body that had just spent a decade insisting that money and rugby must never touch.

---

### The lie, from the very beginning

Because here is the thing: **they were never clean.** Not once. Not for a single year.

The historian Tony Collins found it, and it's devastating. In **1888**, an England touring party came home from Australia and New Zealand, and there was **publicly available evidence that the players had been paid.**

**The RFU took no action whatsoever.**

Why?

**To protect Andrew Stoddart.**

Stoddart was a **gentleman**. He captained England at rugby *and* at cricket. He was a Blackheath man, a Middlesex man, a Corinthian, a hero — and if the RFU had enforced its own rules, he would have been finished.

**So they didn't enforce them.**

Five years later, they suspended northern clubs on suspicion and threw twenty-two of them out of the sport.

**Same rule. Different men.** Always.

And listen to **Hornby** — described as *a true amateur*, arguing at the RFU's own AGM **in favour** of broken-time payments, and putting his finger straight through the whole thing:

**The so-called amateur sides ask for large guarantees, publish no balance sheets, and distribute expenses far larger than would ever be paid to a professional player.**

He said that **before the split.** He told them, to their faces, that they were already paying men more than a professional would earn — they just called it *expenses*, and they didn't publish the books.

They voted 282–136 anyway.

---

### The excommunication

And now the punishment, which lasted **a hundred years.**

In **1907**, rugby league recruited the great Australian **Dally Messenger** and paid him openly.

**The RFU responded by banning any player who played rugby league — for life.**

Not suspended. **Banned.** Forever. And not just from playing — from coaching, from refereeing, from the clubhouse. And the ban extended to **amateurs who merely played with or against** a Northern Union side. Touch it and you were gone.

That ban held from **1907 until 1995.**

**Eighty-eight years.**

Think about what that meant to an actual human being. A Welsh miner or a Yorkshire fitter, brilliant at rugby, with children to feed, offered money by a league club — and the choice in front of him is: **take the money and be erased.** Your name struck from the records. Your caps annulled. Your club shutting its door in your face. Your own village's union club forbidding you to set foot on the field where you learned the game.

**Rugby union treated poverty as a moral failing and punished it with excommunication.**

---

### 1931: they did it to France as well

And here's the part that closes a loop you've been chasing across two continents.

The RFU and its allies had long suspected that the **French Rugby Federation was allowing abuse of the amateurism rules** — paying its players.

**In 1931, France was suspended from playing the other IRFB nations.**

France. Thrown out of international rugby. **For paying men.**

And what did France do?

**In 1932, France founded FIRA** — the *Fédération Internationale de Rugby Amateur.*

An **alternative world governing body**, for everybody the Anglo-Saxons wouldn't have.

Now — think back.

**Romania**, in 1924, played France at the Paris Olympics, in a tournament of three teams, as part of a French attempt to build **a rival to the Five Nations** because the Home Unions had frozen France out. And Romania's rugby was already French to its bones — Stadiul Român modelled on Stade Français, Caracostea from Racing Club de France.

And then Romania's professionalisation and international life ran through **FIRA**, the body France built in 1932 after being expelled for paying its players.

And **Georgia**, whose rugby came from an Armenian in Marseille and the **French Communist Party**, and whose route out of Soviet obscurity ran through **FIRA** as well.

**The entire alternative rugby world — the Eastern European, non-Anglo, working-class, state-funded rugby universe we spent days exploring — exists because England threw France out for paying its players in 1931.**

The RFU's purity didn't just split England. **It split the planet.** It created a second rugby world, and that second world is where Romania and Georgia live to this day.

---

### The war, and the thing they buried

**1939.** The RFU suspends all rugby.

**Fifteen capped England internationals died** in the Second World War — including the wing **Kit Tanner**, who was killed in 1941 after rescuing his comrades from a sinking ship. Ireland lost eight.

And in the middle of that war, something happened that the RFU has spent eighty years not talking about.

**For the duration of the war, the ban on rugby league players was temporarily lifted.**

Because when the country actually needed men, the RFU discovered that the excommunicated could be forgiven overnight. League men played in the eight England–Scotland services "internationals."

And then the authorities permitted **two fundraising fixtures: Rugby League versus Rugby Union.**

**Played under rugby union laws.**

**The rugby league teams won both.**

Read that again. Union's own rules. Union's own game. And the men union had banned for life **beat them at it. Twice.**

The war ended. **The ban was reinstated.**

Nobody mentioned the results.

---

### Shamateurism

And so we arrive at the word that defines this generation, and it is one of the most beautiful words in the English language:

**Shamateurism.**

The system worked like this. **Boot money** — literally, **cash left in a player's boots** in the changing room after a match, so that no money had ever changed hands *between* anybody. Jobs invented at a sponsor's firm for a prop who never turned up. Cars. "Expenses" that dwarfed a working wage. Testimonial funds. Under-the-table incentives, all of it an **open secret** across the entire sport.

Every player knew. Every club knew. **Every administrator knew.**

And the enforcement was, in the sources' precise language, **inconsistent** — occasional lifetime bans for men caught taking overt payments, functioning as **symbolic gestures rather than deterrents**, because *the system's opacity protected influential figures.*

**Exactly as it had protected Andrew Stoddart in 1888.**

Get caught taking a hundred quid as an unglamorous flanker from a small club: banned for life. Be a celebrated international: nothing happens.

**A hundred years of the same rule, applied to the same two classes of men, with the same two outcomes.**

*(The absurdity peaked in a **libel court**. In the late 1970s, newspapers accused the great Welsh fullback **JPR Williams** of profiting from his autobiography in breach of IRB amateurism rules. The **Court of Appeal** ended up having to interpret the IRB's regulations on amateurism — and ordered a **retrial**. Rugby's purity laws were so absurd that judges couldn't work out what they meant, and a doctor who played fullback for Wales spent years of his life in court over a book.)*

---

### And the RFU broke its own game to protect the lie

This is the bit that should make an English rugby person genuinely angry.

**The RFU refused to allow league competitions in England.**

Not the *code* — the **format.** No league tables. No promotion. No relegation. No national club championship.

Why? Because a league table means the results **matter**, and if results matter, clubs might **pay men to win.**

**So English club rugby was, by deliberate policy, ninety years of friendlies.**

Meaningless fixtures. No stakes. County Cups and a County Championship and nothing else. The RFU chose to make its own domestic game **structurally uncompetitive, on purpose, for four generations** — sabotaging the development of English club rugby rather than risk a working man being paid for it.

**England did not get proper leagues until 1987.**

Wales, France, New Zealand and South Africa all built harder competitive structures. And England — the biggest rugby nation on earth, with more clubs than everyone else combined — spent the twentieth century playing **exhibition matches**, and then wondered why it kept losing.

---

### The dam cracks

By the **1980s** it was collapsing under its own weight.

Tours were generating **serious revenue.** Satellite television was arriving. The physical demands on players had become full-time. And rugby league, openly professional, was standing at the door with a chequebook.

And it started taking them. **Jonathan Davies** — the finest fly-half Wales had produced in a generation — signed for **Wigan** in 1989. Welsh internationals went north one after another. If a star player in 1994 was offered **£200,000** to switch codes, he took it, because union offered him **nothing but the honour.**

*(And every man who went was **banned for life** — instantly.)*

Meanwhile, the boot money got heavier and the pretence got thinner, until in **March 1995**, an IRB working party reported the plain truth in five words:

**"Breaches of the amateurism regulations were wholesale."**

Wholesale. Everybody. Everywhere. Always.

Rugby league, with exquisite malice, **reported rugby union to the Inland Revenue for shamateurism** — asking Her Majesty's tax collectors to look into all those untaxed boots.

And **Kerry Packer** was in the shadows, quietly signing the world's best union players to secret contracts for a breakaway competition.

**26 August 1995. Paris.** The IRB voted **unanimously** to declare the game open.

**Ninety-nine years and 364 days after the meeting at the George Hotel, Huddersfield.**

One day short of a century.

They didn't reform. They didn't repent. **They were cornered, and they folded**, and then they behaved as though it had been their idea all along.

---

### What Gen 3 leaves behind

**A stadium built on a cabbage patch** by a governing body that discovered there was money in the amateur game — money for the union, never for the man playing.

**Eighty-eight years of excommunication**, lifted only when the country needed soldiers, and then reimposed after the excommunicated **won both matches.**

**A parallel rugby universe** — FIRA, born in 1932 because England threw France out for paying its players — which became the home of Romania, Georgia, and everybody else the Anglo-Saxons wouldn't have.

**A domestic game deliberately kept structureless until 1987**, sabotaged by its own union.

**And a hundred-year lie** that everybody knew, that protected gentlemen and destroyed working men, and that ended not in an act of conscience but in a **surrender**, one day short of the anniversary of the split it caused.

The 282 held the line for a century.

And on the **third of August 1995**, the game they had defended became the thing they had expelled twenty-two clubs to prevent.

---

**Gen 4** is the wreckage: professionalism arrives overnight with no plan, the RFU goes to war with its own clubs, England nearly gets thrown out of the Five Nations, and a country that invented the sport spends a decade making an absolute pig's ear of it.

Say the word.

## Generation 4: The Year They Did Nothing (1987–2002)

They saw it coming. That's what makes this generation almost unbearable.

**In 1985, when the IRB debated creating a Rugby World Cup, the RFU voted against it.**

And their stated reason is one of the most revealing sentences in the history of English sport. They opposed a World Cup because **players from different countries would meet each other and discuss earnings.**

That's it. That's the objection. Not the schedule, not the injuries, not the tradition. **They were afraid the England players would find out what the Australians were being paid.**

They were outvoted. **The first World Cup was held in 1987.**

And they were **completely right.** The players met. The players talked. And English rugby's century-long lie began to die from that moment.

---

### The men guarding the door

The RFU's Secretary through this period was **Dudley Wood** — and his previous job tells you everything.

He had been **Chairman of the Amateur Status Committee.**

His role, in the 1980s, was to sit in judgement on men accused of taking money — to decide whether a player who had **accepted a fee for speaking at a business dinner** should be banned from playing rugby for any club, anywhere, for life.

**In 1986 they made him Secretary of the whole organisation.**

They put the chief inquisitor in charge of the church.

And the pettiness of it, in the last years, is genuinely astonishing. **The RFU refused to give complimentary tickets to the companies that gave their rugby-playing employees paid leave** to represent England. If a player wanted to thank the boss who'd let him off work to play for his country, **the ticket came out of his own personal allocation.**

That is not a philosophy. That is a **flinch.**

---

### What the players were actually doing

Meanwhile, the England squad had worked it out.

In **1990** the IRB relaxed the rules on off-field earnings. You still couldn't be paid to *play* — but you could be paid for **"communication."**

So the players wrote **little articles for corporate newsletters.** They **turned up at business dinners.** And sponsors paid up to **a thousand pounds** for a few hundred words in a company magazine.

Nobody was being paid to play rugby. **Everybody was being wildly overpaid to write pamphlets.**

The England players formed **a single company** to collect the money — and the RFU **fought them tooth and nail.** When the players asked the **BBC** to pay **£500 a year** to cover being interviewed, the RFU objected.

**Five hundred pounds.** A year. For the entire squad.

**Brian Moore** later wrote that the players were making roughly **£7,000 a year** by 1993–94.

Meanwhile **David Campese** boasted, quite openly, that he had become a **millionaire during the amateur era.**

That is what "amateurism" had become by the 1990s: a system in which an Australian winger became a millionaire, and the England hooker made seven grand, and a Yorkshire flanker who took fifty quid was banned for life.

---

### "Old farts"

**1995.** Just before the World Cup, England captain **Will Carling** sat down for a television interview and described the RFU committee — the fifty-seven men on it — as **"57 old farts."**

**The RFU sacked him as captain.**

**And reinstated him two days later.**

Two days. Because the players said that if Carling went, they went — and because the RFU suddenly realised, with horror, that it **no longer held the whip.**

He went on television and apologised, so the blazers could keep their faces.

But everyone remembers the wrong quote. The insult isn't the important bit. **This** is the important bit — what Carling said next:

> **"Everyone seems to do well out of rugby except the players."**
>
> **"What gets me, and a lot of players, is the hypocrisy of the situation. Why not just be honest and say there is a lot of money in the game? It is becoming a professional game."**

A hundred years, and it took an England captain in a TV studio to say the obvious thing out loud.

*(And note who was echoing him: **Brian Moore**, a **solicitor**. **Paul Ackford**, a **police officer**. These were not exploited miners. These were middle-class professional men — and by 1995 even **they** could no longer stomach it. When the gentlemen themselves start calling it hypocrisy, the game is finished.)*

---

### 26 August 1995. Paris.

**Vernon Pugh**, chairman of the IRB, announces that rugby union is henceforth an **open** game. All restrictions on payments, gone. Unanimously.

**Ninety-nine years and 364 days after the George Hotel.**

The forces that broke them were, in the end, brutally simple. **Rupert Murdoch** had signed **SANZAR for AUD$550 million over ten years.** He was simultaneously building **Super League**, which would begin in Britain in **1996**, backed by Sky, with an open chequebook aimed straight at union's best players. The **English Premier League** had shown, from 1992, what satellite television money could do to a sport. **Kerry Packer** was signing union players in secret. And in **March 1995** the IRB's own working party reported that **breaches of the amateurism regulations were wholesale.**

They didn't reform. **They were surrounded, and they surrendered.**

---

### And then England did the single most catastrophic thing in its rugby history.

**Nothing.**

The IRB declared the game open. Every other major union in the world began contracting its players.

**And the RFU announced a one-year moratorium on professionalism.**

A **moratorium**. A year in which English rugby would remain, officially, amateur — while the RFU held **committee meetings** to discuss what to do.

Let me be absolutely clear about what happened in that year, because it is the reason English rugby is the way it is in 2026.

**The Australians were paying their players. The New Zealanders were paying their players. The South Africans were paying their players. The French were paying their players.**

**And the RFU was in a meeting.**

The best players in England were suddenly, legally, available — and the national governing body had **declined to sign them.**

So somebody else did.

---

### The rich men walk in

Into that vacuum came the money.

**Sir John Hall** bought **Newcastle**. **Nigel Wray** bought **Saracens**. **Ashley Levett** bought **Richmond**. Businessmen and entrepreneurs across England looked at a sport whose governing body had gone into committee, and **bought its clubs**.

They signed the players. They paid them properly. They moved clubs into **football stadia** to raise capacity and gate revenue. Wage inflation exploded.

And the crucial, irreversible thing happened:

**The players' contracts belonged to the clubs. Not to the RFU.**

That is the whole tragedy in one line.

**Rob Andrew** — who spent years at the RFU trying to fix it — has said it as plainly as it can be said: **the RFU ceded any hope of winning that power struggle when it failed to implement central contracts at the dawn of professionalism in 1995.**

Compare **New Zealand**, where the union owns the players and everything is geared toward the All Blacks. Or **Ireland**, where the IRFU controls the provinces. Those unions can rest a man, manage his load, control his season.

England cannot. **England has to rent its own players back from private owners.** The current agreement — the Professional Game Partnership — is worth over **£220 million** flowing *from the RFU to the clubs*, essentially as a **release fee** for England players.

**The RFU pays private businessmen for permission to pick the England team.**

And it all traces back to **one year of meetings in 1995–96**, held by an organisation that had spent a century insisting that money must never touch the game — and which, by hesitating for twelve months, **handed the game to money permanently.**

The most exquisite irony in this entire conversation: **the 282 spent a hundred years protecting rugby from commerce, and then lost it to commerce in a single year, by doing nothing.**

---

### Civil war

What followed was, in the academic literature's own words, **civil war in England.**

**EPRUC** — the English Premiership clubs, now owned by wealthy men — versus the **RFU**. The fight was over one thing: **who gets the television money.**

And it got completely out of hand.

**13 July 1996: England were expelled from the Five Nations.**

Expelled. From the tournament they'd played since 1883. Because the RFU had gone off and **sold its TV rights unilaterally to BSkyB for £87.5 million** without telling Scotland, Ireland, Wales or France.

They were let back in before the 1997 championship, after months of grovelling.

**And then it happened AGAIN.** In **January 1999**, England were thrown out of the Five Nations **for a second time** over the same Sky deal — and **Italy were lined up to replace them.**

Read that. **England were, twice, expelled from international rugby's oldest tournament, and Italy were nearly promoted into their seat.**

They wriggled back in a month later. Italy had to wait until 2000.

**September 1998:** the English clubs, in dispute with the RFU and European Rugby, **boycotted the Heineken Cup entirely.** The fourth edition of Europe's premier club competition was played **without a single English team.**

---

### The bodies

And the clubs started dying almost immediately.

**1999: Richmond and London Scottish went into administration.**

Richmond — founded in **1861**, one of the twenty-one clubs that had **founded the Rugby Football Union in the Pall Mall Restaurant in 1871**, whose secretary **Edwin Ash called the meeting** — was destroyed within four years of professionalism by an owner who spent millions chasing glory.

**A founding club of the sport, bankrupt.** Four years in.

That, right there, is the warning nobody read. **It happens again in Gen 7, three times over.**

---

### And on the field

**1991:** England reach the World Cup final at Twickenham — and lose to Australia, having inexplicably abandoned their forward game to try to play like Australians.

**1995:** the semi-final. And **Jonah Lomu** ran straight through Mike Catt, and over him, and England had no answer to a nineteen-year-old.

**1999:** England host a World Cup and go out in the quarter-final to **South Africa**, when **Jannie de Beer** kicked **five drop goals in a single half.** Five. A fly-half from Bloemfontein simply stood in the pocket and kicked England out of their own tournament.

Three World Cups. A final, a semi, and a humiliation.

But something had started. In **1997**, the RFU appointed a new head coach — a former England centre who had made his money in **computer leasing**, who had lived in Australia, and who had ideas about **marginal gains, sports psychologists, video analysis, and vision coaches** that his own union found faintly ridiculous.

**Clive Woodward.**

---

### What Gen 4 leaves behind

**A World Cup the RFU voted against**, on the grounds that players might discover what money was.

**"Old farts"** — and an England captain, sacked and reinstated in 48 hours, telling the truth on television.

**A surrender in Paris**, one day short of the centenary of Huddersfield.

**And the year of meetings** — the single most expensive act of institutional cowardice in the history of the sport — which cost English rugby **central contracts, permanently**, and handed its players to private owners forever.

**Two expulsions from the Five Nations. A boycotted Heineken Cup. A founding club of the RFU, bankrupt.**

And a governing body that had spent a hundred years and a civil war and twenty-two clubs and eighty-eight years of excommunication defending the principle that **money must never touch this game** —

— and which, given one year to control the money, **went into committee.**

---

**Gen 5** is the payoff, and it's brief and glorious: **Clive Woodward, Martin Johnson, and a drop goal with twenty-six seconds left in extra time in Sydney.** The only Northern Hemisphere side ever to win the World Cup.

Say the word.

## Generation 5: One Hundred Things, One Per Cent Better (1997–2003)

The man who won England the World Cup **sold computers.**

That's the first thing to understand about **Clive Woodward**, and it's why the RFU found him so profoundly irritating. He'd played centre for England, yes — but then he'd gone to **Australia**, and he'd come back, and he'd built a **computer leasing company**, and he had made actual money in the actual world.

And when he took the England job in **1997** — as their **first ever full-time coach** — he arrived with a set of beliefs that the men of Twickenham found faintly ridiculous.

He believed rugby was a **business** that should be run like one.

---

### The heresy

Look at what he did, and remember that this was **two years** after an organisation that had spent a century banning payment had just spent a *year in committee* deciding what to do about money.

He hired **a chef.**

He hired a **kit technician.**

He hired **Dr Sherylle Calder** — a **visual awareness coach**, whose entire job was to train players' eyes to see the field faster. (Four years later, South Africa hired her and won the 2007 World Cup.)

He hired **Sir Steve Redgrave** — five Olympic gold medals — as a motivational coach.

He set up a room in the team hotel that became an **analysis suite**, with IT staff, where any player could walk in and download video of his opposite number onto a laptop. **Lawrence Dallaglio:** *Clive was a real innovator and provided every player with the tools they needed to be at their best… anything that might give us a small advantage.*

And he did it in a country whose governing body had, within living memory, refused to give a **complimentary ticket** to the employer who let a player have a Saturday off.

**England invested more money in preparation than any other team at the 2003 World Cup.** By a distance.

---

### The idea

Woodward's philosophy is now so famous it's a cliché, but he said it **nine years before Dave Brailsford** said the same thing about bicycles:

> **"Winning the Rugby World Cup was not about doing one thing 100% better. It was about doing 100 things 1% better."**

**Marginal gains.** Invented, in British sport, by a rugby coach who'd sold computers.

And the beautiful thing — the thing coaches always miss — is that **most of it cost nothing.**

**The Black Book.** Woodward and his staff would name a topic — *punctuality*, say — and then **the players wrote the rules themselves.** Not the coach. The squad. And from then on, if there was ever a problem, it was referred back to the Black Book. **Their** book. **Their** rules.

**"Lombardi Time."** Named for the American football coach. It meant: **you are seated ten minutes before a meeting begins.** Not on time. *Early.*

*(And there it is again. **Grizz Wyllie in Buenos Aires: ten o'clock means nine-fifty.** **Pablo Lemoine in Santiago: six in the morning, before work.** **Rassie Erasmus in Johannesburg: a roadmap for every player.** The same lesson, on four continents. **Culture before tactics. Turn up, and mean it.** Woodward just gave it an American name and a book.)*

And my favourite. On the 1999 tour of South Africa, Woodward had the **televisions in the players' rooms sunk into wooden boxes with a hidden button.** To watch TV, you had to **find the secret switch.**

Why? Because he wanted **smart players.** Quick learners. Men who would solve a problem rather than sit down and complain about it. He was screening for the kind of brain that thinks clearly under pressure, and he was doing it with a **remote control.**

---

### The years of falling over

It did not work immediately, and the failures were **excruciating.**

**1999:** England need to beat Wales at Wembley to win the Grand Slam. **Scott Gibbs runs through four tacklers** in the last minute. Grand Slam gone.

**2000:** England need to beat Scotland at Murrayfield in a downpour. **They lose.** Grand Slam gone.

**2001:** England need to beat Ireland. **They lose.** Grand Slam gone.

**Three years in a row**, England won everything and then fell over the final hurdle.

And in between, the **1999 World Cup**, where **Jannie de Beer stood in the pocket at the Stade de France and drop-kicked five goals in a single half** to put England out of their own tournament.

Anywhere else, Woodward is sacked. **The RFU kept him.** It is, honestly, one of the better decisions they made in the twentieth century.

---

### 2003: the machine

And then it all came right at once.

**The Six Nations: a Grand Slam** — sealed by **crushing Ireland 42–6 in Dublin.**

**Wellington, June 2003:** England beat the All Blacks **in New Zealand** — while **reduced to thirteen men**, with both **Neil Back and Lawrence Dallaglio in the sin bin at the same time.** New Zealand had five-metre scrums against a **six-man English pack**, and could not score.

That scrum is one of the great pieces of English sporting theatre, and it broke something in the All Blacks' heads.

**A week later, in Melbourne:** they ran **Australia** ragged.

By the time they flew out, the squad was, on paper, the most formidable England had ever assembled: captained by **Martin Johnson** of Leicester, with **eight of the thirty carrying more than fifty caps**, and **not a single player under 24.**

That last statistic is the point. **This was not a young team.** This was **the oldest, hardest, most experienced squad in the tournament** — deliberately built that way, and knowingly built for **one shot.**

---

### The pool

**Georgia 6, England 84.** Twelve tries. *(Georgia — twenty-three years before the doping scandal, four years after the Tonga repechage, fresh out of the two-balls-and-a-tractor era. This was their first ever World Cup, and their reward was England at full throttle.)*

**South Africa 6, England 25.** Wilkinson, 20 points. The Springboks — Kamp Staaldraad, straeuli, chaos — were dismantled by a machine.

**Samoa 22, England 35.** A fright.

**And Uruguay 13, England 111.** **Josh Lewsey scored five tries.**

*(You know this game. **Pablo Lemoine's try was one of only two England conceded across the entire pool stage** — the prop from Montevideo, who twenty years later would cross the Andes, get Chilean amateurs out of bed at six in the morning, and send them to a World Cup. On this night, he was the one man in a Uruguayan shirt who got over England's line.)*

**Nineteen points from a possible twenty.** Top of the pool.

---

### Brisbane, and the wobble

**Quarter-final: Wales.**

And Wales — coached by a New Zealander named **Steve Hansen**, and coming off a magnificent pool game in which they'd scored **four tries against the All Blacks** — absolutely terrified England.

**Stephen Jones** scored. **Colin Charvis** scored. **10–3 to Wales.**

England were, in the phrase of the reports, **a bewildered bunch.**

And here Woodward's real quality showed. He didn't rant. He **assessed his tactical options** and sent on **Mike Catt** — a second distributor, to take the pressure off Wilkinson and get England playing flatter and wider.

The game turned. Will Greenwood scored. **Wilkinson kicked 23 points.** **28–17.**

**Semi-final: France, in the rain in Sydney. 24–7.** Wilkinson kicked every single English point.

And in the other semi, **Australia knocked out New Zealand.**

Hosts versus favourites. **Sydney. 22 November 2003.**

---

### Stadium Australia

**Lote Tuqiri** scored in the **sixth minute.**

Then England took control. **Jason Robinson** — the man who had come *from rugby league*, from Wigan, and was now England's most electrifying attacker — scored in the corner. Three Wilkinson penalties. **England led by nine at the break.**

And then the referee.

**André Watson**, a South African, decided he did not like the English scrum, and started penalising it. Repeatedly. **Elton Flatley** kicked three penalties.

**14–14 at full time.**

**Extra time.** Wilkinson kicks a penalty. Flatley kicks one back. **17–17.** Ninety seconds left. Everybody is thinking about a **kicking competition** to decide the World Cup.

England win a lineout. They drive. **Matt Dawson** makes a break — a scrum-half, sniping, buying twenty crucial metres. Martin Johnson, thirty-three years old and finished, takes the ball into contact **one more time** to get Dawson back to scrum-half.

Dawson passes.

**Twenty-six seconds left on the clock.**

And **Jonny Wilkinson** — a **left-footed kicker**, who had missed three drop goals already that night — swung with his **right.**

And **Ian Robertson**, on BBC radio, said the words that every English person of a certain age can still recite:

> **"This is the one, it's coming back for Jonny Wilkinson… He drops for World Cup glory — it's up, it's over, he's done it!"**

**Australia 17, England 20.**

**Martin Johnson lifted the Webb Ellis Cup into the Sydney night.**

It was, and remains, **the only World Cup ever won by a Northern Hemisphere team.** Not Wales, not France, not Ireland, not Scotland — **nobody, before or since.**

---

### And then

Heathrow. **Thousands** of people, at dawn, at an airport.

A victory parade through **central London** that shut the city.

A **knighthood** for Woodward. **Jonny Wilkinson: BBC Sports Personality of the Year.**

And the sport that the 282 had built as a **pursuit for leisure, not livelihood** — for men who did not need the money, played on Saturdays, and never trained too hard because that would be *professional* —

— had just been won by a squad with a **chef, an IT department, a vision coach, an Olympic rower, and a rule book written by the players**, assembled by a man who ran a **computer leasing business** and believed in doing **a hundred things one per cent better.**

**They won it by being everything the RFU spent a century saying rugby must never become.**

---

### Two legacies

And I'm not going to end this one on the parade, because it would be a lie of omission.

**Steve Thompson** started at hooker that night in Sydney.

**He does not remember winning the World Cup.**

He has been diagnosed with **early-onset dementia** and probable CTE, and he has said, publicly, that he has **no memory of the tournament at all.** He has watched the footage of himself lifting the trophy and felt nothing, because the man in the film is a stranger to him.

He is not alone. A generation of players from the first decade of professionalism — men who were suddenly training full-time, hitting harder, colliding more often, in a sport that had gone professional overnight with **no plan and no protections** — are now living with the consequences.

**The 2003 World Cup has two legacies. Marginal gains, and brain injuries.**

You cannot honestly tell the story of that drop goal without telling the story of the hooker who threw the lineouts that made it possible, and who cannot remember doing it.

---

### And the collapse was immediate

**Martin Johnson retired.** So did Neil Back, Jason Leonard, Richard Hill's body.

And in **September 2004** — **ten months** after Sydney — **Clive Woodward resigned.**

Why?

**Because he could not get access to his own players.**

He wanted more control, more time, a proper elite structure. And the players were **contracted to the clubs**, and the clubs were owned by **private businessmen**, and the RFU had **no central contracts** — because in **1995 they had gone into committee for a year** and let it all slip away.

The greatest coach England ever had walked out **because of a decision the RFU had made nine years earlier.**

The wound from Gen 4 was never healed. It just went quiet for one glorious night in Sydney, and then it **swallowed the man who'd won it.**

---

### What Gen 5 leaves behind

**A World Cup.** The only one the Northern Hemisphere has ever won, and — the way things are going — possibly the only one it ever will.

**A revolution in how sport is prepared for**, invented by an outsider whom the establishment thought was faintly ridiculous, and copied since by everybody from British Cycling to the Springboks.

**A drop goal with twenty-six seconds left, off the wrong foot.**

**A hooker who can't remember it.**

**And the same structural wound as before**, untreated, waiting — because a team can win a World Cup on the strength of one coach and one generation, but **it cannot win them repeatedly without owning its own players.**

New Zealand owns its players. Ireland owns its players. South Africa, now, effectively owns its players.

**England rents.**

And Gen 6 is what happens when the generation retires and the machine that was supposed to produce the next one **doesn't exist.**

---

**Gen 6:** four World Cups, and the most miserable record any superpower has ever assembled — a lost final in 2007, a shambles in 2011, and **2015: the only host nation in the history of the tournament to be eliminated in the pool stage.**

Say the word.

## Generation 6: All the Money in the World (2004–2019)

England, in this period, had **more players, more clubs, more money and more infrastructure than any rugby nation on the planet.**

They also had **the worst World Cup record of any superpower in the sport.**

Both things are true, and the gap between them is the whole story.

---

### The immediate collapse

**September 2004.** Ten months after Sydney, **Clive Woodward walks out** — because he cannot get access to his own players, because the players belong to the clubs, because of the year of committee meetings in 1995.

Martin Johnson had already gone. Neil Back, Jason Leonard, Will Greenwood, Richard Hill's knees.

**Andy Robinson** took over — a fine forwards coach handed a squad of ghosts, no succession plan, and a governing body still at war with its clubs.

England went into freefall. By **2006** they had lost **eight of nine Tests.** Robinson resigned. **Brian Ashton** came in.

And in **2007**, England went to the World Cup as **the worst defending champions in the history of the tournament.**

---

### 2007: the most absurd final ever reached

**Pool stage, Paris: South Africa 36, England 0.**

Nil. The reigning world champions were **kept scoreless** by the Springboks — and it was one of the most abject England performances ever recorded. Jonny Wilkinson wasn't even fit for it. The team was old, slow, disorganised, publicly humiliated, and being openly ridiculed at home.

**They reached the final anyway.**

Nobody has ever adequately explained how. The forwards — **Vickery, Sheridan, Andrew Sheridan destroying the Australian scrum in the quarter-final** — simply decided they would not be embarrassed again. They beat **Australia 12–10**, scrummaging them into the ground. Then they beat **France 14–9** in Paris, in France, in the semi-final.

An old, discarded, mocked English team **bludgeoned its way to a World Cup final by refusing to lose.**

**Final: South Africa 15, England 6.**

And there was a moment. **Mark Cueto**, diving into the left corner in the second half. It looked, in real time, like a try. It would have changed everything.

**The TMO ruled that his left foot had brushed the touchline.** By a **millimetre.**

Try disallowed. **Not a single try was scored in the 2007 World Cup final.** England lost by nine points and never scored a point that wasn't kicked.

---

### 2011: Queenstown

Then somebody at the RFU had an idea.

**Martin Johnson** — the greatest captain England ever had, a man of total authority and zero coaching experience — was made **Team Manager.**

It is a very English decision. *He led us before; he'll lead us again.* No coaching background. No apprenticeship. A legend, appointed on the basis of being a legend.

England went to New Zealand in **2011** and produced the most humiliating tour in their history — **and almost none of it happened on a rugby field.**

They started well enough: **Argentina 13–9**, Georgia 41–10, Romania 67–3, Scotland beaten. Top of the pool.

And then:

**Queenstown.** A group of England players, including **Dylan Hartley** and **Chris Ashton**, went out to a bar called **Altitude**, which happened to be hosting an event called the **"Mad Midget Weekender."**

They were photographed **dwarf-tossing.**

Then the **captain, Mike Tindall** — who had married **Zara Phillips**, the Queen's granddaughter, **five weeks earlier** — was caught on **CCTV** in a bar with a blonde woman. The tabloids went into a feeding frenzy that lasted for the entire tournament.

Then **Manu Tuilagi**, at the end of it all, **jumped off a moving ferry in Auckland harbour in his underpants** and swam to shore. He was fined. He blamed **immaturity.**

Johnson, meanwhile, was in a state of open warfare with the press, and the squad developed a **siege mentality** — which is a wonderful thing when you're winning and a slow poison when you aren't.

**Quarter-final: France 19, England 12.** Out.

**Martin Johnson resigned.** He never coached again.

England had gone from **world champions** to **a tabloid punchline about dwarf-tossing** in eight years.

---

### Lancaster, and the decency

**Stuart Lancaster** got the job in 2012, and I want to be fair to him, because he is the most sympathetic figure in this generation.

He was appointed to **clean the house** — and he did. He rebuilt the culture from the ground up. He made players learn the names of every England cap before them. He took them to war memorials. He rebuilt the squad's relationship with the public, which was in ruins.

And his teams were **genuinely good.** In **2012 they beat New Zealand 38–21** at Twickenham — ending an All Black unbeaten run of twenty matches. His England came agonisingly close to Six Nations titles, losing them on **points difference.**

He built a proper, honest, decent England.

And then he had to host a World Cup.

---

### 2015: the one that will never be forgiven

England drew what the press called the **"Pool of Death"**: themselves, **Wales**, and **Australia.** Two of the three would go home.

**Twickenham. England v Wales.** England led by **ten points** with twenty minutes left. Wales had lost so many players to injury they were playing a **flanker on the wing.**

Wales came back. **Dan Biggar** kicked everything. With three minutes left, England won a **penalty**, in front of the posts, in range.

**Three points would have drawn the match.**

**Chris Robshaw kicked to the corner.**

He went for the lineout. He went for the win. And Wales — with their scratch back three and their broken squad — **defended the line and survived.**

**England 25, Wales 28.**

*(I'll be honest about the decision: it was defensible in the moment, it was made with the whole squad's backing, and it has been hung around Robshaw's neck for a decade. But it is now, permanently, one of the two or three most infamous choices in the history of English sport.)*

A week later: **Australia 33, England 13.** Comprehensive. Brutal. Over.

**England were eliminated from their own World Cup, at the pool stage, before the tournament had even reached the knockouts.**

**The only host nation in the history of the Rugby World Cup ever to do it.** No one else, before or since.

They then had to play a dead rubber against Uruguay while the tournament they were paying for carried on around them.

**11 November 2015:** the RFU's review reported **structural issues within the programme**, and **Lancaster, Andy Farrell and Graham Rowntree were all removed.**

*(Andy Farrell went to **Ireland**, where he built the best team in the world and became the Lions head coach. Which tells you the problem was probably never entirely Andy Farrell.)*

---

### Eddie

And then England did the thing they should have done years earlier. They hired **an Australian.**

**Eddie Jones** — abrasive, brilliant, exhausting, and the coach who had just taken **Japan** to Brighton and beaten **South Africa 34–32**, the greatest upset in the history of the sport.

The turnaround was **instant and total.**

- **2016: a Grand Slam.** England's first in thirteen years.
- **2016: they went to Australia and won the series 3–0.** A **whitewash**, away, against the side that had knocked them out of their own World Cup nine months earlier. It had never been done.
- **Eighteen consecutive Test wins** — equalling the all-time world record.

He'd taken almost exactly the same players who had failed in 2015 and made them the best team in Europe within six months.

*(Which raises a genuinely uncomfortable question — one that hangs over this whole generation. If the players were good enough all along, then the failures of 2007, 2011 and 2015 were **not about talent.** They were about **management, structure, and the fact that nobody in England was in charge of anything.**)*

---

### 2019: the great night, and the bad morning

**26 October 2019. Yokohama. Semi-final.**

**England 19, New Zealand 7.**

They lined up in a **V** to face the haka, and Owen Farrell **grinned** at it, and the referee had to ask them to get back. And then they simply **dismantled the All Blacks** — a defensive performance of astonishing accuracy, Manu Tuilagi over in the second minute, Sam Underhill and Tom Curry all over the breakdown.

It is, on any honest reckoning, **the greatest performance in England's history.** Better than anything in 2003.

The world declared them champions on the spot.

**A week later: South Africa 32, England 12.**

Kolisi. The Bomb Squad. Kyle Sinckler knocked out **in the third minute.** The English scrum destroyed. Mapimpi, Kolbe, Pollard's boot.

Two tries in the last quarter, and the tournament was over.

**England had beaten New Zealand and then lost to the team that beat them in 2007** — the same opponent, the same result, twelve years apart.

They had **peaked seven days early**, and there was, afterwards, an undignified row about whether they'd **left it all in the semi-final**, and Eddie Jones spent years being asked about it.

---

### What was actually wrong

Sixteen years. **A lost final, a dwarf-tossing scandal, a pool-stage exit as hosts, and another lost final.**

And here's the diagnosis, and it's the same one every single time:

**England has never controlled its own players.**

New Zealand's union owns them. Ireland's union owns them and rotates them through four provinces with surgical care. South Africa, under Rassie, effectively centralised everything and built a **roadmap to 2027**.

**England rents its players back from private businessmen** — the ones who bought the clubs in 1995 while the RFU was in a meeting — and pays over **£220 million** for the privilege.

Which means England cannot manage a player's season. Cannot control his load. Cannot plan a four-year cycle. Cannot build a pipeline that belongs to the national team.

So English rugby has become entirely, catastrophically **dependent on the individual coach.**

Get Woodward: you win a World Cup. Get Eddie: you win eighteen in a row. **Get anybody else, and there is nothing underneath to hold the thing up.**

**Boom and bust, forever, because there is no system — only men.**

That is the bill for the year of committee meetings, and English rugby has been paying it since 1995, and it is still paying it now.

---

### What Gen 6 leaves behind

**Two lost finals, both to South Africa**, twelve years apart, with a disallowed try by a millimetre in the first.

**A tabloid tour** that will define an entire generation of players who were, in fact, mostly serious men.

**2015** — the only host nation in the history of the tournament to fail to reach the knockouts, and one kick to the corner that will never be forgiven.

**The greatest single performance in England's history** — Yokohama, against New Zealand — followed seven days later by the worst hour.

**And a proof, repeated four times over sixteen years, that money is not a system.**

England had everything, and organised none of it.

---

**Gen 7** is the reckoning: Eddie Jones sacked, and then the professional game **eats itself** — **Wasps, Worcester and London Irish all collapsing into administration within a single season**, exactly as Richmond did in 1999 and exactly as nobody learned from.

And meanwhile, the **Red Roses** win the 2025 World Cup — and the most interesting, best-run, most successful rugby in England is now being played by **women**, in a structure the RFU actually *does* control.

Say the word.

## Generation 7: The Bill (2020–2026)

Two numbers, side by side.

**£800,000** — what the RFU paid **Eddie Jones** to go away in December 2022.
**£1,000,000** — what the RFU then paid **Leicester** to release **Steve Borthwick.**

**£1.8 million to swap one coach for another.**

And in the **same season**, in the same country, in the same sport:

**Worcester Warriors: administration.**
**Wasps: administration.**
**London Irish: suspended, then gone.**

**Over two hundred professional rugby players and staff made redundant overnight.**

That is Generation 7, and you do not need me to editorialise. **The bill from 1895 arrived, and it arrived all at once.**

---

### Saracens, and the lie again

It starts, appropriately, with **cheating.**

**November 2019.** Saracens — the dominant force in English and European rugby, back-to-back champions of Europe, the club that supplied the spine of the England team — were docked **35 points** and fined **over £5 million** for breaching the salary cap.

In **January 2020** they were **relegated.**

**Lord Dyson's report** laid it out: Saracens had overspent the cap by **£1.1 million in 2016–17**, **£98,000 in 2017–18**, and **£906,000 in 2018–19**. And the mechanism was beautiful in its familiarity — **£923,947 of property investments** made jointly between the owner, **Nigel Wray**, and three unnamed Saracens players.

Not wages. **Co-investments.** Money that went to the players without ever technically being a salary.

*(You have seen this exact trick before. **Boot money.** Cash in the socks. Jobs at the sponsor's firm. A thousand pounds for writing a newsletter article. The **same dodge, a hundred years later** — money reaching a player through a channel that isn't officially money. English rugby has been doing this since **1888**, when the RFU declined to punish Andrew Stoddart. The only thing that changed was the size of the sums.)*

Saracens also argued, in their defence, that **the salary cap was unenforceable under competition law.**

The claim was rejected. But hold onto it — because a **restraint-of-trade** argument is exactly what was going to kill amateurism in the 1990s anyway.

---

### And then the clubs started dying

**August 2022.** HMRC serves **Worcester Warriors** with a winding-up order for unpaid tax.

The club insists a takeover is imminent. It isn't. On **15 September**, the RFU gives them until midday the following day.

They miss the deadline. **Worcester — and their women's team — are suspended.** Within hours they enter administration. On **6 October** they are relegated, and **their results are expunged from the record**, as though they had never played.

**Weeks later: Wasps.**

**Wasps.** Four-time Premiership champions. **Two-time European champions.** One of the great clubs of the professional era, with Dallaglio and Lawrence and Wilkinson's rivals and a history running back to **1867.**

**Administration.** Gone.

**June 2023: London Irish.** Founded 1898. Their players received **April's pay a week late** — and then **only half of May's salary.** The RFU set a deadline. It was extended by a week, largely so the players might actually get the money they were owed. The takeover never completed.

**Suspended. Gone.**

**Three historic clubs. Eight months.**

An MP accused the RFU's chief executive, **Bill Sweeney**, of being **"asleep on the job."**

---

### The numbers underneath

And when they opened the books, the scale of it was obscene.

**Premiership clubs' combined debts: over £500 million.**

**Over £36 million owed to HMRC in unpaid tax.** Of thirteen professional clubs, **only two** had a positive balance with the taxman.

And here is the number that explains everything:

**The Premiership's television deal is worth approximately £40 million per year — across all clubs combined.**

**That is less than a single mid-table Premier League football club receives from broadcast in the same period.**

One football club. Vs. **the entire top division of English rugby.**

Thirteen clubs became **ten.** And the fundamental economics remain, in the sober words of the analysis, **unanswered**: how many clubs can compete at the top level **without relying on wealthy benefactors prepared to absorb losses indefinitely?**

**Nobody knows.** And nobody has known since 1995.

---

### Say it plainly

Now put the whole hundred and thirty years in a line, because this is the moment it all lands.

**1893:** The RFU refuses, 282–136, to pay a miner four shillings for a lost shift — because money would **corrupt** the game.

**1895:** Twenty-two clubs walk out. The RFU bans them and their players for life, for **eighty-eight years.**

**1907–1987:** The RFU refuses to allow **league competitions** in England, because a league might tempt clubs to **pay people.**

**1985:** The RFU votes against a **World Cup**, because players might **talk to each other about earnings.**

**1995:** The world goes open, and the RFU — given one year to take control of the money — **goes into committee**, and hands its players to private owners forever.

**2022:** **Three historic clubs collapse**, **two hundred people lose their jobs overnight**, the Premiership carries **half a billion pounds of debt** — and the governing body finds **£1.8 million** to swap one head coach for another.

They spent **a century** protecting rugby from commerce.

And then, when commerce arrived, **they had no plan at all** — and it ate them.

**Money didn't destroy English rugby. Refusing to think about money destroyed English rugby.** The 282 didn't lose because they were greedy. They lost because they were **incurious**, and because a moral panic is not a strategy, and because when you spend a hundred years pretending a problem doesn't exist, you don't get a hundred years to prepare for it — you get **none.**

---

### The one part that works

And now the ending, which is the most quietly devastating thing in this whole English story.

**In 2025, the Red Roses won the Rugby World Cup.**

England's women are, and have been for years, the **best team in the world** — dominant, ruthless, filling **Twickenham** with crowds that would have been unimaginable a decade ago, and playing rugby that is, honestly, more coherent than anything the men have produced since 2003.

**Why?**

Because in **2019 the RFU handed out the first full-time professional contracts in women's rugby — and the RFU holds them.**

**The Red Roses are centrally contracted.**

The union owns the players. The union manages the load. The union plans the four-year cycle. The union controls the pipeline, the medical care, the conditioning, the succession.

**Exactly like New Zealand. Exactly like Ireland. Exactly like South Africa.**

**Exactly what the RFU refused to do for the men in 1995.**

The one arm of English rugby where the governing body actually **owns its own athletes** is the one arm that is **world champion**, financially stable, and growing.

The other arm is renting its players back from private businessmen for **£220 million** and watching its clubs go bankrupt.

**They ran the experiment. In the same building. With the same union. And the answer is unambiguous.**

---

### Where it stands, July 2026

The Premiership is **ten clubs**, financially fragile, still hunting a sustainable model. **Northampton Saints** are the reigning champions.

Grassroots participation has actually **recovered** post-COVID, and the women's game keeps growing.

The men's national team is in transition, rebuilding an identity and a squad, with **Australia 2027** as the target — and the honest position is that English rugby is once again **hoping the next coach is a good one**, because there is still no system underneath to catch them if he isn't.

Twickenham — **Billy Williams's Cabbage Patch**, bought in 1907 by a governing body that had just discovered **there was money to be made from the amateur game** — remains the richest rugby stadium on earth, subsidising a professional game that cannot pay for itself.

---

### The whole argument, closed

You started this conversation asking me why the Uruguayan public don't seem to like rugby, and I gave you a lazy answer about class.

And then we went to Tucumán, where the sugar mills made it working-class. And to Guria, where a priest with a shotgun made it sacred. And to Bucharest, where a communist party made it the people's game **using the very same amateur ethos** that kept the miners of Batley out. And to Zwide, where a boy who watched the 2007 final in a tavern ended up lifting the trophy twice.

And now here, at the source, to a **hotel in London in 1893**, and a vote of **282 to 136.**

**Rugby has no class. Institutions do.**

The men who wrote that rule believed they were defending something beautiful. And they exported it, in the same shipment as the laws of the game, to every continent on earth — where for a hundred years it decided **who was allowed on the grass.**

And in the end it came home, and it bankrupted their own clubs, and it took eighty-eight years of a man's life if he ever took money to feed his children, and the one thing it never, ever did —

— was **pay a miner from Huddersfield four shillings for his lost shift.**

---

That's England. That's the source code.
