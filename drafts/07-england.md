# England

Every chapter in this book eventually comes back to England, because every rugby culture on earth is an answer to something England did — or something England refused to do. This is the chapter where the thing itself happened: where schoolboys wrote the first rules of any football code, where the word "amateur" was sharpened into a weapon, and where, one August night in 1895, the game tore in half. From that night forward there were two rugbies in England, two unions, two truths — and for a hundred years each told the story as if the other were the villain.

This chapter refuses that shortcut. The Southern union's men believed they were saving a game; the Northern union's men believed they were saving their players. Both were right about something. Both did damage. The history is told here the way it was lived — from inside each room — and it runs in eight generations, from the boys who wrote the first rules to the bill that came due in the 2020s.

## Generation 0: The Boys' Game, and the Story They Told About It (1820s–1870)

**10 October 1876.** *The Meteor*, the magazine of Rugby School, prints a letter from Matthew Holbeche Bloxam, an old boy of the school turned local antiquarian. Bloxam has a theory about how the school's peculiar handling game began. The change from a kicking game to a running game, he writes, was the doing of "a town boy or foundationer of the name of Ellis, Webb Ellis."

William Webb Ellis could not confirm or deny it. He had died four years earlier, in January 1872, in Menton on the French Riviera — a retired Anglican clergyman, unmarried, who left most of his money to charity, and who in his entire recorded adult life is not known to have said one word about football. He had not been asked. Bloxam, for his part, had left the school before 1823, the year he would later pin the story to; he was not there either. He was passing on something he had heard from someone he never named — a dead man's deed, reported second-hand, half a century late.

That letter is the acorn of the most famous origin story in world sport: the boy who, "with a fine disregard for the rules," picked up the ball and ran. The World Cup trophy carries the man's name. And the story is, by every serious historical test, not true — or at least, wholly unprovable. Understanding *why* it was built, and *when*, tells you more about English rugby than the legend ever could. But the manufacture comes at the end of this generation's story. First, the game itself — the real one, which needs no legend, because the documented history is better.

### The game before the schools

Englishmen had been running after balls for seven centuries before anyone thought to lie about it. The earliest reference is from William FitzStephen, around 1174, describing the youth of London streaming out to the fields at Shrovetide for their ball games. What followed was the long, rowdy history of folk football — parish against parish, hundreds a side, played through streets and rivers — and an equally long history of authority trying to kill it: London banned it as early as 1314, and the bans kept coming for five hundred years. The decisive blow was bureaucratic, not moral: the Highway Act of 1835 outlawed football on public roads, and enclosure and the factory towns did the rest. The commons' game was being strangled in the very decades when a handful of fee-paying schools were turning their own walled versions of it into something codified.

That timing is worth sitting with, because it is the first appearance of this chapter's pattern. The game of the crowd did not die of natural causes while a superior schoolboy invention replaced it. The crowd's game was legislated off the streets; the boys' game survived because it was played behind walls the law had no interest in. Rugby football's real origin is not a moment of individual genius — it is a question of who owned ground to play on.

### The boys' republic

Inside Rugby School, football belonged to the boys, not the masters. Thomas Arnold, the celebrated reforming headmaster from 1828 to 1841, is often cast as the father of games-playing character education; the record says otherwise. Arnold was largely indifferent to football — he was not, as the EBSCO research survey on his reforms puts it, an advocate of games as tools for building character. That link was welded on later, by a novel we will come to. The masters ran the classroom; the sprawling, dangerous, self-governed matches were run entirely by the Sixth Form. Whatever rugby was becoming in the 1830s, it was becoming it under boy law.

And what it was becoming was a running game — but slowly, and by consensus, not by one rebel's dash. When the Old Rugbeians later went looking for eyewitnesses to the Webb Ellis moment, the witnesses they actually found told them the opposite of the legend. Thomas Harris, who left the school in 1828, and his brother John, who left in 1832, remembered that picking up the ball and running was *strictly forbidden* through the 1820s and well into the 1830s. Thomas Hughes — the school's own novelist — pointed instead to a boy named Jem Mackie, a famous and much-admired "running in" player of the late 1830s, whose practice was tolerated, then popular, then finally made legal around 1841. That is how the change actually happened: the way rules always change in a boys' republic — somebody does the thrilling illegal thing well enough, for long enough, that the law gives in.

### 28 August 1845: the boys write it down

Then came the act that genuinely did change world sport, and it has three named authors, a date, and a document. On **28 August 1845**, three Sixth Form boys — William Delafield Arnold, aged seventeen, son of the late headmaster; W. W. Shirley, sixteen; and Frederick Hutchins — presented thirty-seven written rules of football, approved by the school's sixth-form levée. They are the first written rules of any code of football known to exist. Soccer's, Australia's, America's, both rugbies' — every football rulebook on earth descends from the fact that somebody wrote one first, and the somebody was three teenagers.

The rules they wrote are recognisably the ancestor of the modern game, right down to its most argued-over sentence: *"No player being off his side shall hack, charge, run in, touch the ball in goal, or interrupt a catch."* Offside — the idea that you may not lurk ahead of the ball, that the game must be earned moving forward together — is there in rule form in 1845. So is hacking, the deliberate kicking of an opponent's shins, fully legal and destined to cause the first great split eighteen years later. Note the irony in the byline: the son of the headmaster who didn't care about football co-wrote the document that made football a thing that could travel.

### The ball, and what it cost

It could travel because it had a ball, and the ball had makers. By the 1850s two rival craftsmen supplied the school: William Gilbert and Richard Lindon. Their balls were pigs' bladders cased in leather, shaped like plums rather than true ovals — the shape followed the bladder — and they were inflated by human breath, blown up through the stem of a clay pipe. Green, fresh bladders were sometimes diseased. Rebecca Lindon, Richard's wife, blew them up for the family workshop, and in 1843 she died of a lung disease attributed to exactly that work. Her widower went on to develop the India-rubber bladder after seeing rubber at the Great Exhibition of 1851, and invented a brass hand-pump so that no one had to put a bladder to their lips again — innovations that, incidentally, first made the ball's size and shape something a rulebook could specify.

The Gilbert name is still on the ball in every professional match played in the 2020s. Rebecca Lindon has no plaque. The boys' game had a working-class cost from the very beginning; it just never appeared on the score-sheet.

### The book that carried it

In 1857 Thomas Hughes — Rugby pupil, 1834 to 1842 — published *Tom Brown's School Days*, and the walled game escaped the walls. The novel was a phenomenon across the English-speaking world, and its long, loving set-piece account of a School House match did two things at once. It advertised the game itself, vividly enough that fifteen years later a New York newspaper reprinted the match description to explain the new sport of college football to Americans. And it invented, more or less single-handedly, the mythology of "muscular Christianity" at Rugby — retro-fitting onto Arnold, who had cared little for games, the ideology that games build Christian men. The book gave the game its passport and its sermon in the same binding.

The old boys did the carrying. Through the 1840s, '50s and '60s, men who had learned the game at school took it to the universities, the hospitals, the garrisons and the new industrial cities, and clubs began to crystallise — the dates are a genealogist's minefield, but the sequence runs roughly: Guy's Hospital, whose claimed 1843 founding would make it the world's oldest, though the claim rests on circumstantial evidence and is openly contested; Liverpool in 1857; Blackheath in 1858, the oldest *open* club — neither school nor hospital, anyone welcome — in continuous existence; Manchester around 1860; Richmond in 1861. Mark the geography: London hospitals and suburbs, yes, but Liverpool and Manchester almost as early. The northern game is not a later infection of a southern original. The North was there nearly from the start, and this book will not let you forget it.

### 1863: the first walkout

Rugby's history of splitting did not begin in 1895. It began at the Freemasons' Tavern on Great Queen Street, London, where on **26 October 1863** representatives of a dozen-odd clubs and schools met to found the Football Association and write one common code for all the incompatible school games. Blackheath was a founder member. The negotiation snagged, immovably, on two practices from the Rugby School code: running with the ball, and hacking.

Blackheath's Francis Maule Campbell defended both, and his defence of hacking at the FA's fifth meeting is one of the great preserved speeches of Victorian sport: abolish it, he warned, and you "do away with all the courage and pluck from the game, and I will be bound over to bring over a lot of Frenchmen who would beat you with a week's practice." Read it twice. The argument for kicking other men's shins is an argument about *manliness* — pain endured as moral credential — delivered with a garnish of chauvinism. It is the first recorded draft of an argument England's rugby establishment would keep making, in politer forms, for the next hundred and thirty years: the game exists to build a certain kind of man, and changing the rules to make it safer, fairer or more popular would dissolve the kind of man it builds.

At the sixth meeting, on **8 December 1863**, the FA's draft laws went against the handling clubs, and Campbell withdrew Blackheath, declaring that the new rules "would destroy the game and all interest in it." Other handling clubs stayed out with them. Be precise about what this was: not yet rugby versus soccer — there was no body called rugby, and would not be until 1871 — but the dribbling code organising itself while the handling clubs chose independence over compromise. The first split in football history, and the rugby men were the ones who walked. It would not be the last time; the next walkout would be aimed *at* them.

### The man who never said a word

Now come back to Bloxam's letter, because you can finally see it in context — and see what was done with it.

The real William Webb Ellis was not even the right class for the legend that would be hung on him. He entered Rugby in 1816 as a *foundationer* — a local boy educated on the foundation's charity, not a fee-paying gentleman boarder — and the one thing a contemporary actually recorded about his sport is quietly devastating to the myth: he was "an admirable cricketer, but… generally regarded as inclined to take unfair advantages at Football." A scholarship day-boy remembered by his peers as a cheat. He went up to Oxford, played in the first Oxford–Cambridge cricket match at Lord's in 1827, took holy orders, preached in Mayfair and the Strand, retired to Essex and then to the Riviera, and died at Menton in January 1872 having never, as far as any record shows, mentioned football again. His grave was lost until 1958.

Bloxam's 1876 letter drew little notice, and a second, more detailed letter — the one that fixed the date at 1823 and supplied the cinematic image of the boy who "on catching the ball, instead of retiring backwards, rushed forwards with the ball in his hands towards the opposite goal" — followed a few years later. (Even the letter's date is disputed between sources, which tells you something about the evidentiary grade of this whole affair.) For nearly two decades the story sat inert. The standard football histories of 1885–1892 — Vincent, Shearman, the Reverend Frank Marshall's — do not mention Webb Ellis at all. Nobody's origin story needed him yet.

Then, in **July 1895**, the Old Rugbeian Society appointed a sub-committee to establish, formally, the origin of rugby football. Hold that date against the calendar of the generation after next: the game's northern clubs were four weeks from walking out of the Rugby Football Union forever. The gentlemen's game was about to lose half of itself — the bigger, stronger, more popular half — and the men of the old school went looking for a founding.

What their inquiry actually found, we have already seen: the Harris brothers saying running was strictly forbidden in the relevant decades; Hughes pointing to Jem Mackie and gradual legalisation around 1841; and, in the committee's own admission, no first-hand evidence of the Webb Ellis incident whatsoever. What the inquiry *concluded*, in its 1897 report, was that Webb Ellis's run was "in all probability" the origin of the running game. In 1900 they bolted the bronze tablet to the school wall — "with a fine disregard for the rules of football as played in his time… A.D. 1823" — and the verdict became scripture.

The historians have been unpicking it ever since, and this book sides with them by name. Eric Dunning and Kenneth Sheard, whose *Barbarians, Gentlemen and Players* was the first systematic demolition, read the inquiry as exactly what its timing suggests: an attempt by the old school "to assert their school's position and authority over a sport that they were losing control of" — a Great Man story imposed on what was really the slow work of many interdependent people. William J. Baker, in a 1981 study, called the tale "a period piece, reflecting more of the era which gave it birth than of the event to which it referred," and noted its almost exact American twin: baseball's Abner Doubleday myth, another gentleman-founder invented by a committee for a game with awkwardly common roots. And Tony Collins, the pre-eminent historian of the split to come, put the human point simplest: no one would have been more surprised by the claim that he invented rugby than William Webb Ellis himself.

Understand the myth generously, though, from inside the room that made it — this chapter will ask that of you often. The Old Rugbeians of 1895 were watching the game their school had given the world fill up with mill hands and miners, with paying crowds and, whisper it, wages. A story in which rugby was *born at Rugby*, in a single flash of well-bred audacity, was a title deed. It said: whatever happens in the North, the game is ours — it has our name on it. That is why the legend was certified in the exact season the game split, and why it endured: not because anyone checked it, but because one of the two rugbies England was about to produce needed it to be true.

### What Generation 0 leaves behind

By 1870, then, the inventory stands like this. A running game with written laws, drafted by schoolboys and spread by a bestselling novel. A network of clubs from London to Liverpool with no common government. A demonstrated taste for walking out of negotiations rather than softening the game — and, in Campbell's Frenchmen speech, a fully formed ideology of pain-as-character waiting to be deployed in politer causes. A working-class woman dead of making the ball, and a working-class game dead by Act of Parliament, beneath a sport that would soon declare itself the province of gentlemen. And a vacancy for an origin story, which would be filled the moment the gentlemen felt their grip loosen.

What it does not yet have is a union. In January 1871, at a restaurant in Pall Mall, twenty-one clubs will fix that — and with a governing body will come governed people, and the question that governs this whole chapter: *which* people? The game is about to roar north into the mill towns of Yorkshire and Lancashire, where it will find its biggest crowds, its best players, and the men the union was never designed for. One game, two Englands. That collision is Generation 1.

## Generation 1: One Game, Two Englands (1871–1892)

**26 January 1871.** A Thursday evening in London, at the Pall Mall Restaurant on Haymarket. Thirty-two men representing twenty-one football clubs have answered a letter that Edwin Ash, secretary of the Richmond club, sent to the newspapers in December: the clubs that play the handling game need common laws, and a society to keep them. In the chair is Edward Carleton Holmes, Richmond's captain. By the end of the evening the Rugby Football Union exists — Algernon Rutter of Richmond its first president, Ash its secretary, membership five shillings a club.

Two things about that room deserve a long look before anyone leaves it.

Read the roll of founding clubs: Blackheath, Richmond, Guy's Hospital, King's College Hospital, Harlequins, Civil Service, Wellington College, St Paul's School, Law, Wimbledon Hornets, the Gipsies, the Flamingoes… Hospitals, schools, the law, the Civil Service, the comfortable suburbs. Every club in the room is southern; most are institutions of the professional classes. Liverpool and Manchester, playing the same game since the 1850s — earlier than most of the men in the room — are not there. The union that will govern all of English rugby is being born as a London club society, and nothing in its constitution will ever quite outgrow that evening.

And one club famously isn't there at all. Wasps' own history cheerfully preserves two versions of the legend — that their man went to the wrong venue on the wrong day, or that he went to a pub of the right name, drank while he waited, and was in no condition to relocate once he realised his error. It is club lore, not documentation, and it should be enjoyed exactly as such: English rugby's founding meeting comes with a punchline about a missing delegate, which is the most English thing in this entire chapter.

### Three lawyers and a broken leg

The new union's first act was to write the laws, and the job went to three men who were both old boys of Rugby School and practising lawyers — Rutter, Holmes, and Leonard James Maton. The bulk of the drafting fell to Maton, for the excellent reason that he had broken his leg and had nothing better to do than sit in chambers and codify a game; the laws were approved in June 1871. The lawyers kept the running game, the offside principle, the scrummaging — and killed hacking. Campbell's courage-and-pluck argument, the hill the handling men had chosen to die on in 1863, was quietly buried by their own union eight years later; Blackheath itself had stopped hacking in 1865, Richmond in 1866. There is even a fine irony in the tail of the story: Rugby School itself, by several accounts, stayed aloof from the union for years rather than give up hacking in its own games. The school that gave the game its name held out longest against the game's laws — a neat early lesson that in England, the institution and the game are never quite the same thing.

### 27 March 1871: Raeburn Place

The union was three weeks old when the reason for its urgency arrived from Edinburgh. In December 1870 the captains of five Scottish clubs had published a challenge in *Bell's Life* and *The Scotsman*: they would meet "any team selected from the whole of England" at the handling code, twenty a side. On **27 March 1871**, at Raeburn Place in Edinburgh, the first rugby international — the first international in any football code — was played in front of some four thousand spectators who paid a shilling each, putting over £200 through the gate. Hold onto that number: from its very first afternoon, international rugby was a business taking money at a turnstile. The amateur game never existed as an economy; it existed as a rule about who was allowed to touch the money.

Scotland won by a goal and a try to a try — and even that scoreline needs translating, because in 1871 a try was worth nothing in itself; it merely bought the *try at goal*, and matches were decided on goals alone. Angus Buchanan crossed for the first international try in history, England protested furiously that the scrum had been shoved over the line illegally, and the Scottish umpire, Dr H. H. Almond, awarded it anyway. R. H. Birkett scored England's try; Frederick Stokes of Blackheath, twenty years old, captained England and would be president of the RFU by twenty-three. An away defeat, a disputed score, and a governing-class career launched — the international game arrived fully formed.

### The union that thought it owned the game

What kind of institution had been born at the Pall Mall Restaurant showed itself most clearly not in what the RFU played, but in how it behaved when crossed. On **1 March 1884**, at Blackheath, England beat Scotland with a try scored by Richard Kindersley after a Scottish forward, C. W. Berry, knocked the ball backwards. The Scots held that any knock was illegal; England held that only a knock *forward* was; the Irish umpire George Scriven pronounced the immortal ruling that "the ball was knocked back by a Scotchman" and let the try stand. Scotland refused to accept the result. The two unions did not play at all in 1885, and Scotland finally conceded the match in February 1886 "only in the interests of rugby football."

Out of that farce the other three home unions drew the obvious conclusion: the laws of an international game cannot belong to one of its players. In 1886 Scotland, Ireland and Wales founded the International Rugby Football Board as a final arbiter — and England refused to join it. The RFU's position, recorded in the histories with a straight face, was that *as the oldest union it was the sole guarantor of the laws of the game*, and that in any case it had more clubs and deserved weighted representation. The stand-off cost England its fixtures — no internationals in 1888 or 1889 — and ended in 1890 with a settlement that tells you everything: England joined the Board it had boycotted, holding six of the twelve votes. Half the world's government, as the price of showing up.

File the posture away, because this book will need it again and again: when the RFU believed itself right, it would rather not play than be governed, and it regarded the game not as a thing it belonged to but as a thing that belonged to it. In 1890 that posture won. It would not always.

### Meanwhile, the North fills up

Now turn away from London, because through these same two decades the game's centre of gravity was moving somewhere the founders had never planned for it to go.

The old boys' network had carried rugby to the merchants and mill-owners of Yorkshire and Lancashire, and from them it dropped, fast, down through the towns — to the weavers, the miners, the foundry men. The vehicle of the drop was a piece of silverware. In 1877 Yorkshire launched a county knockout cup — the first in British rugby — a sterling-silver trophy out of a Sheffield workshop that the towns, with perfect Yorkshire irony, christened **"t'owd tin pot"**: the old tin pot. Halifax beat York in the first final, on a Leeds ground, and something began that the South had no equivalent of: cup rugby as civic warfare. Town against town, works against works, the whole borough at the gate. The cup ties swelled into mass events with paying crowds that dwarfed anything a London club friendly could draw, and the gate money built grandstands, and the grandstands demanded winning teams, and winning teams demanded the best players a town could find — whatever those players did on weekdays.

When the RFU created a County Championship in 1889, the North simply collected it. Yorkshire took the first title without losing a match — the season's aggregate, eighteen goals and seventeen tries scored against a solitary goal and three tries conceded, reads like a misprint — then took the next, and kept taking them. By one count, when England beat Wales 17–0 in 1892, eleven of the fifteen England shirts were filled from northern clubs. Two decades after twenty-one southern clubs founded the union over dinner, the union's playing strength lived in towns none of those clubs had heard of. One game; two Englands. The first was a membership; the second was a public.

### The nine-shilling international

The collision between them had a face, and this book will follow it across two generations. Dicky Lockwood — Richard Evison Lockwood, born 1867 — was a woollen printer in a Dewsbury mill earning nine shillings a week, and he was also, from 1887, one of the finest three-quarters in England. Fourteen caps; England's captain, eventually, in 1894 — a mill hand leading the gentlemen's national side. And around him, always, the buzz of suspicion that followed every working northerner in a game whose expenses rules were written by men with private incomes: Lockwood was twice investigated for professionalism, and twice acquitted.

One small episode with him is worth more than any essay on Victorian amateurism. Unavailable for an England match, Lockwood asked the RFU's permission to turn out for Heckmondwike, his club, that day instead — and was refused, under rules that public-school men, drifting casually between old boys' sides and invitation fifteens, never seemed to feel the weight of. The union's discipline, he discovered, had a grain: it ran one way for men like him. Remember Lockwood. In 1895 he will choose a side, and in 1897 he will be bankrupt, and between those two facts runs most of the moral of this chapter.

### The word

To understand what happened next, you have to stand where the RFU's committee men stood in the mid-1880s and look at what was happening to the *other* football. Association football had gone through exactly this: northern industrial clubs, huge crowds, and players quietly paid. The FA had fought it, lost, and on **20 July 1885** — under threat of its northern clubs seceding to a rival association — legalised professionalism. To the men of the RFU, watching from their committee rooms, this was not an accommodation; it was a surrender, and the beginning of everything they feared: the club as a business, the player as an employee, the game as a product, the gentleman crowded out of his own recreation by men who were simply better because they were paid to be. Be fair to them from inside their room: they had built their whole idea of sport on the game as character — Campbell's pluck, Hughes's muscular Christianity — and they watched soccer become, as they saw it, an industry in a single decade. They did not invent their terror; they read it in the newspapers.

So in 1886 the RFU wrote the word into law. Its new regulations banned any payment to a player beyond legitimate expenses — and named, specifically, the payment that mattered most in the North: **broken time**, the few shillings that compensated a working man for wages lost to a Saturday shift missed for rugby. Read plainly, the rule said: this game is free to play for men whose time costs them nothing. The stopped mill-clock of a Dewsbury printer was declared a corruption; the leisured Saturday of a London solicitor was declared purity. Rugby did not have a class; the regulation gave it one.

The union found its enforcer in the Reverend Frank Marshall, headmaster of Almondbury Grammar School outside Huddersfield — a clergyman-schoolmaster in the North itself, hunting professionalism through Yorkshire rugby with genuine, unembarrassed conviction. Marshall believed the game was, and should remain, a middle-class pastime; he pursued clubs and players with such zeal that in 1893 he would help suspend Huddersfield — his own town's club. He was not a hypocrite; he was something more instructive, a true believer, and the North's lasting verdict on him has outlived the century: when a blue plaque was proposed for him in Huddersfield in 2021, the objections came in loud enough that the council refused it. A hundred and twenty-six years after the split, the town still remembered.

And then, in 1888, the amateur union met the market it pretended did not exist — and blinked. A privately organised British team toured Australia and New Zealand that year, put together not by any union but by three cricket promoters, Alfred Shaw, Arthur Shrewsbury and James Lillywhite, with Robert Seddon as captain: thirty-five rugby matches on the far side of the world, a commercial speculation from first to last. The World Rugby Museum's own account is blunt: there is "no doubt that the majority of British players were paid to tour" — some collected upwards of £200 — and on their return the players handed the RFU statutory declarations swearing they had taken nothing. The union accepted the paperwork.

One man told the truth. Jack Clowes, of Halifax, admitted he had accepted £15 to buy kit, and offered to repay it. The RFU declared him a professional — the ruling reached him aboard the *Kaikoura*, already at sea — and he sailed round the world with the touring party forbidden to play a single game. The liars were capped; the honest man watched from the touchline for eight months. In one small, perfectly documented case, the amateur regulations revealed what they were actually for: not the elimination of money — the money was everywhere, starting with that first £200 gate at Raeburn Place — but the power to decide *which men* the money would be held against.

### What Generation 1 leaves behind

By 1892 the pieces are all on the table. A union built by and for the London professional classes, holding half the votes on the international board it once boycotted, convinced it owns the game outright. A northern public that has made rugby the passion of the industrial towns — the cup, the county, the gate money, the grandstands — and now supplies most of England's best players, Lockwood the mill hand foremost among them. A regulation, written in 1886, that defines the northern player's lost wages as corruption and the southern player's leisure as virtue. And an 1888 tour that proved the union would tolerate any amount of professionalism, so long as it came with a gentleman's signature on a false declaration.

All that remained was for the North to ask, openly and formally, for the one thing the rule forbade: six shillings a day for broken time. They would ask in September 1893, in a London hotel half a mile from where the union was founded — and the answer, and the way the answer was engineered, would break the game in two. That is Generation 2.

## Generation 2: The Great Schism (1893–1895)

**20 September 1893.** Two special trains have come down from the North overnight, and the men stepping out of them onto the London platforms are club treasurers, county committee-men, mill-owners and tradesmen — the government of northern rugby, travelling together like a delegation to a foreign capital, which in every way that matters is what they are. Their destination is the Westminster Palace Hotel, where the Rugby Football Union is holding a general meeting, and their business is a single sentence. James Miller, president of the Yorkshire union, will rise and move:

> *"That players be allowed compensation for bona fide loss of time."*

Not wages. Not professionalism. Compensation — the six shillings or so that a Saturday shift would have paid — for men whose absence from the mill or the pit on a match day cost them money that the men proposing the rule would never miss, because their time had never been sold by the hour to anyone. Miller is seconded by Mark Newsome of Dewsbury, himself an RFU committee-man. This is not an insurrection; it is a motion, moved through the proper channels, by men who have spent twenty years building the biggest rugby public in the world and want their players to stop losing money for the privilege of providing it.

The union's answer is moved by its honorary secretary of twelve years, G. Rowland Hill:

> *"That this meeting, believing that the above principle is contrary to the true interest of the game and its spirit, declines to sanction the same."*

The debate is long; the outcome is not in doubt, and both sides know it — the London leadership has spent the weeks beforehand organising its vote, and however the tally is reckoned (282 to 136 in most accounts, 146 in some), the northern men are beaten before their trains reach Euston. The word "spirit" does the work. Broken time is not defeated as impractical, or costly, or open to abuse — grounds on which the North might have negotiated. It is defeated as *contrary to the spirit of the game*: a moral verdict, from which there is no appeal, delivered on men who are not in the room in any numbers because the meeting is held, as the union's bylaws by then required, in London, on a working day.

### The southern room, from inside

Stand in that room for a moment, though, and take its occupants as seriously as they took themselves — because the maddening thing about the Westminster Palace Hotel is that almost no one in it was lying.

Rowland Hill's stated conviction — that broken time, once sanctioned, "would lead to professionalism" — was sincerely held and, as prophecy, entirely correct. The men of the RFU had watched the Football Association concede exactly this ground in 1885, and from where they sat the consequences were already visible: the association game transformed inside a decade into an industry of paid players, transfer fees and limited companies, its clubs owned rather than belonged to. They believed — genuinely, not as cover — that a game played for money must eventually be played *about* money, and that everything they loved in rugby (the amateur's freedom, the club as a society of equals, sport as schooling for character) would be devoured. Even the presidency complicates the cartoon: William Cail, the president who stood firm against the broken-time clubs, was no Home Counties squire but a Newcastle industrialist — a northerner who chose the amateur creed over the northern cause, proof that the line ran through convictions before it ran through counties.

But the creed had a load-bearing assumption, and it is best heard from the union's own most candid man. Arthur Budd, a former RFU president, put it in print without embarrassment:

> *"It must be candidly stated that the troubles of the union commenced with the advent of the working man. If he cannot afford the leisure to play the game he must do without it."*

There is the whole architecture in two sentences. The game is defined by leisure; leisure is defined by income; therefore the game belongs, by definition, to men of means — and a rule against compensating lost wages is not an unfortunate barrier to the working man but a working *definition* of him as the problem. Tony Collins, the split's great historian, would put the scholarly version plainly a century later: the RFU "didn't like what they saw as the property of the private schools being taken over by ordinary people from the north." And Collins adds the sharpest twist: the broken-time fight was never really the war itself, only "a battle for position before the inevitable showdown over payment for play." Both sides understood that. That is why neither would give an inch over six shillings.

### The screw turns

What followed the vote was not stalemate but escalation. The union's amateur regulations already reversed the ordinary logic of justice — an accused club was required to prove its innocence — and the enforcement machinery ran hottest in Yorkshire itself, where the Reverend Frank Marshall sat on the committees like, in a contemporary's words, "the man with bell, book and candle facing the evil spirit of professionalism." In 1893 the machinery produced its defining case: Huddersfield — Marshall's own town, the club whose ground lay a walk from his school — was suspended over the signing of two Cumberland players, George Boak and Jock Forsyth, with Marshall as chief witness for the prosecution. Nothing tells you more about the war's character: it was not a war of South against North so much as a war *inside* the North, between the men who ran its rugby and an ideology policed by true believers among them.

By the summer of 1895 the leading Yorkshire and Lancashire clubs had drawn the only conclusion left. They were the game's paying public and most of its playing strength; they were also, permanently, defendants — suspendable at any moment, under rules they could not change, by meetings they could not attend, in a union that had just written into its bylaws that its headquarters and all its general meetings belonged to London. Coexistence was not on offer. The question was no longer whether to leave, but how, and together.

### 29 August 1895. The George Hotel, Huddersfield.

At half past six on a Thursday evening, in the George Hotel on St George's Square in Huddersfield — chosen, with northern practicality, as roughly equidistant between Widnes and Hull — representatives of twenty-one clubs sat down: Batley, Bradford, Brighouse Rangers, Broughton Rangers, Dewsbury, Halifax, Huddersfield, Hull, Hunslet, Leeds, Leigh, Liversedge, Manningham, Oldham, Rochdale Hornets, St Helens, Tyldesley, Wakefield Trinity, Warrington, Widnes, Wigan. Read the list aloud sometime; it is the industrial North singing itself. Before the evening was out a twenty-second club, Stockport, had been admitted by wire to the hotel — the accounts vary charmingly on whether telegram or telephone — and the meeting had passed, unanimously, the sentence that created a second rugby:

> *"That the clubs here represented decide to form a Northern Rugby Football Union, and pledge themselves to push forward, without delay, its establishment on the principle of payment for bona-fide broken-time only."*

Mark the last three words. Broken time *only* — six shillings a day, the very compromise the RFU had refused at the Westminster Palace Hotel two years earlier. The men at the George did not vote for professionalism; they voted for the thing they had asked permission for and been denied, and they built a union around it. (Full professional wages would come later, as Rowland Hill had predicted and as the George Hotel men surely suspected; on the founding night, the principle was the lost Saturday wage and nothing more.) Then twenty of the clubs in the room resigned from the Rugby Football Union, appointed a ten-man subcommittee to write bylaws, agreed to alternate future meetings between Huddersfield and Manchester — and had a fixture list running within nine days. Manningham, of Bradford, would be the new union's first champions the following spring.

And one chair was, so to speak, half-empty. Dewsbury — the club of Mark Newsome, seconder of the original broken-time motion — attended, hesitated, asked for time, and days later withdrew, staying loyal to the RFU. Their own public was appalled; a local journalist reported that there wasn't a supporter in the town who wouldn't say *"Let us have the Northern Union and the sooner the better."* Keep Dewsbury in the picture: the North was not a bloc, any more than the South was. It was a community of clubs making the hardest institutional decision in the sport's history, one committee room at a time, against their own friendships and their own histories.

### The southern answer

The RFU replied in ten days flat. On **19 September 1895**, at the Cannon Street Hotel in London, the union adopted a new code of professionalism laws built on a single principle: everything is professionalism unless proved otherwise. Union clubs were forbidden to play Northern Union clubs. Testimonials were banned; compensation of any kind — time, training, travel — was banned; and any player who took the field *for or against* a Northern Union team was thereby a professional, banned from rugby union for life. Not paid players: *any* players. The wall was built not against money but against contact.

Enforcement reached the level of the surreal almost immediately, and Tony Collins has preserved the case law. A player from Wyke was banned for life for travelling with the Brighouse Rangers team to a match — he never played. Elland lost a man for playing *against* a team that merely contained a Northern Union player. And in January 1898 Goole's rugby union club was ordered not to play a charity match against a touring pantomime troupe — Little Red Riding Hood — because the troupe had earlier played a charity fixture against Batley. A children's pantomime cast, professionalised by contamination. More than a hundred northern players were expelled in the early purges; clubs that stayed loyal to the union, like Beverley, found themselves with almost no one left to play. The doctrine was total because it was not really a doctrine about payment. It was a quarantine — and what was being quarantined was a class.

### What it cost

Now the arithmetic of the schism, and it should be read slowly. The RFU had 481 member clubs at its 1893 peak; a decade later it had roughly 244. Yorkshire and Lancashire, which between them had held some 240 union clubs before the split, were down to about 22 by 1897 — Lancashire alone to thirteen, mostly old-boys' sides. By 1897 Halifax, a town that had won the first Yorkshire Cup final twenty years earlier, contained not a single rugby union club, and union rugby in Leeds was described as practically non-existent. The Northern Union, twenty-two clubs on its founding night, counted fifty-nine by its first annual general meeting in 1896 — and some of the later arrivals are the bitterest part of the story. Salford and Swinton had stayed loyal in 1895; the union's southern clubs starved them of fixtures anyway, and by the summer of 1896 both had crossed over. The RFU drove out even the northerners who chose it.

On the field, England's national side — which had drawn most of its strength from the seceded clubs, Lockwood's caps among them — did not win the Home Nations Championship for fifteen years. Dicky Lockwood himself, England's captain the year before the split, went where his life was: professional with Wakefield Trinity in 1895, bankrupt by 1897. The union lost half its clubs, its best players and a generation of results; the Northern Union gained a game it could call its own and a hundred-year sentence to second-class status in the eyes of the England it had left. Collins's summary judgment stands over all of it: this was not, at bottom, a quarrel about geography or even about money, but about whether "the property of the private schools" could be taken over by "ordinary people from the north" — and in 1895 both sides answered *no*, one with a purge and one with an exit.

### What Generation 2 leaves behind

Two unions, then, where there had been one — and this must be said with Latham-like fairness, because it is true: **each side walked out of 1895 convinced it had saved the game, and each, by its own lights, had.** The RFU had preserved, at ruinous cost, the thing it actually valued — not rugby as such, but *amateur* rugby, the game as a school of character insulated from the market. The Northern Union had preserved the thing *it* actually valued — the game as the property of its towns and the men who played it, on terms a working life could afford. The tragedy is not that either was wrong about what it loved. The tragedy is that the one institution that could have held both loves — the union founded at the Pall Mall Restaurant to govern everyone who played the handling game — had chosen, when the test came, to be the instrument of one England against the other.

From here the book must do something unusual, and the next generation will do it: follow both rugbies at once. Behind the wall the RFU built in September 1895, two complete games will now grow for fifty years — one with Twickenham, the other with Wembley; one with excommunications, the other with a new rulebook and a code spreading to the far side of the world; both with their dead in the same wars. Two games behind one wall: that is Generation 3.

## Generation 3: Two Games Behind One Wall (1895–1945)

**24 April 1897.** Headingley, Leeds, on a spring Saturday, and 13,492 people have paid to watch the final of a cup competition that did not exist a year ago, in a rugby union that did not exist two years ago. Batley against St Helens — Yorkshire against Lancashire — for the Northern Union's new Challenge Cup: a drop goal from Joe Oakland, tries traded, Batley home 10–3, and a trophy carried back over the Pennines to a mill town of thirty-odd thousand people. Nothing about the afternoon is tentative. No one on the terraces is wondering whether they did the right thing in 1895. The seceded game has silverware, crowds, and somewhere to be next season.

For the next fifty years England contains two complete rugbies, and this generation must be read the way it was lived — in parallel. On one side of the wall, a game rebuilding itself around the paying crowd; on the other, a game rebuilding itself around the amateur creed and a fortress in the London suburbs. The wall between them, made of lifetime bans, will be tested by two world wars and — in one extraordinary pair of wartime afternoons — by the only direct experiment ever run between the codes. Hold on for that. It comes at the end, and it is worth the wait.

### The North writes its own rulebook

Free of London, the Northern Union discovered it was free of something deeper: the assumption that the game's laws were sacred inheritance. If the clubs' revenue came from spectators, then the spectator was a stakeholder, and the game could be *redesigned* — deliberately, by vote — to be worth a working family's shilling. The line-out went as early as 1897, with a justification that would have been unsayable at the RFU: **"The rugby public does not pay to see scrimmaging."** Read that sentence against Rowland Hill's "spirit of the game" amendment and you have the whole schism in two quotations — one code answerable to a creed, the other to a crowd. Full professionalism followed in 1898, replacing the broken-time fiction with honest wages (in its early years, famously, on terms designed to keep players in ordinary jobs — the new union had its own anxieties about what money might do).

The great redesign came in June 1906, and history has its little jokes: the meeting was held at the George Hotel, the same Huddersfield rooms where the union was born. By 43 votes to 18 the clubs cut teams from fifteen players to **thirteen**, and replaced the contested scrum after every tackle with the **play-the-ball**: the tackled man gets up and plays on. Two fewer forwards, one less pile-up — space, speed, continuity, all voted through in an afternoon, partly for the spectacle and partly (the Northern Union being run by treasurers) because two fewer wages saved every club money. The first fortnight of the new rules produced a torrent of scoring the old game had never seen. Rugby league — the name would come later, but this is its birthday as a distinct *game* rather than a breakaway administration — was now a different sport, built on a different theory of who football is for.

### The code goes abroad

The theory travelled. In 1907 a Wellington postal clerk named Albert Henry Baskerville — who had never seen a Northern Union match — organised a professional New Zealand touring team in defiance of his country's rugby union, with the great All Black wing George Smith helping to pick the side. New Zealand's press sneered at the mercenaries; the tour sailed anyway, and on **1 January 1908**, at Aberdare in the Welsh valleys, the first rugby league international was played: **Wales 9, New Zealand 8**, before some twenty thousand people, the winning try scored by Dai "Tarw" Jones — a Welsh forward of the union's 1905 triumph over the All Blacks, now a professional in the new code. The tourists took their Test series against the Northern Union's best, and the "All Golds," as they were jeered (they played for gold, after all), went home having made the rebel English game an international one. Baskerville never saw what he had started grow up: he caught pneumonia on the Australian leg and died in May 1908, aged twenty-five. The trophy contested between Great Britain and New Zealand league sides into the twenty-first century — the Baskerville Shield — carries the postal clerk's name, the northern code's own quiet answer to the Webb Ellis Cup: their founding legend has the advantage of being true.

Australia needed no persuading. The New South Wales Rugby League had already formed at a Sydney hotel in August 1907, fielded nine clubs the next winter, and produced the code's first superstar in Dally Messenger, whose name on a placard — *"Messenger Will Play"* — sold tickets by itself. Within thirteen years of the George Hotel, the game invented by seceding Pennine treasurers was the working man's football of two hemispheres. Whatever the RFU had expelled in 1895, it had not expelled a failure.

### Going north

And for the next century the two English codes were connected by a one-way road. The Northern Union's clubs paid, and union's best players — above all Welsh ones, from a country where rugby union was itself the working man's game — kept arriving at their doors. The historian Robert Gate counted them: between 1895 and 1986, **148 Welsh rugby union internationals** "went north." Wales's chapter in this book tells that drain from the valleys' side — the scout at the door, the slate of cash on the kitchen table, the village that stopped saying a man's name. Here it need only be said what the road proved: professional rugby did not have to bribe men away from amateurism. It had only to offer a wage to men whose game had always refused them one.

One arrival stands for all of them. In June 1921 Wigan signed a teenage Cardiff full-back named Jim Sullivan, and over the next quarter-century he scored 4,883 points for the club — a career that simply could not exist in the amateur game, a working life made of rugby. Union's histories of this era are full of what was lost; stand in Wigan and the same years read as what was built.

### Meanwhile, in the South: the cabbage patch

The RFU, meanwhile, was learning the northern lesson without ever admitting it. The union that had held gate money to be the root of the game's corruption spent **£5,572 12s 6d** in August 1907 on ten and a quarter acres of market garden in the London suburbs — the press promptly christened it "Billy Williams' Cabbage Patch," after the committee man who found it — and built a stadium on it. Look at the second name on the purchase: William Cail, the Newcastle industrialist who as president had faced down the broken-time motion in 1893, now the union's treasurer, buying the union a gate of its own. The men who ran the RFU were never fools about money; they objected to money reaching *players*, not to money reaching the institution.

Twickenham opened on 2 October 1909 with Harlequins beating Richmond 14–10 on a waterlogged field in front of a couple of thousand curious Londoners, and staged its first international on **15 January 1910**: England 11, Wales 6, the decisive try flowing from fly-half Adrian Stoop's audacious counter-attack straight off the opening kick — England's first win over Wales since the 1890s, in the first minutes of their new home. The symbolism was total. The union that had lost the North now had a fortress in the South: a permanent, profitable, RFU-owned ground that anchored the game's identity — financial and social — in middle-class London for the century to come. The two rugbies now each had a temple, and the temples told you everything: one reached by tram from the mills, one reached by motor car through the Surrey suburbs.

### The gentlemen's golden age

Say this plainly, because fairness demands it: the amateur game of this generation was not a husk. England in the 1920s played some of the best rugby on earth. With the wartime generation's survivors and the public schools' production line, England took Grand Slams in **1921, 1923, 1924 and 1928** — three of them in four seasons, a density no side had matched — and the era had a genuine innovator at its heart. Wavell Wakefield, thirty-one caps, captain for the 1923 and 1924 Slams, effectively invented modern back-row play: where wing forwards had been static scrummagers, Wakefield organised them into a coordinated pressure system hunting the opposing half-backs, defence turned into a form of attack. The amateur game could still produce tactical revolution; it simply produced it from men with the leisure to think about rugby between professions. Wakefield's later career completes the portrait — Member of Parliament, RFU president, eventually a peer: in the South, rugby eminence and establishment eminence were the same ladder.

And in 1931 the union's creed showed its teeth abroad. France — admitted to the championship in 1910 — was expelled from the Five Nations after years of British complaints about violent club rugby and "veiled professionalism" in the French league. The charge was, in substance, true; French club rugby was semi-professional in all but name. But sit with the picture a moment: the union whose 1888 tourists had sworn false amateur declarations, whose own shamateurism would run another sixty years, expelled an entire nation for the same offence, and the Five Nations played as Four until France was invited back in 1939 — whereupon the war postponed the reunion to 1947. The doctrine of 1895 was now foreign policy.

### The wars

Twice in this generation the wall between the codes was overrun by history, and what happened each time deserves to be told slowly.

The Great War emptied both games into the same trenches. Rugby union's sacrifice was extraordinary and is rightly remembered: **twenty-seven England internationals died**, among them the two men who had lit up Twickenham's first seasons. Ronald Poulton-Palmer — seventeen caps, England's captain, by common consent the most gifted attacking three-quarter of his age — was killed by a sniper's bullet near Ploegsteert Wood on 5 May 1915, working on a trench in the dark. Edgar Mobbs, the Northampton captain who had been told at thirty-two he was too old to enlist, raised his own company instead — some 250 sportsmen, "Mobbs' Own" — and died at Passchendaele in July 1917, a lieutenant-colonel charging a machine-gun post; of his battalion's 264 originals, barely a third survived. (A century on, England and Australia contest the Ella-Mobbs Trophy — his name is still on the game.) The Northern Union's towns sent their professionals to the same war and buried them in the same ground; grief, at least, was played under common rules.

When it ended, rugby union staged something remarkable: the **King's Cup** of 1919, six service teams — New Zealand, Australia, South Africa, Canada, the RAF, the British Army's "Mother Country" side — playing a round-robin across Britain, the final at Twickenham, where New Zealand's soldiers beat the Mother Country 9–3 and took the cup from George V's hands. Tony Collins calls it rugby union's first world cup, and the point stings on both sides of the wall: the amateur game proved it could run a world tournament in 1919 — then declined to hold another for sixty-eight years, largely for fear of what such events did to amateurism. The Northern Union, note, was already selecting its Great Britain sides from the start — league's men had been touring the world since 1907, and in July 1914, in Sydney, had produced the code's foundation epic: the "Rorke's Drift" Test, Great Britain finishing with ten men and winning 14–6, Harold Wagstaff — the Sydney press said he tackled "with the tenacity of a grizzly bear" — captaining what league's memory keeps as its Agincourt.

Between the wars the northern game finished growing up. The Northern Union renamed itself the **Rugby Football League** in 1922, and on **4 May 1929** it did the most northern thing imaginable: it took its Challenge Cup final *to London*, to the new Wembley — Wigan 13, Dewsbury 2, before 41,500 people, a fixture that would become one of British sport's great annual pilgrimages, two towns' populations moving down the map for a day. And note the teams, because the detail explodes a myth the South told about the North: by one account Wigan that day fielded five Welshmen, two New Zealanders and a Scot, while Dewsbury were Yorkshiremen to a man but one. "Parochial" was the word union men used for league. The parochial code was the one already importing talent from three nations.

### The truce, and the experiment nobody repeated

Then the second war — and this time the wall itself bent. With every fit man in uniform and every code's fixtures suspended into service sport, the RFU did something unprecedented: it relaxed the lifetime bans, *for the duration of the war only*, so that rugby league men serving in the forces could play rugby union alongside their union comrades. The concession was meant as pragmatism. It became, twice, an experiment no one in authority had ever dared license: what happens when the two rugbies actually meet?

**23 January 1943, Headingley.** A Northern Command Rugby *League* XV plays a Northern Command Rugby *Union* XV — under union rules, the fifteen-a-side game the league men do not play, with the line-outs they abolished in 1897 — in front of eight thousand people. The league side wins, 18–11. The following year, at Bradford, a Combined Services League XV meets a Combined Services Union XV, again under union laws. League wins again, 15–10.

Be scrupulously fair to the union here, because the design of this chapter demands it. These were service matches, scratch sides, wartime rugby; the union's amateur regulations had never claimed league men were *worse players* — only that they were professionals, and therefore outside the family. Nothing in the amateur creed was formally refuted at Headingley. And yet. For forty-eight years the wall's builders had let stand the comfortable implication that the thirteen-a-side game was a lesser rugby played by lesser rugby men — and in the only two direct tests ever run, under the union's own laws, the professionals won both. The scoreline sat there, unarguable and unmentionable. When the war ended, the RFU did not convene an inquiry into what the experiment had shown. It reinstated the bans exactly as before — lifetime, total, contamination doctrine and all — and they would stand for another fifty years, until 1995. The wall had been tested, and the wall had failed, and the wall was rebuilt anyway, because it had never really been about rugby.

### What Generation 3 leaves behind

By 1945, then: two complete games, each with a temple (Twickenham; Wembley in May), each with an international family (the Five Nations, once France is readmitted; Great Britain, Australia, New Zealand), each with its dead on the same memorials, and a wall between them restored within months of a war in which its absurdity had been demonstrated in public, twice, by scoreboard. The union side of the wall holds a genuine golden inheritance — Wakefield's tactical revolution, the Slams, the fortress — and an unexamined verdict from Headingley 1943 that it will spend decades not thinking about. The league side holds a redesigned, exported, self-confident game — and a sentence, passed in London, that it remains socially invisible: no honours, no establishment, no place in the England the BBC broadcasts to.

What comes next is not a war but a long, cold peace — the era of boot money and brown envelopes on one side of the wall, of Odsal's hundred thousand on the other; of schoolboys banned from union for having once touched a league ball; of the amateur creed decaying from principle into pretence while the world's rugby unions, one by one, learned to cheat better than England did. Forty-nine years of it, until the wall's keepers, at the last, tore it down themselves in a Paris hotel. That long cold war is Generation 4.

## Generation 4: The Long Cold War (1946–1994)

**5 May 1954.** Odsal Stadium, Bradford — a vast clay bowl of a ground, a former rubbish tip banked with cinders — and the largest crowd ever to watch a game of rugby in either code, on any continent, is pouring into it. The Challenge Cup final between Warrington and Halifax had drawn 81,841 to Wembley eleven days earlier and finished 4–4; the replay is at Odsal, and the official count through the turnstiles is 102,569. The men who were there always said the real figure was nearer 120,000 — that the gates gave way, that thousands watched from the surrounding banks without ever passing a counter. Warrington win a taut, exhausted match 8–4. And the number stands for decades as a world record for rugby of any kind.

Hold that image against the calendar, because it is the pivot of this whole generation. In the spring of 1954, the biggest rugby crowd on earth assembled in a tip in Bradford to watch the professional, working-class, thirteen-a-side code that England's establishment had spent sixty years pretending was a squalid provincial embarrassment. And in those same years, on the other side of the wall, the amateur union was busy banning its own national heroes for the crime of keeping the royalties on their own life stories. Both those sentences are true; both belong to the same decade. This is the long cold war, and it lasted almost fifty years.

### The North at high tide, and low

Odsal was the crest. Rugby league in the late 1940s was one of the best-supported sports in Britain — aggregate season crowds touched 6.8 million in 1948–49 and again the year after, Wembley finals routinely past ninety thousand, the towns of the M62 corridor pouring out every Saturday for a game that was theirs and nobody else's. And then, slowly, the tide went out. The 1960s took their crowds the way they took everyone's — television, the motor car, the slum clearances that scattered the terraced streets a club had been the beating heart of — and league, lacking union's national footprint and establishment patronage, felt every loss more keenly.

The code responded the way it always had: by redesigning itself for the paying spectator. Where union treated its laws as inheritance, league treated them as tools. Unlimited possession was producing dull, stalling rugby, so in December 1966 league simply abolished it — the four-tackle rule, handing the ball over after four tackles — then refined it to six tackles in 1972, the structure the game still keeps. Substitutes came in, and Sunday matches. The instinct that had cut the game to thirteen men in 1906 was still alive: change the rules for the crowd, because the crowd is who the game is for.

On the field these were years of pride curdling into warning. In 1970 Great Britain won the Ashes in Australia — and no one watching knew they were watching the last time: it remains, more than half a century on, the final Ashes series Britain has ever won against the Australians. The reason why arrived on tour in 1982, when the Kangaroos came to Britain and France and won all twenty-two matches, scoring a thousand points and conceding barely a hundred, whitewashing Great Britain 3–0. The "Invincibles" did not just beat British league; they revealed a chasm — in fitness, in professionalism, in preparation — that the British game had not known was there. At home the era belonged to one club: Wigan, who between 1988 and 1995 won eight consecutive Challenge Cups and seven straight championships, a machine assembled with a chequebook the union game across the wall still could not, in law, open.

And how did the rest of England — the South, the middle class, the television audience — meet this game through all of it? Chiefly through the voice of Eddie Waring, the BBC's commentator, whose broad Yorkshire and knowing comic delivery *was* rugby league to millions who had never stood on a northern terrace. To the North he was a treasure; to a certain kind of southern viewer he confirmed exactly what they wished to believe — that league was a quaint, cloth-cap turn, funny and foreign and safely beneath them. The wall of 1895 had become, by the 1960s, a tone of voice.

### The gentlemen's game, still policing the money it pretended not to have

Now cross the wall, and watch the amateur union spend the same decades enforcing a lie that had long since stopped fooling anyone, including itself.

The creed was still absolute on paper: a rugby union man played for love, took nothing, and any breach professionalised him for life. The reality, by the 1970s and 1980s, was "boot money" — cash folded quietly into a player's boots in the changing room after a big match, an open secret at every major union in the world. England's players, watching Welsh and French and South African opponents, assumed — correctly — that everyone was being paid a little under the table, and that the only real rule was to keep the fiction intact. The union had come full circle to 1888: money everywhere, and a regulation whose true function was not to stop it but to decide who could be punished for it.

Who got punished tells you what the regulation was actually protecting. Consider Bill Beaumont — the Lancashire lock who captained England to the 1980 Grand Slam, their first in twenty-three years, and captained the Lions the same year: as beloved a figure as English rugby produced in his era. While still playing, he had to hand the RFU the fee he earned appearing on the television quiz *A Question of Sport*. And when concussions forced his retirement in 1982 and he wrote his autobiography, the act of keeping the royalties on his own life story made him, in the eyes of his own union, a professional — barred not just from playing but from coaching or administering the game he had led. A national hero, exiled over a book. The amateur regulations had reached the point of eating their own most decorated servants, and the men enforcing them could not see anything strange in it, because the principle was, to them, the whole point.

Be fair to what that principle contained, though — this chapter has insisted on it throughout, and must here too. The amateur ideal was not only snobbery. It carried a genuine vision: sport as something done for itself, not for money; the player as a man with a life and a trade beyond the game, not a commodity to be bought and used up. When John Pullin's England lost to Ireland in Dublin in 1973 — having travelled to a city that Wales and Scotland had refused to visit at the height of the Troubles — Pullin rose at the dinner and said: *"We might not be very good, but at least we turned up."* That line holds the amateur game at its best: modest, decent, brave in a way that had nothing to do with the scoreboard. The tragedy of English rugby union is not that this ideal was worthless. It is that the same institution which produced Pullin's grace produced, simultaneously, the machinery that exiled Beaumont and — as we are about to see — banned students for playing the wrong code for free.

### The absurdity that would not die

Here is where the long cold war reveals its cruelty most nakedly, and it is the beat this generation has been building toward.

The 1895 quarantine was still fully operational a century later, and it ran one way. A rugby union player who played rugby league — even a single match, even as an unpaid amateur, even for nothing but expenses — was professionalised on the spot and banned from union, potentially for life. A rugby league player faced no equivalent bar coming the other way, because the league game did not believe contact with union was a contamination. The wall was not a border. It was a one-way membrane, and the union side had built it.

In early 1993 Steve Pilgrim, a full-back for Wasps, turned out anonymously — listed as "A. N. Other" — in a rugby league trial for Leeds's reserves. He was paid nothing; the Leeds coach confirmed he had taken only expenses. When he was identified, the RFU declared him persona non grata and banned him. A union international-in-waiting, ended for playing a trial, for free, in the other code.

And then, in December 1994, the case that dragged the absurdity into Parliament. Ady Spencer was a centre for Cambridge University, in line for the Varsity Match — the most gilded amateur fixture in England, the one occasion when the university game still commanded a Twickenham crowd. Spencer had also played amateur, unpaid rugby league. The RFU banned him for twelve months and barred him from the Varsity Match. This time the reaction escaped the rugby press: an Early Day Motion was tabled in the House of Commons condemning the ban as hypocrisy, and it carried the detail that finished the argument — the Inland Revenue had by then formally assessed top-level rugby union as *at least as professional* as top-level rugby league. The taxman had ruled the union game professional while the union was banning a student for playing the other code for nothing. There had been a nominal "free gangway" agreed for amateurs to move between codes since 1987; the Pilgrim and Spencer bans show what it was worth. A hundred years after the George Hotel, the wall was still taking prisoners — and now it was doing it to unpaid undergraduates, in public, in front of a Parliament that had begun to laugh.

### The world changes the game before the game can stop it

The thing that finally killed the amateur regime was not a moral awakening. It was a World Cup and a television cheque.

Rugby union had refused to hold a global tournament for the entire twentieth century, largely because the men who ran it understood exactly what such an event would do: create stars, create audiences, create value — and value, in a sport of unpaid players, is a pressure that has to go somewhere. When the International Rugby Football Board finally voted, in Paris in March 1985, to stage a first World Cup, it passed by ten votes to six, and the two unions that voted against were Ireland and Scotland — the amateur game's truest believers, who saw precisely where it led. England and Wales came round. The tournament went ahead in 1987, and England played poorly in it, losing a dismal quarter-final to Wales 16–3 in Brisbane. But the box had been opened.

What came out of it, on the field, was the best England side in a generation. In May 1988 the RFU handed the captaincy to Will Carling, a centre of twenty-two — the youngest England captain anyone could remember — and around him assembled a genuinely formidable team: Guscott's ease, Underwood on the wing, Winterbottom in the back row, a granite front five. England won back-to-back Grand Slams in 1991 and 1992, the first sealed 21–19 against France at Twickenham in a match remembered now partly for a length-of-the-field French try voted one of the ground's greatest. And in 1991 they reached a home World Cup final — where they made the tactical blunder that has followed them ever since, abandoning the bludgeoning forward game that had carried them there for an unfamiliar running game, and losing to Australia 12–6 at Twickenham. A great side, an amateur side, playing for a trophy worth millions to everyone except the men on the pitch.

That gap — between what the players generated and what the players were allowed to receive — was now unsustainable, and everyone inside the game could feel it. Trust funds, appearance money, kit and boot sponsorships crept further into the open; France and the southern hemisphere paid in all but name. The RFU's secretary Dudley Wood remained the amateur creed's last articulate defender, insisting most of the game still wished to stay amateur even as the evidence buried him. The players knew better. In May 1995, caught on camera describing the RFU committee in a phrase that will open the next generation, Will Carling said aloud what the whole playing class had concluded about the men still guarding the amateur wall. He was sacked as captain, and reinstated within forty-eight hours, because by 1995 everyone knew who actually held the power — and it was no longer the committee room.

### What Generation 4 leaves behind

By 1994, then, both English rugbies are standing at the edge of a cliff they cannot see the bottom of. League has passed its Odsal peak and been humbled by Australia, but it remains what it has been since 1895: honest about being a paid game, redesigned again and again for the people who pay to watch it, unashamed. Union has produced Pullin's grace and Beaumont's dignity and Carling's Grand Slams — and also boot money, the exile of its own heroes, and the spectacle of banning unpaid students while the taxman calls the whole enterprise professional. The amateur wall is still standing. It is also, visibly, a fiction that no one under the age of forty believes any more.

What neither code yet knows is that the wall is about to come down — not brick by brick over decades, but all at once, in a single stunned week in a Paris hotel in August 1995, a hundred years almost to the day after the George Hotel. And when it falls, both games will be hit by the same tidal wave of money, and English rugby union will spend the following decade proving that it learned nothing at all from the century it spent keeping the working man out — except, at the very end, and by accident, how to win a World Cup with one of his sons. That is Generation 5.

## Generation 5: Open — The Hundred-Year Debt (1995–2003)

**26 August 1995.** A conference room in Paris, the International Rugby Football Board in session, and the chairman — a Welsh QC named Vernon Pugh — puts the only realistic sentence on the table: *"Whether or not we promote it, the game will be openly professional within a very short space of time."* The Board votes. Rugby union, after a hundred and twenty-four years of insisting that a man who took a shilling for playing had defiled something sacred, declares itself an open game. Payment is legal. The amateur wall — the thing the RFU broke its own sport in half to defend in 1895 — is gone, dismantled by the very institution that built it, in an afternoon, without a shot fired.

Now look at the date, because this book has been waiting the whole chapter to point at it. **29 August 1895:** twenty-one clubs at the George Hotel in Huddersfield form the Northern Union rather than live another season as suspected criminals for wanting six shillings of lost wages back. **26 August 1995:** the Rugby Football Union's own world body legalises payment outright. Ninety-nine years and three days apart. The men expelled and quarantined and banned-for-life across a century — Lockwood bankrupt, Beaumont exiled over a book, Spencer barred from the Varsity Match weeks before the end — were, it turned out, simply early. The debt English rugby union ran up at the George Hotel came due, almost to the day, a hundred years later. And nobody in that Paris room said sorry, because institutions do not apologise; they adjust.

### The same money, on both sides of the wall, at once

Here is the detail that makes 1995 a true convergence and not merely an ending: the wave that broke over union broke over league in the same twelve months, and it came from the same source.

What forced the IRB's hand in Paris was money — specifically, a US$550 million deal that Rupert Murdoch's News Corporation had struck to televise a new professional southern-hemisphere union competition, and the counter-threat of a rival circuit backed by Kerry Packer signing up the world's players. Union went open because the broadcasters had already made it professional in everything but law. And at the very same moment, the identical forces were tearing through rugby league: News Corp had bought league too, bankrolling a new Super League, and in the spring of 1995 the game's authorities tried to rationalise a century of proud little clubs into a handful of television-friendly mega-teams — proposed mergers with invented names like Calder and Cheshire and Humberside. The towns rose up. The fan revolt that greeted those merger plans, announced on a Good Friday, was so ferocious that the whole scheme was scrapped within weeks — but the deeper change held: from 1996 league abandoned its winter and moved to summer, remade for the television schedule that now paid its bills.

So the two English rugbies, separated by a wall for a hundred years, were remade by the same Murdoch-era cheque in the same year. Union stopped pretending its players were amateurs; league stopped playing in winter. Same mogul, same moment, the two halves of the 1895 divorce dragged into the modern world side by side. Whatever else professionalism was, it was the first thing since the George Hotel that happened to both codes at once.

### Union discovers what league learned in 1895

There is a bitter comedy in what happened to English rugby union next, and it is best appreciated by remembering that everything about to go wrong was something rugby league had already lived through, and warned about, and survived.

The moment payment became legal, money arrived — but the game had no professional structure to receive it, so it arrived as chaos. Wealthy men bought the leading clubs: Nigel Wray at Saracens, Sir John Hall — fresh from building Newcastle United — at Newcastle, dreaming of a rugby Barcelona; Ashley Levett at Richmond. These owners now paid the players' wages, and promptly discovered they did not control the players' most valuable use: England internationals were released to the RFU for matches the clubs got nothing for. The result was a civil war between the club owners and the union over who owned the professional player — the exact question the Northern Union had answered cleanly in 1895 by simply paying men to play, and which English union now had to fight out from scratch, furiously, in public.

It reached farce in June 1998. With the clubs refusing to release their stars for a summer tour of the southern hemisphere, the RFU sent a depleted, half-capped England to be slaughtered — beaten 76–0 by Australia in Brisbane, thrashed by New Zealand twice and South Africa besides, seven matches without a win, a tour the players themselves called the tour from hell. Matt Dawson named the disease exactly: *"Nobody was thinking about what was best for the players — it was all about what was best for the clubs, the RFU and England."* And the free market that professionalism unleashed did what unregulated markets do: in 1999 Richmond — one of the union's founding twenty-one clubs from the Pall Mall Restaurant in 1871 — went into administration with debts around eight million pounds and effectively ceased to exist as a senior side; London Scottish went with them. Within four years of going open, English union had bankrupted two of its historic clubs and had to impose a salary cap to stop the bleeding. Everything the amateur men had prophesied about money destroying the club game came true — not because they had been right about money, but because they had spent a century refusing to build the professional structures that would have managed it. League had built those in 1895. Union had to improvise them in 1996, in a panic.

### The membrane reverses

But professionalism did one thing that was pure poetry, and it is the heart of this generation.

For a hundred years the wall of 1895 had been a one-way membrane. Union's players drained north to league and could never come back; league's players were forbidden to come south at all. Now, with union able to pay, the current could finally run the other way — and it did. Union clubs began signing league's stars: Iestyn Harris from Leeds to Cardiff, Henry Paul from Wigan to Gloucester, and — the signing that mattered above every other — Jason Robinson, from Wigan to Sale, in November 2000.

Read the direction of that traffic against the whole chapter. Since the George Hotel, talent had flowed one way, from the gentlemen's game to the working man's, because only the working man's game would pay. Now a professional union was reaching *north*, into the towns it had walled itself off from, and asking their best men to come and play the fifteen-a-side code — not as contaminated outcasts, but as expensive, coveted signings. The men who had been banned for life for touching a league ball were replaced, within one generation, by men transferred *from* league for six-figure fees. The wall had not just fallen. The river had turned around and started flowing back through the gap.

### The convergence man

Jason Robinson was born in Leeds in 1974, and he was rugby league to the marrow: Wigan from the age of seventeen, two hundred and eighty-one appearances, a hundred and seventy-one tries, twelve Great Britain caps, a world club championship — a small, impossibly quick working-class Yorkshireman they called Billy Whizz, a product of exactly the game and exactly the class that the Rugby Football Union had built its wall to keep out in 1895. In November 2000 he crossed to union with Sale, and within months he was an England wing and a British Lion, scoring in the first Test in Brisbane in 2001. And on 22 November 2003, in Sydney, he scored the only try England have ever scored in a World Cup final.

We will come to the match. Hold, first, on the meaning of that try. English rugby union's single greatest day, the summit of everything the amateur establishment's descendants built — and its defining score was dived over in the corner by a rugby league man from Leeds, the son of the code and the class the RFU spent a hundred years excommunicating. In the South Africa chapter of this book, the braid of a divided nation was shown to have held the day a boy from Zwide lifted the World Cup. This is England's version of the same moment. The proof that the hundred-year wall was truly down was not the vote in Paris in 1995. It was a working-class northerner scoring union's most important try eight years later, and nobody — not one person at Twickenham — thinking there was anything strange about it at all.

### The machine that got him there

The man who built the team around Robinson was Clive Woodward, appointed England's first full-time professional head coach in September 1997, and Woodward's contribution was to treat rugby union as if it had actually become professional — which, remarkably, no one else quite had. His philosophy was the accumulation of tiny advantages: hundreds of what he called "critical non-essentials," the marginal gains later summarised as the idea of doing a hundred things one per cent better. Vision coaches, a dedicated kit that was harder to grab, throat lozenges to counter the Sydney humidity, his own analysts and doctors and psychologists — the apparatus of a business, applied to a game that had until yesterday been run by volunteers on committees.

It did not work at once. Woodward's England spent years falling at the final step: a home World Cup quarter-final in 1999 lost to South Africa when Jannie de Beer dropped five impossible goals out of the Paris sky; three Six Nations campaigns in a row — 1999, 2000, 2001 — where England won four matches and then lost the Grand Slam on the last day, twice to Scotland, once to Ireland in a decider that foot-and-mouth disease had pushed all the way to the autumn. The machine kept generating power and stalling at the summit. And then, in 2003, it stopped stalling. England won the Grand Slam at last, sealing it 42–6 in Dublin; and that summer they went south and did what no England side had done, beating New Zealand in Wellington — famously holding out a whole All Black onslaught while reduced to thirteen men — and Australia in Melbourne, on consecutive weekends. They arrived at the World Cup ranked first in the world and expected, for once, to win it.

### 22 November 2003. Telstra Stadium, Sydney.

Nearly eighty-three thousand people, a South African referee, and the two best sides in the world: England, Martin Johnson's granite team built for exactly this, against a Wallaby side with home advantage and nothing to lose. England led 14–5 at half-time, the try Robinson's — Lawrence Dallaglio breaking, Jonny Wilkinson's pass, Billy Whizz over in the left corner. It should have been enough. It was not, because Elton Flatley kicked Australia back into it with the patience of a metronome, and the match reached full time locked at 14–14 and went to extra time.

In extra time Wilkinson kicked England ahead; Flatley, with almost the last act of normal time in the second period, kicked Australia level again, 17–17. And so it came down to the final seconds and the single most replayed passage of play in English rugby history. England worked the ball into position; Matt Dawson darted and made the yards; the forwards set it; the ball came back to Wilkinson — a left-footed kicker — who, with about twenty-six seconds of the World Cup remaining, dropped the goal off his *weaker right foot*. 20–17. Australia had no time to reply. England were world champions: the first side from the northern hemisphere ever to win the tournament, and — as things still stood when this book closed in 2026 — the only one.

### What Generation 5 leaves behind

Sit with the completed picture, because it is the argument of the entire chapter delivered in a single generation. In 1995, near the hundredth anniversary of the day the North walked out, English rugby union abolished the amateur wall it had shattered the game to build — and paid, immediately, in the currency it had always feared, with bankruptcies and civil war and a 76–0 humiliation, learning by disaster the lessons league had learned by design in 1895. And in 2003 it reached the mountaintop it had cleared the working man out of the way to climb — and got there carried, at the decisive moment, by a working man from Leeds who had learned his rugby in the code the wall was built to exclude. The debt of the George Hotel was called in twice: once in Paris, as money, and once in Sydney, as grace.

What this generation does not know is how quickly the money it let in would start to rot the thing it built. The World Cup of 2003 was the last unambiguous good morning English rugby union would have for a long time. What followed was fifteen years of all the money in the world, and remarkably little to show for it but two lost finals, one unforgivable home humiliation, and a professional club game quietly inflating toward a reckoning that no trophy could hold off. That is Generation 6.

## Generation 6: All the Money in the World (2004–2019)

**14 September 2007.** The Stade de France, the pool stage of the World Cup, and the reigning champions of the world are being taken apart. South Africa 36, England 0 — the first time in the tournament's history that England have failed to score a single point, the holders of the Webb Ellis Cup humiliated in the group stage of the competition they won four years earlier. It is, on its face, the lowest afternoon of the professional era.

It is also, on its face, a lie about the story — because this same broken England side would drag itself, impossibly, all the way back to the final of that very tournament, and lose it by nine points to the same South Africans, denied a match-turning try by the width of a bootlace. That whipsaw — catastrophe and near-glory tangled together, neither one the real story — is the shape of this entire generation. For fifteen years after Sydney, English rugby union had more money than it had ever dreamed of and almost nothing coherent to buy with it. This is the era of all the money in the world, and of watching the game not quite know what to do with any of it.

### The morning after the mountaintop

The collapse began, as these things do, at the summit. Clive Woodward — the architect of 2003 — resigned less than a year after Sydney, worn down by exactly the club-versus-country war that professionalism had unleashed and never resolved: he could not get the control over players and preparation that his machine required, and he walked, off to coach a Lions tour to New Zealand in 2005 that was whitewashed 3–0. The champions retired around the same time — Johnson, Vickery, Kay, the spine of the thing — and England discovered that its World Cup had been the achievement of a particular group of men and a particular coach, not the arrival of a durable system. Andy Robinson inherited the wreckage and then Brian Ashton did, and the world champions spent three years looking like a side that had mislaid the instructions.

Which is what made 2007 so strange. A team in visible disarray, thrashed 36–0 in the pool, somehow found in the knockout rounds the one thing the professional English game could always still summon — a suffocating forward game and a flawless kicker — and ground its way past Australia 12–10 in Marseille and past the host nation France 14–9 in Paris, Jonny Wilkinson's boot doing what it had done in Sydney. And in the final, against South Africa, England were denied the score that might have changed everything: Mark Cueto dived in at the left corner, and after an agonising video review the try was disallowed because his left foot had grazed the touchline a fraction before he grounded the ball. South Africa won 15–6. It was Jason Robinson's last match for England — the convergence man, the league boy from Leeds, leaving the field to an ovation in his fifty-first and final Test. England had reached a World Cup final they had no business reaching, and lost it by the margin of a brushed white line. Nobody could say what England actually *were* any more.

### The tour of shame

What came next was worse, and it was not about rugby at all. In 2008 the RFU handed control of the national team to Martin Johnson — the 2003 captain, a colossus as a player, with no coaching experience whatsoever — on the theory that his aura would substitute for a system. It did not. His England won a Six Nations title but the story of his tenure was written at the 2011 World Cup in New Zealand, and it was written in the tabloids, not the sports pages.

After beating Argentina, England players spent an evening at a Queenstown bar hosting a "Mad Midget Weekender," and the "dwarf-throwing" headlines wrote themselves. The captain, Mike Tindall — newly married into the Royal Family — was filmed in a bar with a woman and fined £25,000; a bouncer later tried to sell the CCTV. Off the field it was a fortnight of squalid farce; on it, England were knocked out in the quarter-final by France, 19–12, having trailed 16–0. Johnson resigned. The richest rugby union in the world had, within eight years of being world champions, become a story about drunk millionaires embarrassing themselves in a ski town. Whatever professionalism had bought, it had not bought seriousness.

### Decency, and the day it wasn't enough

The RFU's answer was Stuart Lancaster, and Lancaster's answer was character. A thoughtful, earnest coach with a schoolmaster's belief in values, he was hired in 2012 explicitly to cleanse the culture Johnson's era had befouled — to make wearing the England shirt mean something decent again. He very nearly built a good team. Four years running his England finished second in the Six Nations, always close, never champions. And then the fixture that his whole project had been pointing at arrived: the 2015 World Cup, in England, hosted at Twickenham, the tournament England had waited a generation to stage on home soil.

They went out in the pool. England became the first host nation in the history of the World Cup to fail to reach the knockout stages — beaten 28–25 by Wales at Twickenham after leading, then put away 33–13 by Australia. It remains the single most humiliating result in English rugby union history, worse than any 36–0, because it happened at home, in the tournament they were running, in front of the whole watching country. Lancaster was sacked. And the decision that came to symbolise the whole collapse — fairly or not — returns this chapter, one final time, to the wall of 1895.

### Sam Burgess, and the braid that didn't take

Lancaster had picked Sam Burgess in the centre against Wales, and the row over that selection swallowed everything else.

Burgess was a rugby league man — and not a marginal one. In the 2014 NRL Grand Final in Australia, playing for South Sydney, he had broken his cheekbone and eye socket in the opening tackle of the match, played the remaining seventy-nine minutes anyway, and been named the man of the match: one of the iconic acts of hardness in the modern history of the thirteen-a-side code. Bath had brought him to union in 2014, and Lancaster, entranced, fast-tracked him into a home World Cup at a position he had barely played, over specialist centres who had spent their lives there. When it went wrong against Wales, Burgess became the emblem of the failure — and within months he had gone back to league, back to South Sydney, the union experiment abandoned.

Set him beside Jason Robinson and you have the whole truth about convergence in one comparison, and it is a truth that honours both codes. The wall of 1895 came down in 1995, and league men could finally cross to union — but crossing was never magic, and the crowd that watched Robinson glide in at Sydney was watching a man who had spent five years learning a new game, not a league star parachuted in and asked to save a tournament. Burgess was blameless; he was a great player asked to do an impossible thing on an impossible timetable, by a coaching staff that mistook the removal of the wall for the abolition of the difference between the two games. The codes had converged. They had not become the same. Robinson is what happens when you respect that; Burgess is what happens when you forget it.

### Eddie

The man hired to end the chaos was, pointedly, not an Englishman. Eddie Jones — Australian, abrasive, the coach who had just masterminded Japan's stunning defeat of South Africa at the 2015 World Cup — became England's first foreign head coach in November 2015, and he did what no home-grown appointment had managed since Woodward: he made England relentless. A Grand Slam in his first season, the first since 2003; a run of eighteen consecutive Test victories that equalled the world record; a team that in 2016 simply did not lose.

And then the familiar English pattern reasserted itself, because Jones's England, like every England of this generation, could not hold an altitude. A five-Test losing streak in 2018 had people questioning his future — before the whole cycle turned once more toward the light for one last, genuinely magnificent night. In the semi-final of the 2019 World Cup in Yokohama, England produced perhaps the finest single performance of the professional era, dismantling the All Blacks 19–7, Manu Tuilagi over inside two minutes, New Zealand never allowed to breathe. For eighty minutes England looked like the best team on earth.

Six days later they lost the final. South Africa beat them 32–12, and the shape of the defeat told its own story: Kyle Sinckler, England's tighthead prop, was knocked unconscious in the opening minutes, and England were simply overpowered up front by a South African team built for exactly that war, Mapimpi and Kolbe finishing it off late. The captain who lifted the trophy was Siya Kolisi — the first Black man to raise the Webb Ellis Cup, the story this book tells in full in its South Africa chapter. England, for the third time in the professional era, had reached a World Cup final; for the second time, they had lost it. Magnificent, and not enough. It was becoming the epitaph of the whole generation.

### The reckoning being built underneath

But the deepest story of these years was not on the field at all. It was in the accounts, and it was being written toward a disaster the next generation would have to pay for.

Professionalism had promised that money would make English club rugby great. Instead, the money went out faster than it came in. The Premiership played to full, happy stadiums across the 2010s and lost a fortune doing it — collective losses of around £44 million in a single season, 2017–18, on turnover of a little over £200 million, with individual clubs bleeding millions a year each. The salary cap crept upward, wages outran revenue, and the model simply did not close. In December 2018 the league sold roughly a quarter of itself to the private-equity firm CVC for about £200 million — a cash injection dressed as a growth strategy, which in truth mostly papered over the hole for a few more years.

And in November 2019, as the World Cup final's echoes were still fading, the rot surfaced with a name attached. Saracens — the dominant English club of the decade, four titles in five years — were found to have breached the salary cap for three seasons running, their owner Nigel Wray having funnelled money to star players through image-rights and co-investment arrangements the authorities ruled were disguised wages. The punishment was the heaviest in the English game's history: a £5.36 million fine, a huge points deduction, and relegation from the Premiership for a club that had been its champion. It was 1895's oldest question in a twenty-first-century suit — who is allowed to pay players, and how, and what happens when the rules meet the money — and it broke exactly as the professional club game was heading into its most dangerous decade. Flag it, and hold it: it belongs to the reckoning of Generation 7.

### And across the wall, the same years

Rugby league lived these years in its own key — smaller, poorer, and, as ever, more honest about both. Super League ran its summer game through the era of Leeds Rhinos, eight times champions, led by Kevin Sinfield, the greatest points-scorer the code had produced — a man who, in a small perfect sign of how far the century had travelled, would himself cross to rugby union in 2021, not as a player but as a coach at Leicester. The two codes now met in the coaches' box as easily as they had once refused to meet at all. When the Challenge Cup returned to the rebuilt Wembley in 2007, more than eighty-four thousand watched St Helens beat a French club in the final — the thirteen-a-side game still, quietly, spreading. But the crowds drifted down through the decade, the London and expansion clubs never took root, and league entered the 2020s as it had entered every decade since the George Hotel: surviving, shrinking a little, and entirely itself.

### What Generation 6 leaves behind

By the end of 2019, then, English rugby union is a paradox with a full bank account. It has been to three World Cup finals in the professional era and won none of the last two; it has produced, in the same fifteen years, the 36–0 and the tour of shame and the home-pool humiliation *and* the Yokohama semi-final and Eddie Jones's perfect 2016 — greatness and disgrace in restless alternation, a game that cannot decide what it is. And underneath the whole spectacle, the professional club game it built has been quietly insolvent for years, held up by private equity and rule-bending, one shock away from clubs actually dying.

The shock is coming. The next generation is where the bill for all of it arrives at once — the financial bill, as historic clubs go bankrupt and vanish; and a second bill, deeper and more terrible, that neither code saw being run up across a century of collisions: the bill for what the game does to the human brain. The players who gave English rugby its greatest days are about to sue it, in both codes, for the damage it did them. That reckoning — financial and physical, and owed by both sides of a wall that no longer means anything — is Generation 7.

## Generation 7: The Bill (2020–2026)

**December 2020.** A former England hooker sits in front of a camera and says the thing that ends the chapter. Steve Thompson won the Rugby World Cup in 2003 — he was in the front row that night in Sydney, part of the pack that ground Australia down while Jonny Wilkinson waited for his drop goal. He is in his early forties. He has been diagnosed with early-onset dementia and probable chronic traumatic encephalopathy, the degenerative brain disease caused by repeated blows to the head. And he cannot remember the greatest day of his professional life.

*"You see us lifting the World Cup and I can see me there jumping around,"* he says. *"But I can't remember it."* He has said, elsewhere, that he can no longer remember being in Australia at all; that there are mornings he cannot summon his own children's names. He has pledged to donate his brain to science when he dies, so that it can be sliced and studied for the disease that is hollowing it now.

This is the generation the bill comes due. Every earlier generation of this chapter has been about who was allowed *in* to rugby, and on what terms, and who paid the price for the wall between the two codes. This one is about a different price entirely — one paid in the same currency by everyone who ever played the game hard enough, on either side of the wall, regardless of class, code, or the colour of the money. It is about what the collisions did to the men who made them. And it arrives, in these years, as two literal bills: one financial, as the professional club game English rugby built finally begins to collapse; and one human, immeasurably heavier, as the players who gave the game its greatest days take it to court for the damage it did to their brains.

### The case

Steve Thompson was not alone, and that is the point. He was the face of a group legal action that by the middle of 2025 had gathered more than a thousand former players — and it was brought not against one governing body but against the lot of them: World Rugby, the Rugby Football Union, the Welsh Rugby Union, *and the Rugby Football League*. Read that list against the whole of this chapter. For a hundred years the two codes had defined themselves against each other across a wall of bans and boycotts and mutual class contempt. Now their former players stood on the same side of a courtroom, suing both institutions at once, for the same injury. The wall of 1895 meant nothing to a diseased brain. A concussion does not ask whether you were paid.

The claimants were a roll-call of the game's own heroes. Alongside Thompson stood Michael Lipman and, from Wales, Alix Popham and the former national captain Ryan Jones, diagnosed with dementia in his forties; the great All Black Carl Hayman; and, revealed as the numbers swelled, two more of Thompson's own 2003 World Cup winners, Phil Vickery and Mark Regan. Their case was simple and grave: that the authorities had known for years about the danger of concussive and — crucially — *sub-concussive* blows, the countless smaller impacts of every training session and every match, and had failed to take reasonable steps to protect the men absorbing them. By mid-2026 the litigation was still grinding through its preliminaries — a High Court judge had, in December 2025, cleared the way for a set of lead "bellwether" cases to be selected, the great questions of liability and cause still unanswered — and the governing bodies were still contesting it. No admission had been made. No settlement had been reached. A thousand damaged men were still waiting.

### What the science was finding while they waited

The waiting was made worse by what the laboratories were turning up. When researchers at Boston University — the institution that had exposed the same disease in American football — examined the brains of thirty-one former rugby union players, they found chronic traumatic encephalopathy in around two-thirds of them. And they found something that struck at the deepest assumption the sport had always made about itself: the risk rose with the number of *years played*, more or less independently of how many diagnosed concussions a man had suffered. It was not, principally, the big knockouts that did the damage. It was the accumulation — the ordinary, unremarkable, thousands of collisions of an ordinary career. "CTE is a preventable disease," the Boston neuropathologist Ann McKee said; "there is an urgent need to reduce not only the number of head impacts, but the strength of those impacts, in rugby." Preventable. The word sat over the whole reckoning like a verdict, because preventable means someone could have prevented it, and did not.

### The same grief, across the wall

Rugby league was living its own version of the reckoning, and it was living it, as league lives everything, more visibly and with less cushioning.

Its emblem was Rob Burrow — the tiny, beloved Leeds Rhinos half-back, one of the great figures of the summer game, diagnosed at thirty-seven with motor neurone disease and dead at forty-one in June 2024. In his dying years Burrow and his old Leeds captain Kevin Sinfield — the same Sinfield who by then had crossed to coach in rugby union, the wall dissolving even here — raised some twenty million pounds for MND care, Sinfield running seven marathons in seven days, the two men's friendship becoming one of the most moving things in British sport. Burrow was not the only one: the Scotland lock Doddie Weir and the Gloucester forward Ed Slater carried the same disease.

Here a historian has to be careful, and this book will be: the scientific link between rugby and motor neurone disease is *not* established — the MND charities say so plainly, and this book will not claim a causation the evidence has not proven. But the league game's brain-injury litigation was real and ran in exact parallel to union's, the two actions folded into the same thousand-plus claimants, covered by the same court ruling; a sub-group of former league players tested by neurologists reported degenerative-brain symptoms at rates that frightened everyone who read them. Whatever the courts eventually decided about cause, the shape was undeniable: both codes, a century after they split over who deserved six shillings for a Saturday's lost wages, were now being asked by their own people to account for what a lifetime of the game had cost their heads. The bill for the brain was the first bill in this entire history that fell equally on the gentleman and the miner. Institutions had spent a hundred years deciding rugby's class. The disease did not care.

### The other bill: the clubs die

While that reckoning gathered, the financial structure of English professional union — the one whose insolvency had been flagged at the close of the last generation — simply gave way.

In the space of eight months across 2022 and 2023, three historic clubs collapsed. Worcester Warriors went first, hit with a winding-up order over unpaid tax and tipped into administration in October 2022. Wasps — a club that traced its name to the founding age of the game, one of the great institutions of English rugby — went into administration the same month, and by May 2023 had had its licence revoked and been dropped to the very bottom of the pyramid. London Irish followed in June 2023, bust with debts north of thirty million pounds. Three clubs, thousands of jobs, whole communities of supporters — gone inside a single season. The Premiership, which had run at twelve clubs, shrank to ten, and in February 2026 the union formally abolished automatic promotion and relegation altogether, sealing the top flight into a closed shop — Ealing Trailfinders won the second-tier title and were refused entry regardless. The professional club game that going open had promised to build had, thirty years on, contracted into a smaller, frightened, ring-fenced version of itself, propped up by private equity and closed to the clubs below.

### The house that Twickenham built, wobbling

Even the RFU itself — the richest union in the world, the body that had run English rugby since the Pall Mall Restaurant in 1871 — was in open crisis. It posted a record operating loss of nearly thirty-eight million pounds for 2023–24. And in the middle of the wreckage came the detail that turned crisis into revolt: the union's chief executive, Bill Sweeney, had been paid a package of around 1.1 million pounds — salary plus a large bonus — in the same period that the RFU shed staff and the England team struggled. A hundred and forty-one member clubs, the grassroots of the whole edifice, forced a Special General Meeting, the first in twenty years. Sweeney survived the confidence vote; the chairman, Tom Ilube, had already resigned.

And here the chapter closes a circle it opened forty years of narrative ago. The man brought in as interim chairman to steady the RFU in 2025 was Sir Bill Beaumont — the same Bill Beaumont whom this same union had cast out as a "professional" in 1982, banned from all involvement in the game, for the crime of keeping the royalties on his own autobiography. The institution that had exiled its 1980 Grand Slam captain over a book now called him back, four decades later, to hold the wreck together. No sentence in this chapter makes the argument better than that one true fact. Rugby has no class, and no fixed loyalties, and no memory that money cannot revise; institutions have all three, and they change their minds about a man the moment their needs change. Beaumont was unclean in 1982 and indispensable in 2025, and the only thing that had altered was what the RFU required of him.

### The team, still playing

Through all of it, England kept taking the field, because they always do. Eddie Jones was sacked at the end of 2022 and replaced by Steve Borthwick, a former England captain — who brought with him, as his defence coach, Kevin Sinfield, the rugby league legend, so that a working-class hero of the thirteen-a-side code now helped run the England union side, the wall of 1895 gone even from the coaches' box. Borthwick's England, unfancied, reached the semi-final of the 2023 World Cup and came within a single late Handré Pollard penalty of the final, losing 16–15 to the South Africa side this book follows to its own conclusion in another chapter, before taking the bronze — England's first — against Argentina.

There were human costs here too, of a quieter kind. Owen Farrell — England's captain, one of the most driven men ever to play for them — stepped away from international rugby in 2024 to protect his mental health, having been booed and picked over through the World Cup. This book will not diagnose him from a distance; it only notes that the game exacts more than one kind of toll on the people who carry it, and that a generation which had learned, at last, to talk about the brain was learning, slowly and incompletely, to talk about the mind as well. The captaincy passed to Maro Itoje, who led the 2025 Lions and won his hundredth cap in February 2026. And England's rugby, at the mid-2026 edge of this history, was neither triumphant nor disgraced but simply, recognisably itself: capable of beating anyone on its day, capable of a wretched Six Nations the next spring, forever a great power that could not quite become a settled one.

### Where things stood, mid-2026

On 4 July 2026, at Ellis Park in Johannesburg, in the opening round of the new global Nations Championship, South Africa beat England 45–21 — seven tries to a side that fought its way back to 17–14 before two sin-binnings left it defending with thirteen men. It was a fair snapshot of where England stood a year out from the 2027 World Cup in Australia: good enough to trouble the world champions for half an hour, not yet good enough to live with them for eighty. Borthwick had the union's backing through to that tournament. The horizon was Australia, 2027.

But the true measure of English rugby in mid-2026 was not the scoreboard at Ellis Park. It was the courtroom, and it was still unresolved. More than a thousand former players, from both codes, were still waiting for World Rugby, the RFU, the Welsh union and the Rugby League to answer for what the game had done to their brains. The sport had, by now, begun to change what it could: smart mouthguards in the elite game from 2024, flagging every dangerous impact to an independent doctor; the tackle height in the community game lowered below the sternum from 2023; a players' campaign, Progressive Rugby, pushing hard for real limits on contact. These were not nothing. But no institution had yet admitted that it had known, and failed, and let the damage accrue. The reckoning was in motion and radically incomplete — the sport reforming its future while still contesting its past.

### The whole argument, closed

Stand back now, at the end, and look at the shape of the whole thing — because England is where the book began and England is where its argument comes to rest.

A game invented by schoolboys and given a fake gentleman founder the very year it split. A union built by and for the professional classes of the South, which chose, when the working North asked for six shillings of lost wages, to break the sport in half rather than share it — and then spent a century enforcing the wall it had built, banning children and exiling its own heroes and losing to the professionals every time the two codes actually met. A hundred-year debt, called in at last in a Paris hotel in 1995, near the anniversary of the day the North walked out; and a redemption, of a kind, in a working-class league man from Leeds scoring the only try in England's one World Cup final. And then, at the end, the discovery that all of it — the wall, the class war, the amateur lie, the money that finally drowned the lie — was a quarrel conducted *above* a truth none of them were looking at: that the game itself, the beautiful violent collision at the heart of both codes, was quietly injuring the brains of everyone who played it, gentleman and miner alike, in Huddersfield and Twickenham without distinction.

That is the last word of this chapter, and it is the book's thesis carried to its hardest conclusion. **Rugby has no class — institutions do.** For a hundred and fifty years English rugby's institutions produced a class war, a myth, a wall, and a lie, and called the result the nature of the game. But the game's real nature was never in the class of the men who played it. It was in the collision itself — and the collision, it turns out, was the one thing the institutions never thought to protect anyone from. The men who won the World Cup and the men who filled Odsal are sending their brains to the same laboratory now. Whatever the courts decide, that is the truest thing the game ever said about itself, and it took two hundred years and a thousand damaged men to say it: on the field, at the moment of contact, rugby really has no class at all.
