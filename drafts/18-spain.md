# Spain

## Generation 0: Over the Pyrenees (1894–1928)

**February 1901. The Bonanova velodrome, Barcelona.**

The footballers have the ground. Players of the **Foot-Ball Club Barcelona** and of **Català FC** have come to the cycle track to play the game they are known for, and at some point in the afternoon they put the round-ball game aside and try another. The sports correspondent who wrote it up signed as "A Forward" (*Un Delantero*), and gave it a single line: some trials of rugby football were also held on the same ground (*"Efectuáronse asimismo en dicho terreno algunos ensayos de foot-ball Rugby"*).

The line names no score and no sides, and passes no verdict. It would be a footnote if not for who was on the ground.

### "Too Rough and Brutal"

In 1902 a profile of FC Barcelona's first and second teams set out where the club's men had learned their sport, and a surprising amount of what it listed was rugby. **Arthur Witty** had gone to school in Liverpool at eleven and played in its first XV from 1893 to 1895. **Jaime Alberto Eckes** had been at school in Liverpool in 1893. **Alfonso Albéniz** was "a member of Racing Club in Paris, where he has played rugby football" (*"miembro del 'Racing Club' de París en donde ha practicado el foot-ball rugby"*). **Cyril Alexander** had played in Scotland, and the profile was precise about it: "rugby only" (*"rugby solamente"*). The club's founder, **Joan Gamper**, had played at Lyon.

Gamper's Lyon year is the one the club itself tells. In the year before he founded FC Barcelona he joined **Union Athlétique de Lyon**, the town's rugby club, and took to a code he had never played. The paper *Lyon Sport* noticed, in the club's English translation: "Although he had only played association football … he quickly learned the rules of rugby football, contributing to our first team his excellent dribbling qualities. His kicks and dribbling have placed him among the top players in the region." By the club's own account he had walked into a feud: the French rugby union of the day banned anyone who played association football from rugby for a year. Gamper was not a specialist of any kind. Besides football, he did athletics, cycling, rugby and golf.

The rugby may even be on the shirt. Where FC Barcelona's blue and maroon came from is not certain, and the club itself backs, as a theory, the idea that they came from the rugby team of **Merchant Taylors' School** in Crosby, outside Liverpool, which Arthur Witty and his brother attended. The school has confirmed that blue and maroon was its rugby strip in the late nineteenth century.

So FC Barcelona was founded by a man who had played rugby for a season, in a side where rugby was part of the education of several of its first players, and it tried the game out at the Bonanova in 1901. It chose football. Nothing forced that choice, and for the next decade the Spanish press explained why the country was right to leave rugby alone.

The game was not unknown in print. As early as **1894** a professor of gymnastics, **José Esteban García Fraguas**, had described and illustrated its rules in a Spanish treatise. In **1903** there was talk in Barcelona of two sides from **Toulouse** coming to play a match at the Hipódromo, and of the Barcelona, Español and Hispania clubs raising a team to meet them. The same year a columnist gave the view that would keep recurring: neither the Spanish temperament nor the game's effect on the body made it worth acclimatising here (*"ni por nuestro temperamento ni por las condiciones higiénicas de ese juego, no creemos que convenga que aquí se juegue mucho ni se trate de aclimatar el tal Rugby"*).

The news from America did not help. In 1904 the Spanish press was reporting the casualties of the American college game, whose injured were not always told apart from rugby's. One report ran under the heading "Victims of football" (*"Víctimas del foot-ball"*): a student had lost his reason "from the blows he had taken" (*"a consecuencia de los golpes recibidos"*). Rugby had a defender that year, a writer signing as Huso, who granted that it was brutal and argued for it anyway on the grounds of what it replaced. Better, he thought, that the young spent their holidays at it than in the café, at the gambling table, at the bullfight or out carousing (*"Creo que es preferible esta distracción a pasar los días festivos en el café, en la timba, en los toros o de juerga"*).

Six years later Barcelona's own city council tried to stage it. For the **Mercè festival of 1910** the council planned a match between two French sides, to show the city "for the first time in Spain this exciting and evocative game" (*"por primera vez en España este emocionante y sugestivo juego"*). It never took place. Why is not known.

The rugby Spaniards did get to watch was played by the British. That same year, 1910, two teams from the English squadron played at the ground of the **Real Club Fortuna** in **Vigo**, the first match of its kind in the region. The report was unimpressed: the small crowd had not liked the new sport, finding it inelegant and very violent (*"Al público escaso que presenció la lucha no le gustó el nuevo deporte, por lo poco elegante y muy violento"*). What the match was good for, the reporter decided, was admiring "the composure and cold blood of Britain's sons" (*"la serenidad y la sangre fría de los hijos de la Gran Bretaña"*).

On **12 and 13 February 1911**, at **Monelos park in A Coruña**, the officers of two Royal Navy warships, the *Liverpool* and the *Gloucester*, who had been playing football against the **Real Club Deportivo**, put on a rugby match. The Coruña paper's verdict was the one this whole period deserves: the new sport had failed to win over the public. "It is too rough and brutal" (*"Resulta demasiado fuerte y brutal"*).

The report travelled. In Barcelona it was retold as a game of "trips, wallops and other barbarities" (*"zancadillas, trastazos y demás barbaridades"*), at the very moment a rugby team was being planned in the city, and the objection it met was the old one from 1903: the game was not made for the Spanish temperament.

By 1911, then, Spain had seen rugby played by its own footballers, by British sailors in two Galician ports, and on paper for seventeen years. FC Barcelona had been founded by a rugby player and may have been wearing a rugby school's colours. What it did not have was anyone who wanted to carry the game, and every time the idea came up, the same verdict put it down.

### The Veterinarian from Toulouse

On **16 October 1911**, eight months after the Coruña verdict, the veterinary school at Toulouse enrolled a seventeen-year-old from Sant Boi de Llobregat. His matriculation sheet survives, made out to Baudilio Aleu, the Castilian form of his name. His father had encouraged him to go, and the reason was a practical one. In that era, as his club would later put it, anyone who wanted to study veterinary medicine properly had to study at Toulouse, "the nearest city, and the focus of culture in southern Europe" (*"la ciutat més propera i focus de cultura al sud d'Europa"*).

Nobody sent **Baldiri Aleu Torres** to Toulouse for rugby. He found it there because, in Toulouse, the veterinary school was one of the places the game had come from. In 1899 a player named Coulon had broken away from the students' club, the Stade Olympien des Étudiants de Toulouse, to found the **Union Sportive de l'École Vétérinaire**, which played in grey shirts and black shorts. In 1905 it joined the Sport Athlétique Toulousain to form Véto-Sport. In May 1907 Véto-Sport merged with the students' club Coulon had left, and in 1908 the combined club took a name that was, in the words of the vets' own rugby association, easier to digest: **Stade Toulousain**. The vets did not vanish into it. Octave Léry, François Moura, François-Xavier Dutour and Pierre Jauréguy all came from the school, and they starred when the Stade became champion of France for the first time, in 1912, while Aleu was in his first year there.

His club believes that is where his enthusiasm came from: from the university matches, and from a Toulouse side that had grown out of student groups and would be, by the end of the 1910s, "a model for all of France" (*"un referent a tot França"*). The club says "probably" (*segurament*), and that is as far as the record goes. The Catalan encyclopedia is plainer: at Toulouse he learned the rules.

He finished in 1913 and moved on to Paris, to the veterinary school at Alfort, then did his practical training in Normandy during the Great War. After that he went home to Sant Boi to practise as a vet, and brought the game with him.

What he did with it first was talk. The café **Cal Ninyu** was the centre of the town's social life, and Aleu worked on the regulars of its conversation circle (*tertúlia*), praising a sport that, as his club's history puts it, was unknown in the country until then. His recruits were not students. Sant Boi's municipal register gives their trades: peasants, bricklayers, carpenters, mechanics, shopkeepers and industrialists, young men "of every stratum and social condition" (*"de todos los estratos y condición social"*). The pitch, as the club remembered it fifty years later, was that this was a game in which intelligence counted for "much more … than brute force" (*"mucho más … que la fuerza bruta"*). A decade of Spanish newspapers had called rugby brutal. The first man to carry it in Spain sold it as the opposite.

Training began in the first months of 1921, on a piece of ground on the bank of the Llobregat that the players turned into the **Camp del Riu**, the river ground. By **18 April 1921** the group was in the Barcelona press. *La Veu de Catalunya* called on anyone who wanted to try the new sport to join the twenty-five members already signed up to the Unió Esportiva Santboiana.

The man who would teach them the technique arrived by letter. On **12 May 1921** **Michel Reynard** wrote to Aleu that "many French, English and Spanish sportsmen" (*"muchos sportmen franceses, ingleses y españoles"*) wanted to play the oval game; the letter survives in Santboiana's archive. Reynard was born at Arles in 1890 and worked in Barcelona as an engineer for the Energía Eléctrica de Cataluña. Three days after the letter the two men met. On **15 and 16 May** Union Sportive Perpignan and Racing Club de Narbonne played two exhibition matches in Barcelona for the victims of the Great War, which the club remembers as being at Espanyol's ground. Aleu and his friends came away, in the club's words, enthused (*entusiasmats*). By club tradition they also came away with their colour. Perpignan's second strip was the blue of the uniforms of the soldiers who had gone to the war and died there, and Sant Boi chose blue in their memory. That too the club offers as probable, not certain.

From that meeting Aleu and Reynard became the two men at the centre of the club's organisation, Reynard as player, coach and administrator. A tribute in *La Jornada Deportiva* in 1923 would call Reynard Aleu's "fellow student" (*"compañero de estudios"*), and his principal mentor in the technique of the game. The club's own papers have them meeting for the first time that May, in Barcelona.

The club was incorporated on **4 July 1921**. Its founding act gave its object as "the practice and spread of amateur sport, especially rugby" (*"la pràctica i difusió de l'esport amateur, especialment el Rugby"*), and its first minute book opens the same day. The sport historian Xavier Torrebadella-Flix, who has gone back through the period's press, calls it the first sporting association in the country given over entirely to rugby. In September it formally leased its field. Aleu would be its president until 1936, and the ground was later named after him.

What the club lacked was anyone to play against. Its first match came on **13 November 1921**, against the **Club Natació Atlètic**. *La Jornada Deportiva* wished the beginners the reward their effort deserved, and looked forward to the day when the Estadi Català would see the embrace of the two Unions: Perpignan's, the champions of France, and Sant Boi's (in the paper's own forms, the *Unión Sportiva Perpignaise* and the *Unión Sportiva Samboyana*). After that match Aleu wrote in *La Veu de Catalunya* himself, calling for new clubs to be formed, so that Sant Boi would have rivals and the game could take root in Catalonia and in Spain. Sant Boi remembers a manifesto of his in the same paper, "About Rugby Football" (*"Entorn el Futbol-Rugby"*), and dates it to 1922; whether that was the same article is not settled.

The clubs came, and the men who ran them had learned the game where Aleu had. **David Cuyás**, who built the rugby section of the **Club Natació Barcelona**, the third club in Catalonia, founded at the end of 1921, had been educated in France, and had learned there to play and to referee. The men who taught on the field were French as well. When the first competition was played, in the spring of 1922, Santboiana fielded Reynard, Vinçon and Dubois. The Natació Atlètic had Renaud and Hoche, the Natació Barcelona had Lafitte, and the Catalunya Sporting Club had Ducrós and Julien.

On **2 May 1922**, on the central ground of the old Barcelona hippodrome, Santboiana beat the Club Natació Barcelona 3–0 to win the **Copa Real Sociedad de Carreras**, the first rugby competition in Catalonia. The three points were a single try, not converted, scored by Dubois, who survives in every account only as a French surname. *La Jornada Deportiva* reported that Santboiana had taken the trophy brilliantly (*brillantemente*).

Reviewing the year for *El año deportivo*, Lluís Gibert singled out the thing that most needed saying: "the influence French players have had on our rugby" (*"la influencia que los jugadores franceses han tenido en nuestro rugby"*). The first Catalan players, he wrote, would remember Dubois, Julien, Reynard, Lafitte and Vinçon forever, "and especially Reynard and Vinçon were the masters" (*"y especialmente Reynard y Vençon fueron los maestros"*).

The game that British sailors had shown to Galicia, and that Barcelona's footballers had tried and put down, had come back into Spain over the Pyrenees, carried by a vet from a river town and a handful of Frenchmen. Barcelona, which had turned it down for twenty years, now had clubs of its own playing it.

### Nineteen Teams

On **20 and 21 May 1923** the Catalan federation closed its season at the hippodrome of **Can Tunis**, the racecourse built by the Compañía Francesa de Carreras de Caballos. The rugby clubs played a great many of their matches there, because they had so few proper grounds of their own. That weekend the visitors were **Toulouse Lalande Olympique**. On the first day they played Santboiana; on the second they played a **Catalan selection**, with Baldiri Aleu in its side, and won 9–0. *La Jornada Deportiva* ran the result the next day under a plain headline: in the match against the Catalan selection, Lalande had triumphed 9 to 0. Four days later its correspondent Delsol came back to the two matches at the hippodrome with some further thoughts. His point was that the French had seen how far the Catalans had come, and across the border *La Dépêche*, *La France* and *L'Auto* all covered the matches.

Two years before that weekend there had been one club, in Sant Boi. By then there was a federation calling itself Spanish, a Spanish championship, and more teams than anyone had space for.

The federation came first, and it came from a swimming club. It was the **Club Natació Barcelona** that put it to the others that rugby needed an association to look after the game's interests beyond any one club, and on **13 April 1922** the **Federación Española de Rugby** was constituted in Barcelona. Five clubs made it: the Natació Atlètic, the Natació Barcelona, Santboiana, the Catalunya Sporting and a side called Universitary. **David Cuyás**, the Natació Barcelona's rugby man, was its first secretary. By the end of August its presidency was with **Jaime Camino** of the Natació Atlètic. It was a national body in name. In fact it was five clubs from Barcelona and Sant Boi.

Clubs kept coming. By the end of 1922, Torrebadella counts thirteen: Santboiana, the Natació Atlètic, the Sociedad Deportiva de Sant Andreu, the Catalunya Athlètic, the Natació Barcelona, the Catalunya Sporting, the Barcelona Internacional Rugby Club, the rugby section of the **CADCI**, the Olimpic Rugby Club, the Universitary, the Faculties of **Medicine** and **Pharmacy**, and the Agrupació Atlètica Vida. The two faculties met on **18 December 1922** in what Torrebadella records as the first school match. The CADCI was something else again. The **Centre Autonomista de Dependents del Comerç i de la Indústria** had been founded in 1903 by shop and office clerks of Catalanist leanings, and its rugby committee announced itself publicly in the Centre's magazine, *Acció*, in January 1923. Almost every one of these sides had a Frenchman to teach it, and the French coached for nothing: the former player Laurent Etxeverry took the CADCI, a man named Grenier ran Santboiana's forwards, Vida had three former French players, and Michel Reynard, the most prominent of them, coached Santboiana and the Catalan and university selections.

A Catalan federation followed the Spanish one, in late 1922 or early 1923, with **Cuyás** as its first president. It was already organising a match in December 1922.

The first **Campeonato de España**, the championship of Spain, was played at the start of **January 1923**, organised by the Spanish federation, and only Catalan clubs entered it. There was no one else to enter. As Torrebadella puts it, the championship of Catalonia was the championship of Spain as well, since there were no teams outside the city. Santboiana won it, beating the Catalunya Athlètic 13–3 in the final, and the Spanish federation counts that as the first title in the history of what became the King's Cup. By February 1923 the Spanish federation listed six affiliated clubs: Santboiana, the two Natació clubs, Sant Andreu, the CADCI and the Olimpic. In two years, Torrebadella writes, Barcelona rugby had laid "the first institutional stone of Spanish rugby" (*"la primera piedra institucional del rugby español"*).

Whose game was it? Torrebadella's answer is a class one. In a city where football was turning proletarian and drifting toward a much-criticised professionalism, rugby answered to a different model, one found among young men "of the petite bourgeoisie and the liberal professions" (*"de la pequeña burguesía y de las profesionales liberales"*), and it stood as a counterpoint. Pasarello, Pujadas and Martín, historians who worked from Santboiana's archive, draw the pioneers the same way: young, white, from comfortable families, men who had found their passion for the game while studying in France, working alongside Frenchmen who were making their careers in Barcelona. The faculty sides and the clerks of the CADCI fit that picture. The peasants and bricklayers whom Aleu had signed up in the café at Sant Boi fit it less well, yet all of them played in the same competitions.

There was a politics to it too. After the Lliga Regionalista's campaign for an autonomy statute in 1918 and 1919, Torrebadella writes, Catalan sporting associations began to take on a Catalanist sign that within a few years had turned openly to the idea of independence, and in 1922 that sharper politics found two new parties, **Acció Catalana** and Francesc Macià's **Estat Català**. Rugby, in Pasarello, Pujadas and Martín's reading, took on a marked Catalanist tone from the start. They note that Perpignan, the French champions whose visit had enthused Aleu, carried the Catalan flag on their crest, and that this was valued as a symbol of pan-Catalanism. The CADCI's politics would in time cost it some of its sports sections, its first rugby team among them. The specialist press, meanwhile, *La Jornada Deportiva*, *El Mundo Deportivo*, *El Campeón*, *Stadium* and *L'Esport Català*, gave the new game its unconditional support.

It did not always get a game worth supporting. In April 1923 the Copa Real Sociedad de Carreras was played for a second time, and *Aire Libre*'s account of the final that spring was of a match in which the tension got to the players and there were moments of marked violence. The magazine's rugby writer, signing as "Drop-Goal", took it seriously. Brutal play, or at least violent play, had always been the greatest enemy of a strong and manly sport like rugby (*"El juego brutal, o cuando menos violento, ha sido siempre el mayor enemigo que ha tenido un deporte fuerte y viril como es el rugby"*), and if it went unchecked the city would see "the sport we love disappear from our sporting world" (*"desaparecer de nuestro mundo deportivo el deporte de nuestros amores"*). It was the old verdict of 1911, this time from the game's own side.

The weekend at Can Tunis closed that season, and the next one began at once. Six more clubs took up the game in 1923: the Unió Deportiva de Sants, the Club Deportiu Europa, the Helènic, a Català Rugby Club, and the two great football clubs of the city, the **Real Club Deportiu Espanyol** and **Fútbol Club Barcelona**, twenty-two years after its footballers had tried the game at the Bonanova and put it down. By the end of 1923, Torrebadella concludes, Barcelona had nineteen rugby teams, clubs or sections. In August Santboiana and the Natació Barcelona took the game to **Valencia** for two promotional matches, refereed by Cuyás, by then president of both the Catalan and the Spanish federations, and at the Tona town festival the Natació Barcelona played the CADCI. Not everyone liked the summer fixtures. In a piece in *La Veu de Catalunya* titled "An outrage against sportsmanship" (*"Un atentat de lesa esportivitat"*), the chairman of the CADCI's rugby committee, R. Sarsanedas, objected to playing in the middle of August. In the 1923–24 season the Catalan championship had seven teams in it, Espanyol among them.

By then the game had gathered in the best-known sporting clubs of Barcelona, which Torrebadella calls the most football-minded and sporting city in Spain, and those clubs had promised to spread it to other Spanish cities. The Spanish federation, its championship and nearly all of its players were still in Barcelona.

### The King at the Metropolitano

On **20 May 1923**, the Sunday on which Toulouse Lalande Olympique played Santboiana at Can Tunis, Madrid was shown the game. The match was staged at the **Stadium Metropolitano**, a ground so new it had only just opened, and it was played on grass, before about 10,000 people. The sides were French, **Biarritz Olympique** and the **Stadoceste Tarbais**, brought down to show the game, and the referee was the Spanish federation's president that spring, **Jaime Camino**. **Alfonso XIII** was there. The king was no stranger to the game; he had watched it several times in London and in the French Basque Country, and the following spring he would be photographed at Bordeaux, watching Stade Toulousain play Perpignan in the final of the French championship. Torrebadella calls him a very special spectator at the great rugby matches of those years, when he was going to more and more sporting events.

The match was promotion, and it was business too. The company that owned the Metropolitano had its own reasons for putting it on: it thought rugby might come to offer as much spectacle as football. The ground had been prepared for it by the press. On 29 April the Madrid daily *La Libertad* had announced a campaign to introduce the game (*"campaña de iniciación"*), with training sessions so that the first matches against Spanish teams could be played that autumn (*"para poder efectuar los primeros encuentros con equipos nacionales en el próximo otoño"*). Madrileños who wished to take part were warned that the game called for a complete physical development, and were asked to send their names in writing to Joaquín Álvarez Puebla.

More matches followed at the same ground on 31 May and on 2 and 3 June, and the king came to those as well. This time the Catalans were invited: the Natació Atlètic and the Natació Barcelona played, along with Biarritz and a Basque-French selection. The king's presence was what Madrid read about. It was reported prominently in the news dailies and in the illustrated and society magazines of the capital, and in Torrebadella's reading that gave Madrid rugby, from its first weeks, a marked distinction of social class. In Barcelona the game had been played by clerks, students, professional men and Sant Boi's bricklayers. In Madrid it arrived in the society pages.

Not every reviewer was won over. Some wrote that the game could never take root in Spain because of "the dominant impulsive character of the Spanish man" (*"el dominante carácter impulsivo del hombre español"*). Ten days after the first match, in *Vida financiera* of 30 May, Gómez Puig put his own doubts in writing: "this game demands an absolute command of the nerves" (*"Este juego requiere un dominio absoluto de los nervios"*), a firm equanimity, a constant thinking about the play that forgets what happened in the last movement before starting the next. Spanish players, he predicted, would end up at the police station before the match was over, "though not before giving a real show on the field" (*"no sin antes dar un verdadero espectáculo en el campo"*). Even so, he conceded, "Madrid's first rugby team is already being formed" (*"se está ya formando el primer equipo madrileño de rugby"*).

The French coast was sending the game in by a second road. On 24 June 1923, to open the Estadio de Berazubi at **Tolosa** in Guipúzcoa, players from Boucau, Biarritz, Bayonne and Hendaye put on an exhibition. At **San Mamés** in Bilbao, on 25 May 1924, the Association Sportive Bayonnaise played the Stade Hendayais in a benefit for the widow and daughter of the footballer Pichichi. Bilbao's writers were polite and unmoved: they loved football like nobody else, and did not believe rugby could ever interest them (*"somos amantes, como ninguno del fútbol; pero jamás creemos que pueda interesarnos el rugby"*), and most of the crowd at San Mamés, they added, agreed. Rugby, Torrebadella concludes, did not really begin in Basque sport until the 1930s.

In Madrid the game found a home in the football clubs, though not among their footballers. By the autumn of 1924 a team had formed of English enthusiasts and some "Britannicised Spaniards" (*"españoles 'britanizados'"*). At the Athletic Club the game was taught by a young Czech, Bereny. By 18 December 1924 the **Sociedad Gimnástica Española** had a team of its own, formed with the idea of "nationalising rugby" (*"nacionalizar el rugby"*), of making the game Spanish. By the end of that year four Castilian clubs had teams: **Real Madrid**, **Athletic de Madrid**, the Gimnástica, and the **Academia de Infantería** at Toledo. Real Madrid's rugby side, which went by the name of Britania R.F.C., played its first match, an exhibition, at the Metropolitano on 10 January 1925, and beat the Athletic 27–0, thanks to the play of its foreign members. It was Real Madrid's in name only, Torrebadella notes: most of its players were foreigners.

The Toledo side came out of the army. Outside Catalonia, the first Spaniards to try the game for themselves were at the **Escuela Central de Gimnasia**, the army's central gymnastics school at Toledo, which ran from 1919 to 1936 and was attached to the infantry academy there until 1930. The academy's cadets played the Athletic Club de Madrid in their first match, beat them, and proclaimed themselves champions of the Centro, as *Armas y Deportes* reported in 1925. The school took the game seriously enough to print its own rulebook, *Foot-ball Rugby: Reglamento*, at Toledo in 1927.

Madrid now had clubs, and it wanted a body of its own. On 7 December 1925 delegates of the Gimnástica and the Agrupación Deportiva Ferroviaria, the railwaymen's sports club, met at the Gimnástica's rooms, with the Catalan referee Carmelo Davalillos Artigas present, and founded the **Federación de Centro de Rugby**. Its president was Ángel Leoz Cendoya, president of the Ferroviaria. Its technical director was **Dr Enrique Gutiérrez**, a former captain of the French national side and a member of the French selection committee. The Spanish federation's own history describes the new body as meeting the needs of the clubs of the centre of the peninsula. Early in 1926, as the *Heraldo de Madrid* reported, **Baldiri Aleu** was named president of the Spanish federation and its seat fixed in Barcelona. At the end of March the whole committee of the Centro federation resigned, and its new president was Fernando López-Quesada y Bourbón, a former Real Madrid footballer. That year the Centro federation also made **Captain José Hermosa Gutiérrez**, a sports teacher at the gymnastics school in Toledo, president of its referees' committee.

On the evening of Thursday **13 May 1926**, at Sant Boi de Llobregat, the Toledo cadets, champions of Castile, met **FC Barcelona**, champions of Catalonia, in the final of the Campeonato de España. It was the federation's second championship, the first since January 1923, and the first final in which a side from outside Catalonia played. David Cuyás of the Natació Barcelona refereed, and Barcelona won 19–0. In Barcelona it was read as more than a match. *L'Esport Català* of 17 May wrote of a team that "united the aspirations of all Catalans around a sporting supremacy" (*"hermanaba las aspiraciones de todos los catalanes alrededor de una supremacía deportiva"*). For Torrebadella it was a symbolic triumph for Catalan nationalism: behind FC Barcelona's victory stood a victory for Catalonia.

The cadets did not play on. After their success, as Hermosa told it in *Gran Sport* in October 1930, the unexpected happened, and the team was dissolved. Some of the academy's superiors and professors considered rugby "barbarous" (*bárbaro*), "though there had not been a single serious injury" (*"a pesar de no haber ocurrido ni una lesión de importancia"*). "But those gentlemen," Hermosa wrote, "thought of rugby what was said of football twenty years ago" (*"pero esos señores pensaban del Rugby lo mismo que hace veinte años se decía del fútbol"*).

The break between the two cities came over a match with France. On **19 February 1927**, after disagreements over organising a Spain–France fixture, the Centro federation separated from the Spanish federation and set up the **Rugby Unión Española**, organised and presided over by Gutiérrez with the backing of Manuel Ordóñez, and it meant to ask for the title of Royal (*Real*). The Madrid clubs' own argument, in the federation's later history, was that they needed more attention. Barcelona's press saw it otherwise. It accused Madrid's federation men of doing nothing to spread the game and pursuing only their private interests, of going about "sowing rancour and intrigue" (*"sembrando rencores e intrigas"*). The planned match, against a French side far stronger in body and in technique, was a reckless escapade (*calaverada*), an unnecessary display of French power, a disaster (*descalabro*) that would ruin everything Spanish rugby had achieved.

The match went ahead at the Metropolitano on **26 May 1927**. The Spanish side was picked by the Centro federation alone; the Spanish federation's own history counts a single player from the Catalan federation among them. It had no sanction from the Spanish federation. The pro-government *La Nación*, the daily that served Primo de Rivera, regretted that for reasons of his diary, "this first international match cannot be presided over, as it otherwise would have been, by the first sportsman of Spain" (*"este primer encuentro internacional no podrá ser presidido, como lo hubiera sido en otro caso, por el primer deportista español"*). It meant the king. France won 66–6. The Madrid press still called it the first official match of the Spanish national side, and the criticism of the "Spanish selection" (*"Selección española"*), Torrebadella writes, was unsparing.

Torrebadella reads the whole quarrel as politics carried on in sport. The rivalry between Barcelona and Madrid, he writes, lay beyond sport itself, and rugby reflected the schism between two nationalisms. In his account Primo de Rivera's dictatorship obstructed Catalan rugby and supported Castilian rugby, and the Castilian leaders who formed the Centro federation meant to sustain the dictatorship's ideological frame: "the Castilian Spanishisation of rugby" (*"la españolización castellana del rugby"*), which required disarming the power of a federation held mainly by Catalan players. Captain Hermosa, as a sports teacher at the army school, was in his reading one of those charged with the nationalisation of sport.

Aleu, unable even to attempt a mediation, resigned. In **January 1928** a new Spanish federation was constituted in Madrid, merging the directorates of the Rugby Unión Española and the old federation, with Enrique Gutiérrez as its president. It kept the old name, Federación Española de Rugby, and its seat moved from Barcelona to Madrid. The reconciliation was sealed at the Metropolitano, with a match for the Copa Lord Derward between selections of Castile and Catalonia. The Catalans won 17–0. The quarrel had cost the championship three seasons: the Campeonato de España was not played from 1926–27 to 1928–29, and when it came back in 1929–30, FC Barcelona beat Real Madrid 39–3 in the final. Five years after the king first watched it on the Metropolitano's grass, the game Barcelona had made was run from Madrid.

### What this generation leaves behind

Spanish rugby came out of its first generation marked by the road it had taken in. British sailors had played it in two Galician ports and been told it was too rough. Barcelona's footballers had tried it and chosen football. It took root only when it came back over the Pyrenees with a vet from Sant Boi, and Frenchmen taught it for nothing. Even the man now at the head of the federation had captained France.

What the generation built was a federation with two homes, and a quarrel that each city told its own way. Five clubs had founded it in Barcelona and called it Spanish from its first day. Madrid's clubs had broken away from it saying they needed more attention, and one of them had formed its team to make the game Spanish. Barcelona's press called the Madrid men intriguers, and wrote of FC Barcelona's 1926 champions as a team that had united the aspirations of all Catalans.

The settlement of January 1928 did not end the quarrel. The federation's own list of its presidents has Enrique Gutiérrez at its head, from Madrid, in 1928, and Baldiri Aleu back in the chair, from Barcelona, in 1929. For the next few years the two cities would pass the federation back and forth. Spanish rugby still had to decide whether it belonged to the city that had made it or to the capital that now ran it.

## Generation 1: Barcelona and Madrid (1928–1936)

**20 May 1929.** The Italians came out first, carrying a large Spanish flag. Behind them came the Spaniards with the flag of Italy. At the new stadium on Montjuïc, Barcelona's municipal band played the two national anthems, and the teams exchanged bouquets of flowers. Rugby was opening the ground. Football would follow, the Catalan selection against Bolton Wanderers, but the first match belonged to these two fifteens.

The flag in the Italians' hands named the home side; the city around the stadium supplied its stage. Madrid had taken the federation's office in the settlement of 1928. Now Barcelona was hosting the first senior home international played with that federation's sanction. Taking the office was one way to claim the national game; staging its side was another. At Montjuïc, Barcelona had the first place on the programme.

That division between the office and the field had been made possible by a settlement one year earlier.

### The Settlement

On the morning of **Easter Sunday, 8 April 1928**, the Côte d'Argent committee, acting for the French federation, gave a reception in the salons of the Hôtel de Bordeaux for the visitors from Spain, the French players and the officials. The Spanish consul raised a glass, and so did the president of the Spanish federation, **Dr Enrique Gutiérrez**. They drank to Franco-Spanish friendship, to "the king of sports" (*"au sport-roi"*), and to the greater growth of Spanish rugby. Gutiérrez was among people who knew him. He had captained France, and *La Petite Gironde* called him an old boy of the Bordeaux-Étudiants-Club (*"un ancien du Bordeaux-Étudiants-Club"*). Three months after the settlement that had made him president, he had brought a Spanish side to his old club's city.

The settlement had moved the federation's seat to Madrid and put a Madrid man at its head. The side it sent to Bordeaux came from Barcelona. Three days before the match Manuel Ordóñez, who had backed Gutiérrez's breakaway the year before, previewed the trip in *Heraldo Deportivo*. His headline was about another match, the reconciliation at the Metropolitano in March: "The Catalan selection beats the Madrid one by 17 points to 0" (*"La Selección catalana vence a la madrileña 17 puntos a 0"*). The national side came further down. "We can state," he wrote, "that the Spanish national selection that will represent us on 8 April at Bordeaux will be made up of all the Catalans, with the Madrid man Vázquez included in the side" (*"Podemos asegurar que la selección nacional española que nos representará el 8 de abril en Burdeos estará formada por la totalidad de los catalanes, incluyéndose en la formación al madrileño Vázquez"*).

A year earlier it had been the other way round. The side Madrid picked for the Metropolitano in May 1927, the federation's history records, was all from the Centro federation, with only one player from the Catalan federation. Now the Catalans made up the side, and Madrid had one man in it. The federation's register of capped players shows who went. Thirteen men won their first cap on 8 April 1928: José Aguilar Cortiella, Domingo Aiximeno Moyano, Rafael Artigas, José Baides Facios, Vicente Bisbal Climent, Juan Blanquet Fondevilla, Amadeo Blasco Yus, Fernando Carreras Parset, Jaime Garrigosa Julia, Manuel Isart Subirach, Rafael Rues, Eduardo Ruiz Méndez, and the one Madrid man, **Ricardo Vázquez Granjell**. Spain had played only one Test before, so the other two of the fifteen had played in the 66–6 of 1927. The register does not say which two.

The Madrid press had called the 1927 match the first official match of the Spanish national side. When the federation came to write its own history, it gave the title to Bordeaux instead: "First official match of the Spanish men's national side, 1928" (*"Primer partido oficial de la Selección española masculina – 1928"*). So Spanish rugby ended up with two first official matches, a year apart. The first was picked in Madrid without the federation's sanction. The second had the sanction of a federation now seated in Madrid, and was played by Barcelona's men.

The opponent was not France, as in 1927, but its second string. *La Petite Gironde* described "a France B team made up of our international hopefuls" (*"une équipe de France B composée de nos espoirs internationaux"*). They played in the afternoon at the Parc des Sports at Lescure, in ideal weather, before 10,000 people. The paper thought that a very big crowd for Easter, when the townspeople usually left the city. France B won by 53 points to 5, with thirteen tries, five goals and a drop goal. Spain defended well, for a time with fourteen men after Aiximeno left the field. Their one score came with France 45 points up. A Spanish dribbling rush broke through the French defence, and the captain, Blanquet, scored the try and converted it himself. It was "wildly applauded" (*"follement applaudi"*).

What was certain, the paper's critic wrote, was that "the Spanish players have been brought up in a good school. They know only what is good and attractive in rugby" (*"les joueurs espagnols sont élevés à bonne école. Ils ne connaissent du rugby que ce qu'il contient de bon, de joli"*). They had been taught to open the play and keep the ball moving, and kept away from the tricks and the dirty play that France's best players could not resist. "So they opened their eyes wide in astonishment when they found themselves jostled in the line-outs" (*"C'est ainsi qu'ils ouvraient de grands yeux étonnés lorsqu'ils se voyaient bousculés dans les touches"*). Only Blanquet, the captain, had a thorough knowledge of the game, and he had played in leading French sides. The forwards had the most to learn: "rarely were all eight of them together to go into a scrum" (*"rarement ils se trouvèrent les huit pour se mettre en mêlée"*). Behind the scrum, Vázquez on the wing and Bisbal in the centre could not be shaken off by the men opposite them, and Garrigosa and Blanquet, taking turns at full-back, tackled well. The Spaniards had a great deal to learn, the critic concluded, "but in the unanimous opinion of those who saw them last year, they have made great progress" (*"mais de l'avis unanime de ceux qui les ont vus l'an passé, ils sont très en progrès"*). The side seen the year before had been Madrid's. Thirteen of the men at Lescure had never played for Spain.

The match report was signed by G. Chappert, and he placed the visitors among the game's new arrivals: "like the Italians, the Spaniards, newcomers to rugby" (*"comme les Italiens, les Espagnols, nouveaux venus au rugby"*). He also wrote that the subjects of Alfonso XIII had played the game for only two years. They had played it for longer, since Santboiana was founded in 1921. He claimed a kinship too. The Spaniards' qualities, he wrote, were those "we find in our Basques and our Catalans, still closer brothers of the Iberian Basques and Catalans" (*"que nous trouvons chez nos Basques et nos Catalans, frères encore plus proches des Basques et des Catalans ibériques"*). The game had reached Barcelona over the Pyrenees, and Chappert saw the tie running back the same way: the Spaniards in front of him were kin to France's own Catalans.

The settlement's first season gave half an answer to the question of who owned Spanish rugby. The federation's office sat in Madrid, under a president from Madrid, and its national side was Barcelona's. On the ground the two cities were close to level: by the end of 1928, Torrebadella counts six established rugby clubs in Barcelona and five in Madrid. The only Test Spain had played at home was still Madrid's, the 66–6 against France that the federation had never sanctioned.

### The Exposition Match

Barcelona's turn came with a different opponent and a much larger occasion. The International Exposition opened on 19 May 1929; the following day, Spain and Italy inaugurated the Montjuïc stadium. The FER's ledger would put the match on the 19th too, but the contemporary programme and the account published in Barcelona on 21 May place it on **20 May**, as does the Italian federation. The opening of the Exposition and the opening of its stadium were a day apart.

Behind the afternoon stood the Exposition's organising committee. It had created a commission for games and sports, chaired by **Mariano de Foronda**, to arrange the sporting programme and oversee the facilities, working with the Catalan sports federations. Rugby had a place within that programme and, at Montjuïc, the first place. Barcelona was able to put the national side inside a public occasion much bigger than the game itself. The work of staging an international extended beyond choosing fifteen men: here it included the organisation that could give them a stadium and an audience.

That audience needs its proper name. *La Vanguardia* estimated **more than 60,000** people at the inauguration, an enormous setting for the rugby match. But the crowd had chiefly come for the football that followed. *La Veu de Catalunya* made the distinction bluntly: "The people did not go to the stadium for rugby" (*"La gent no anà pas a l'Estadi pel rugby"*). The Catalan football selection's meeting with Bolton Wanderers was the principal attraction. Rugby's place at the front of the programme put it before that public; it did not turn the public into rugby followers. The opportunity was real without requiring sixty thousand converts.

Across from Spain stood another national game beginning to organise itself. On **20 September 1928**, the new **Federazione Italiana Rugby** had elected **Giorgio Vaccaro** president. Its national side was playing the first Test in its history. Its first international opponent was Spain, on Barcelona's new ground, in the opening event of the stadium programme.

For Spain, the distinction was the federation's sanction. Madrid had already staged an international, but the FER had not authorised its side. Barcelona was staging the **first FER-sanctioned home senior international**. That was a first in the Spanish federation's chronology; "Test" could imply the narrower standard of full caps recognised by both countries, a separate question from the FER's approval. Italy counted this match as its first Test. Spain came to it with the two defeats, in Madrid and Bordeaux, behind it, and no international victory.

Spain's fifteen included Bisbal and Garrigosa, Isart and Baides, and Aguilar. The last three would supply the pass and two of the tries that decided the match. Between the teams stood a French referee, **M. Brutus**. After the band's anthems and the exchange of flowers, the ceremony gave way to the differences between the sides.

The Italians brought the greater physical weight. *La Vanguardia* described them as more athletic, able to impose themselves through their size; the Spaniards answered with greater skill, speed and knowledge of the game. That was the contest within the occasion. Spain's advantage, as the Barcelona account saw it, lay in how its players used the ball and how quickly they played. The difference would become visible in the first score.

The ceremony was not yet over. About fifteen minutes into the match, the Spanish monarchs arrived at the stadium. Play stopped for the royal anthem. The rugby had begun before the royal arrival; now the inauguration briefly took precedence over the game that was opening it. The pause belonged to the same afternoon as the scrums and the scoring, a reminder of the occasion into which this small international fixture had been placed. Then the players could resume it.

Spain's first-half try came from a scrum. **Baides** took a pass from **Isart** as the ball came out and scored. The conversion failed. The movement gave the Spanish advantage a pair of names: Isart supplying the ball, Baides finishing, three points that the Italians' greater weight had not prevented. Spain had the lead, though no points from the kick to enlarge it. For all the size of the stadium and the inauguration around them, the difference between the teams was still one try.

In the second half **Pedraja** scored the next. Again the conversion failed. Then **Aguilar** scored Spain's third try and attempted the conversion himself. This time the ball went through the posts, but an Italian had touched it, and Brutus disallowed the conversion. The kick supplied no points. All three Spanish tries stood unconverted, and Italy had scored nothing: **Spain 9, Italy 0**. Barcelona had staged the sanctioned home international and, with it, Spain's first win.

The return carried the two sides to **the Arena in Milan, on 29 May 1930**. There Italy won **3–0**. It was the Italian national side's second Test and its first victory. These were the countries' first two meetings: each had won at home, each had kept the visitor scoreless, and each had taken its first international win from the other. The nine points at Montjuïc had given Spain a beginning, not a settled superiority. A year later the result ran the other way.

Barcelona nevertheless kept what the inauguration had given it. The national side's first victory had happened there, under the Spanish name, as part of an occasion the city had assembled. Madrid's claim through the federation's office now had that public achievement beside it. Winning a match could put Spanish rugby before a crowd; it could not decide where the federation belonged. Barcelona had staged the national side. The question of who would speak for it, and from which city, remained.

### Who Spoke for Spain

**5 January 1934.** Joan Boix was waiting for Francesc Massana to return from Paris. The Catalan federation had been invited to the assembly constituting a new international rugby body, FIRA; Massana had gone to represent it, carrying instructions and the powers to act. News of Catalonia's acceptance had reached Barcelona before he did. In the interview published that day by *El Mundo Deportivo*, Boix, the Catalan federation's president, was asked whether the outcome had surprised him. “No, it has not surprised me.”

The news had caused a sensation, he said. But he would go only so far: “Until Massana returns, I can say nothing.” There was satisfaction in the interview, and a limit to what its speaker would claim. Catalan rugby had obtained recognition abroad while the authority of the Spanish federation was still being disputed at home.

The Republic had given that argument a more explicit political language. Catalan again became the Catalan federation's official language; the president of the Generalitat became its honorary president; the federation supported the Catalan Statute project. These were acts of the institution itself. The body that organised the game was also declaring the community it understood itself to represent.

The Spanish presidency, meanwhile, was moving the other way. José María Vilar, listed by the FER as a Barcelona president, held it from 1931 to 1933. Francisco Martínez Larrañaga, of Madrid, followed in 1933; Ambrosio Ristori de la Cuadra in 1935; Luis García San Miguel in 1936. All three successors were listed under Madrid. **From 1933, the presidential sequence belonged to the capital.**

The federation's address was less easily settled. The city beside a president's name did not necessarily tell anyone where the office stood. In late 1933, the Castilian federation refused to recognise a FER headquarters in Barcelona. One board resigned; another resigned in late December, leaving the federation without settled government until an assembly in late January 1934. This was the domestic background to Boix's interview. The national organisation was struggling to establish who could act for it at precisely the moment a new international organisation was accepting members.

On the field, Madrid's challenge had become harder to dismiss. Real Madrid lost three successive national finals to Catalan clubs: **12–6 to Santboiana in 1930–31, 20–3 to FC Barcelona in 1931–32, and 24–6 to Santboiana in 1932–33**. Then the sequence broke. In 1933–34 Real Madrid beat Valencia's Federación Universitaria Escolar **14–6** to take the championship. The Valencian students reached the final again in 1934–35 and lost **11–3** to the Real Sociedad Gimnástica Española. The last two prewar titles had gone beyond the Catalan clubs, and the side contesting both finals came from a third city. Barcelona against Madrid no longer described the whole competition.

Madrid also had places where a player could enter rugby without beginning at Real Madrid. Arquitectura's club history traced its team to fifteen architecture students who organised in 1931. A photograph recorded their first match against Medicina, at the old Gimnástica ground on Diego de León, in January 1932. Industriales, Agronomía and Derecho appeared among their early opponents. Faculties supplied teams and a circle of opponents: the university could carry the game from one intake of students to another. Military rugby had changed its carrier too. The Toledo Infantry Academy side had disappeared early; a distinct aviation team at Cuatro Vientos entered Madrid's civilian championship in 1935.

Further north, the evidence was still of a game being proposed. A Club Deportivo de Bilbao history reproduced an *Excelsior* discussion of **30 January 1936** asking whether a rugby team was being formed at the club, and urging that the sport should begin in Vizcaya. That was an invitation to build something. It did not yet establish a Basque club capable of entering the contest already being played by the Catalan, Madrid and Valencian sides.

Abroad, the opening came through France. Expelled from the Five Nations in 1931, France was outside a tournament that reverted to the four Home Nations from 1932. A continental federation offered another framework for the game. But even FIRA's beginning acquired competing dates. The Italian federation's history placed its constitution in **Turin in 1933**. A study based on the period press and federation records described a failed attempt in September 1933 and a creation meeting in **Paris on 2 January 1934**. Rugby Europe, FIRA's successor, gave **20 April 1934 in Paris** as its legal foundation in its 2022 bylaws. These might record stages in one foundation, but the accounts did not establish that reconciliation. There was no single uncontested birthday.

Boix's interview belonged to the January meeting's immediate aftermath. The archival study recorded Catalonia's acceptance as an autonomous federation with full rights, confirmed at Hanover in March. The FER's own account supplied the other half of the story: its reconstituted board had not received its powers in time to attend the Paris meeting. The Catalan federation had its paperwork ready, and sponsored the Spanish federation's admission. According to the Catalan federation's history, **Catalonia held full membership; the FER entered as an affiliated federation**. The national body had gained entry with the help of the regional body whose separate standing would become a dispute in its own right.

That was more complicated than a Spanish seat passing between two cities. Both bodies had a place, on different terms. It also sat awkwardly beside Rugby Europe's later legal account: the 2022 bylaws named Spain among ten founding national unions that had participated in either of two constituent meetings in 1934, and did not name Catalonia. That classification and the historical accounts of membership were not interchangeable. Nor did the accounts settle the exact date or instrument of the FER's accession, or its voting terms. What they did establish was Catalonia's separate presence and its part in bringing the FER into the organisation.

The distinction mattered beyond the founding meetings. In 1936 the Spanish federation sought Catalonia's expulsion from FIRA. FIRA rejected the petition. Madrid's hold on the national presidency had not given it sole possession of international representation; Catalonia's standing had survived an attempt by the national body to remove it.

Alongside that argument, the Spanish side had a small, uneven run of matches. The FER's senior men's record listed four against **French Morocco** in 1931–32, all as friendlies. Spain won **16–14 in Rabat on 25 December 1931**, then drew **10–10** there three days later. French Morocco won the return in Madrid **14–8 on 16 April 1932**, and beat Spain **14–0 in Rabat on 25 December**. This was the colonial opponent named in the register, not France's senior fifteen or the later independent Moroccan national side.

Then came Portugal. At the **Campo das Amoreiras in Lisbon, on 13 April 1935**, Spain won **6–5** against a Portuguese side captained by Francisco Xavier Araújo. Portugal's federation counted it as its national team's first official match. On **28 March 1936**, the two met in Madrid and Spain won again, **16–9**. The following day's *La Voz* in Almería carried the result in a short notice from Madrid. The score travelled; the notice supplied little of the match around it.

That March result was the last prewar entry identified in the FER's opponent-by-opponent senior record. The next identified match lay fifteen years away: **Italy 12–0 Spain, Rome, 6 May 1951**. It was a silence in the national side's record, not proof that every club ceased playing throughout the intervening years.

In Barcelona, there was still rugby on a programme for the summer of 1936. The People's Olympiad was scheduled for **19–26 July**. Its surviving programme listed rugby among the sports, alongside football, boxing, rowing and others. It preserved an intended place for the game, without establishing which rugby teams would have met. The military uprising prevented the Olympiad from taking place. The rugby remained on the programme; there would be no result to put beside it.

### What this generation leaves behind

By July 1936, Spanish rugby had answered the question of who owned it without resolving the argument. **Madrid held the presidency.** **Barcelona had staged the first sanctioned home international and the first victory.** The Catalan federation had entered FIRA in its own right and sponsored the Spanish federation's admission; when the FER later tried to have it expelled, FIRA refused. The national game had not passed cleanly from one city to the other. Its authority had been divided among institutions that claimed different powers.

The field had widened too. Catalan clubs won the first three championships of the decade, then Real Madrid and the Gimnástica took the last two. Valencia's students reached both of those finals. Madrid's university faculties supplied teams and opponents beyond the great football clubs, while Bilbao was still discussing whether a side might be formed. The two-city struggle had built a game that no longer fitted completely inside two cities.

It had also built a national side, though not a continuous one. Spain had played in Bordeaux and Barcelona, Rabat, Lisbon and Madrid; it had beaten Italy once, French Morocco once and Portugal twice. Its last prewar result was the victory over Portugal in March 1936. The next match identified in the federation's record would not come until 1951.

The last image of the generation is therefore not another international but an empty line on a programme. Rugby was listed for the People's Olympiad in Barcelona in July 1936. No teams can be securely named, and no score exists. The military uprising stopped the Olympiad before it could begin. Spanish rugby entered its next generation not with the argument between Barcelona and Madrid settled, but with the game itself interrupted.

## Generation 2: The Generalísimo's Cup (1936–1965)

**12 October 1943.** The visit to the Faculty of Chemical Sciences ended at five in the afternoon, and the Head of State went on to the School of Architecture. It was the Día de la Hispanidad, and Madrid's university city was being inaugurated, building by building: Philosophy and Letters, Pharmacy, Chemical Sciences, Architecture, the sports grounds, the School of Agronomists. In the war the campus had been a front line. That morning a flag flew at half-mast over the Hospital Clínico, in mourning, the next morning's papers said, "for the fallen in that historic place" (*"por los caídos en aquél histórico lugar"*).

From Architecture the cars went to the sports ground. "On the bridge that separates the football pitch from the rugby pitch," ran the agency report they printed, "the Caudillo stepped down from his car and went on foot to the stand set up for the occasion, amid the deafening applause and cheers of the crowd that filled the terraces and ringed the field" (*"En el puente que separa el campo de fútbol del de rugby, el Caudillo descendió de su coche y se dirigió a pie a la tribuna instalada al efecto, entre los aplausos y vítores ensordecedores de la multitud que llenaba las gradas y circundaba el campo"*). The crowd closed round him. He took his seat with the Minister of Education and the national chief of the **Sindicato Español Universitario**, the Falange's student union, and "watched a phase of the rugby match that was being played" (*"presenció una fase del partido de rugby que se estaba disputando"*) between a side from Barcelona and the **S.E.U. de Madrid**. The report gives no score. From the stand he went on to a hall of residence, the Colegio Mayor Jiménez de Cisneros, and at a quarter to seven he left the campus.

A dictator in the stand, his party's students on the pitch, a Barcelona fifteen opposite them, and a college that would one day win the cup: the afternoon held most of what the next two decades would be made of. To see how a game that Barcelona had built came to be played there, in front of him, go back to the summer in which the Olympiad was not held.

### The Party's Game

The war stopped the game where it stood. The federation's championship, last won by the Gimnástica in 1935, had not been played in 1935–36, and the war kept it away for four seasons more. The national side, which had beaten Portugal in Madrid in March 1936, did not take the field. The federation's own history gives the three years a single sentence: the Civil War "razed the advances that had been made in rugby" (*"arrasó con los avances que se habían dado en el rugby"*). Its list of presidents records a Madrid man, **Luis García San Miguel**, against the year 1936, and another, **Juan López de Chicheri**, against 1939, and nothing in between.

What the war cost can be seen best in one small club. Madrid's School of Architecture had won the last two university championships before 1936. When its students came back, the club's history says, "many had disappeared in the war or in exile and would never return to the team" (*"Muchos habían desaparecido en la guerra o en el exilio y nunca volverían al equipo"*). It names one of them, the architect Félix Candela. The man who put the side together again, Manuel López-Mateos, captained it for several seasons, and the club remembers how he did it: "The beginnings were hard, recruiting players classroom by classroom" (*"Los inicios fueron duros, reclutando jugadores aula por aula"*).

He did it under a new roof. "After the Civil War," the same history says, "the competition resumed under the protection of the Sindicato Español Universitario" (*"Tras la Guerra Civil, la competición se reanudó al amparo del Sindicato Español Universitario"*). The S.E.U. had been tied to the Falange since its founding in 1933, and a decree of 21 April 1937, in the middle of the war, had refounded it. A history of the university's ground records that the rebuilding of the sports fields began in November 1940, encouraged by the S.E.U.; that the union held its first University Games there on 20 April 1942; and that people called the place "the S.E.U.'s sports fields" (*"campos de deporte del SEU"*). Readers of the Italy chapter have met the arrangement before: a single party's university groups, and a game played in their colours.

The party did not stop at the students. A decree of **22 February 1941**, published in the state gazette on 5 March, created the Delegación Nacional de Deportes of the Falange. Its first article was one sentence: "The direction and promotion of Spanish sport is entrusted to Falange Española Tradicionalista y de las J.O.N.S." (*"La dirección y el fomento del deporte español se encomienda a Falange Española Tradicionalista y de las J. O. N. S."*). Its preamble promised to work with what it found, "conserving all that is healthy and usable in the initiative of associations that have a brilliant history of service to Spanish sport" (*"conservando cuanto hay de sano y aprovechable en la iniciativa de Agrupaciones que cuentan con una brillante historia de servicios al deporte español"*). Its fourth article set out the terms. The Delegación would "appoint the Presidents and Vice-presidents of the national sporting Federations" (*"Nombrar los Presidentes y Vicepresidentes de las Federaciones deportivas nacionales"*), approve the statutes and regulations of federations and clubs, and approve and correct their fixture lists.

In the generation before, Barcelona and Madrid had passed the federation's presidency back and forth and argued over who had the right to it. Now the question had an answer that neither city had given. The federation's list had been Madrid's since 1933, and it stayed Madrid's to the end of this generation. What changed was who held the power to name the man: the party's delegation. The list does not say how any of them was chosen.

Four months after the decree the championship came back, under a new title. The federation's roll calls it the ***Campeonato de S.E. el Generalísimo***, His Excellency the Generalísimo's Championship, and it kept the name until the season of 1975–76. The first final was played in Madrid, at the ground in Vallecas, on the weekend of the football cup final of **29 June 1941**. *La Vanguardia*'s man in the capital, writing about the football at Chamartín, where twenty-three thousand were expected, passed on a Madrid paper's advice to those who could not get in: they could make up for it "by going to watch the rugby championship at Vallecas, between the teams of Español and Madrid's S.E.U." (*"yendo a presenciar el campeonato de rugby en Vallecas, entre los equipos del «Español» y el madrileño del S.E.U."*). Espanyol of Barcelona, Español in the press of those years, had a side in both finals. Valencia beat its footballers. The students beat its rugby players, **14–10**. The match itself survives only as a line in the roll: the first cup played under Franco's name was won by the Falange's student union.

The union's fifteens were not only Madrid's. In April 1941 *La Vanguardia*'s results column had an S.E.U. side leading the Catalan championship. An S.E.U. de León reached the national final of 1947–48. In Valladolid, a local paper recalled long afterwards, "the great flame" of the city's rugby "was lit by the S.E.U. Valladolid" (*"la gran llama del rugby pucelano fue encendida por el SEU Valladolid"*), in matches against sides from Zaragoza and Madrid. And the union was not the party's only team. When FC Barcelona played their semi-final in April 1946, the club's history records, the side they met was the Frente de Juventudes de Madrid, the Falange's youth front.

So by the afternoon Franco stepped out of his car, the regime had everything in Spanish rugby that a decree could give it. It held the power to name the president. Its leader's title was on the cup. Its students held the university ground, and had won the first final. What it did not have was a side that could keep winning. In the six finals that followed its first win the S.E.U. de Madrid appeared five times, each time against the same club.

### Barcelona Keeps the Cup

**5 May 1946.** The final was at Les Corts, FC Barcelona's ground, and it kicked off at six on a Sunday evening, after the two captains had exchanged the customary gifts. Among those present, *La Vanguardia* reported, were "the national directors of rugby, señores Chicheri, Blanco and Marín" and, listed after them, "those of the Catalan", señores Sentís, Císcar, Pascual and Baltasar (*"los directivos nacionales de Rugby señores Chicheri, Blanco y Marín, los de la Catalana señores Sentís, Císcar, Pascual y Baltasar"*). With them was the president of the host club, José Vendrell.

Bella scored for Barcelona after eight minutes. Olascuaga replied for the S.E.U. de Madrid and Arrechea converted, and for a while the students led 5–3. García and Homs put Barcelona back in front before half-time. After it Casas kicked a penalty, the S.E.U. scored twice more through Gamazo and Arrechea, and Casas scored twice himself. It finished **23–13**. The paper's reporter had expected Barcelona to win. What surprised him, he wrote, was "the quality of the good play, the sportsmanship and the gentlemanliness that both fifteens showed for the eighty minutes" (*"la calidad de buen juego, deportividad y caballerosidad que desarrollaron ambos «quinces» durante los ochenta minutos"*). And he noted what the result meant. Barcelona had the title "for the third time running, for which reason the Trophy of the Head of State is awarded to them outright" (*"por tercera vez consecutiva por lo que se adjudica en propiedad el Trofeo del Jefe del Estado"*). The cup that carried Franco's title was Barcelona's to keep.

Six years earlier it had not been obvious there would be a Catalan game to win it. *La Vanguardia*, previewing a fixture in the first week of **January 1940**, wrote of "the forced inactivity of three years" (*"la forzosa inactividad de tres años"*). The fixture was the game's return: two matches at Les Corts, on 6 and 7 January, between a Barcelona selection and the rugby champions of Romania, a Bucharest club. The paper hoped that "the first visit of a foreign team to our grounds" (*"la primera visita de un equipo extranjero en nuestros campos"*) would be a first marker in the sport's revival. The first match went badly for the home side, by the paper's account, and the second was drawn, 3–3, a try each, Elías scoring Barcelona's. The headline was "Our rugby achieved a brilliant draw against the Romanians" (*"Nuestro Rugby logró un brillante empate ante los rumanos"*).

The detail that matters is in the city news of the same week. The visitors were taken to the city hall and to the provincial council, and with them, alongside the Romanian consul, went "the president of the Catalan Rugby Federation, Captain Don Luis Sentís" (*"el presidente de la Federación Catalana de Rugby, capitán don Luis Sentís"*). A second notice gives him a different title: "delegate president of the Rugby Federation in Catalonia" (*"delegado presidente de la Federación de Rugby en Cataluña"*). Under the Republic the Catalan federation had sat in FIRA in its own right and had sponsored the Spanish federation's entry. Its own history says that independence "lasted until 1940" (*"Aquesta independència es perllongà fins al 1940"*). The paper's words for the side that met the Bucharest club mark the change: it was "our team, representative of the city" (*"nuestro equipo, representativo de la ciudad"*). Catalan rugby had started again some nine months after the war's end, under a president the paper styled Captain, and it no longer spoke for itself abroad. The second voice that the last generation had fought over was gone.

The federation itself was not. The Catalan encyclopaedia of sport says it "lost its independence but went on playing friendly matches" (*"perdé la seva independència però seguí disputant partits amistosos"*). FC Barcelona's history has its rugby section playing friendlies from the middle of 1940, at Montjuïc, on the Diagonal and at Sant Boi, and "the official competitions of the Catalan Rugby Federation" (*"las competiciones oficiales de la Federación Catalana de Rugby"*) beginning early in 1941. It also remembers an exhibition at Manresa against Espanyol, before a full ground. And six years on there was Sentís at Les Corts, first among "those of the Catalan", listed after the men from Madrid. With him was a Baltasar. FC Barcelona's history names a **Francisco Baltasar** as the delegate of its rugby section from its first match in 1924, and a Baltasar had played for the city against the Bucharest side.

What the Catalan clubs kept was the field, and they kept nearly all of it. FC Barcelona went to Madrid in June 1942 and beat Real Madrid 28–0 in a semi-final at Vallecas. Three days later, on **24 June**, they met the S.E.U. de Madrid at Chamartín. The agency note in the next morning's *La Vanguardia* said that Barcelona, "after clear superiority through both halves" (*"después de una neta superioridad a través de los dos tiempos"*), had won 17–8. The club's own history adds the scene the agency left out: its captain, Sardá, "received the Cup of H.E. the Generalísimo from the hands of General Moscardó, national delegate of sports" (*"recibió la Copa del S.E. el Generalísimo de manos del General Moscardó delegado nacional de Deportes"*). The club's president had stayed in Madrid with his players through every day of the wait.

Santboiana, the club the vet from Sant Boi had founded in 1921, won the next final, 16–0 against Tavernes, a side from Valencia. Then Barcelona beat the S.E.U. de Madrid in three finals running, 9–0, 3–0 and the 23–13 at Les Corts. The students won the rematch of 1946–47, 16–3. Santboiana beat the S.E.U. de León, 13–9. Atlético de Madrid took the cup in 1948–49, beating Santboiana 8–3. Then FC Barcelona won four in a row, three of the finals against Atlético. The S.E.U. de Madrid won once more, in 1953–54. After that the cup did not leave greater Barcelona for nine seasons: FC Barcelona twice, the Club Natació Barcelona, Santboiana five times running from 1957–58 to 1961–62, and the Natació again.

The count for the whole generation is this. From the first *Campeonato de S.E. el Generalísimo* in 1940–41 to the final of 1964–65 there were twenty-five finals. Madrid sides won five of them, the fifth in 1964, which belongs later in this story. Clubs from Barcelona and Sant Boi won twenty. Before the war, when the argument between the two cities was open and the Catalans had their own seat abroad, they had won six cups of eight.

The record does not explain it. The federation's history says only that the game was rebuilt after the war on two models, "a French model (a flowering of clubs)" in Catalonia and "a British model (the growth of university clubs)" in Madrid (*"en Cataluña se dio un modelo francés (florecimiento de clubes), en Madrid se dio un modelo británico (crecimiento de clubes universitarios)"*). The roll of finals is not the whole of how the period is remembered in Catalonia. Decades later the Catalan federation's press officer told the daily *Ara* that "in 30 years of Franco's regime, from 1939 to 1970, only one new club was born" there. Both things can be true: few new clubs, and the old ones holding the cup. They would also supply players to the national side, when there was one again.

### Montjuïc Again

**13 April 1952.** The stadium on Montjuïc had been opened in 1929 by a rugby match, Spain against Italy, with flags, anthems and flowers, and Spain had won 9–0. Twenty-three years later the same two countries came out on the same ground. The captains exchanged pennants. The two national anthems were played. *La Vanguardia* counted "a numerous public" (*"numeroso público"*). It was Spain's first international at home since the win over Portugal in Madrid sixteen years before.

Spain began fast and unsettled the Italians. Blanch missed a penalty after nine minutes. A pass from **Ramón Rabassa**, who would win nineteen caps and captain the side, set the whole line moving after fifteen, and the last man could not finish it. There was no score at half-time. Ten minutes into the second half the Italians kicked a penalty, and at twenty-two minutes their wing scored a spectacular try. It ended **6–0** to Italy. The paper thought the result fair: the visitors had been "superior in preparation, technique and physical condition" (*"superior en preparación, técnica y condiciones físicas"*). The federation's history counts the afternoon as Spain's first match in the European Cup, the competition FIRA had organised for its members, and notes that a try was then worth three points. Five countries entered. Spain went out in the first preliminary round, and France won the cup.

The national side had come back the year before, away from home. On **6 May 1951**, in Rome, Italy beat Spain 12–0. It was the first international in the federation's record since March 1936. Its trace is in the Barcelona press. That weekend FC Barcelona played a club from Frankfurt at La Bordeta and won 13–5, without shining, and the reporter offered a reason: "the absence of some of its regulars, who were in Rome with the national side" (*"la ausencia de algunos de sus titulares que estaban en Roma con la selección nacional"*).

The matches came slowly. West Germany won 17–6 in Madrid at the end of December 1952. A Royal Air Force fifteen drew 3–3 there in April 1953. Then, in the spring of **1954**, came the best five weeks the national side had in this generation. The European Cup was played again, with six countries. Spain drew 6–6 with West Germany in Frankfurt on 21 March. On 5 April it beat Portugal 23–0 in Madrid, its first win since 1936. On 11 April it beat Belgium 14–0 at Treviso. On 19 April it lost the semi-final to Italy at Naples, 16–6. And on **25 April**, in a match the federation's tables count in the tournament without saying what stage it was, it played France's second team at Les Corts.

"The most sensational and spectacular of all the rugby matches played in our city up to now," *La Vanguardia* called it (*"el más sensacional y espectacular de todos los partidos de rugby hasta la fecha celebrados en nuestra ciudad"*). France B won **45–28**. The French were young, hard to tackle and never seemed to lose the ball. Spain were well behind at the interval and made up a good part of the difference after it, with eleven points from Blanch, six from Morales, five from Corominas and three each from Jorge and Arganza. The reporter did not think the score needed an apology: "When a pupil, however gifted, meets masters like those of France's B team, no more can be asked of him than they did on Sunday" (*"Cuando un discípulo, por aventajado que sea, se enfrenta con unos maestros como los del equipo B de Francia, no puede exigírseles más de lo que hicieron el domingo"*). He gave much of the credit for the whole run to the national selector, **Juan Vázquez Tamayo**. At the end the French captain was handed a trophy and Rabassa, for Spain, the cup of *El Mundo Deportivo*. Spain finished third of the six.

It was a pupil's place, and the generation did not improve on it. The Royal Air Force drew again, 5–5, in Barcelona in 1955. France's full side beat Spain 50–6 at Limoges that year, 45–6 at the Mediterranean Games in Barcelona in July, and 42–3 at Valencia in 1956. Italy won in Barcelona, and so did West Germany. In 1958 Spain met Romania at Liège, during the Brussels World's Fair, and lost by two points, 14–12; two days later it beat Belgium at Charleroi. It beat Belgium twice more, in Brussels and Madrid, and West Germany in Barcelona in 1960. Then the list thins. There was no international in 1961. In April 1962 West Germany and the Royal Air Force both won in Barcelona. There was none in 1963 and none in 1964. In 1965 the Royal Air Force won at Seville, and on 1 May Spain beat Portugal 12–9 in Lisbon.

From Rome in 1951 to Lisbon in 1965 the federation's record has twenty-five matches, four of them against the airmen. Spain won seven, drew three and lost fifteen. It beat Belgium four times, Portugal twice and West Germany once, and never beat Italy, France or Romania. Fifteen of the twenty-five were at home, and the venues answer a question the last generation had left open. **Nine were in Barcelona. Four were in Madrid.** The federation's presidents were Madrid men, every one. When the national side needed a ground and a crowd, it went to Montjuïc and Les Corts.

In Madrid, meanwhile, the game that the party's union had rebuilt was changing under its feet.

### The Faculties and the Colleges

There is a photograph in the history of the Architecture club that says most of it. Its caption reads: "Architecture team, University Champions in 1947, in the kit of the S.E.U." (*"Equipo de Arquitectura Campeón Universitario en 1947 con la equipación del SEU"*). The shirts were the union's. The team was the faculty's.

Madrid's university rugby ran on two ladders. One was the university competition, faculty against faculty: Architecture's rivals, its history says, were Medicine, Agriculture, Law, Dentistry, the engineering schools. The other was the federation's, the championship of Castile and beyond it the cup, where the opponents were clubs like Atlético de Madrid and Real Madrid. The best players climbed both. The club says they "alternated their participation in the university competition with their membership of the teams that were then federated" (*"alternaban su participación en la competición universitaria con su militancia en los equipos entonces federados"*). Some of the surnames in its roll of the late 1940s, Matamoros, Medel, Vázquez, are also in the S.E.U. de Madrid fifteen that *La Vanguardia* printed for the final at Les Corts. The names recur; whether they are the same men the sources do not say. If they are, the union's side in the national cup was partly the faculties' side in another shirt.

For a time the arrangement worked. The S.E.U. de Madrid played in eight finals between 1941 and 1954 and won three. Atlético de Madrid, who had beaten Architecture on the way, won the cup in 1949 and lost four finals to FC Barcelona. In 1952 the federation began a national league, the *Copa Presidente del Gobierno*: FC Barcelona won it, Atlético were second, and the S.E.U. de Madrid, under the name Mayo Madrid, were fourth in its second season.

Then both stopped. The league was played twice and not again until 1969–70. The S.E.U. de Madrid's cup of **1953–54**, won 16–3 against the Natació Barcelona, was its last, and it never reached a final again. The faculties went on as before: Architecture won the Madrid university championship every year from 1948 to 1959. But by the end of the decade even Atlético was short of players, and Atlético and Architecture merged. The merger was possible, the club explains, because Architecture's captain, **Javier Barroso**, was also Atlético's president. The side played as Architecture among the universities and as Atlético in the cup, and lost the 1958 semi-final to Santboiana.

Atlético had run short, the club's history says, after "the move to Cisneros of Fernando Monge and other players" (*"tras el paso al Cisneros de Fernando Monge y otros jugadores"*). The Colegio Mayor Jiménez de Cisneros was the hall of residence Franco had visited straight from the rugby in October 1943, received by its secretary and by the architect in charge of the building, a señor Barroso; whether he was Architecture's Barroso the report does not say. Its rugby club is dated to the same year. Architecture's history calls it "the revived team of the Colegio Cisneros, where Architecture students abounded" (*"el recuperado equipo del Colegio Cisneros, donde abundaban los estudiantes de Arquitectura"*), and by the 1960s it calls it something else: in the university competition, "the Colegio Mayor Cisneros, the strongest team then" (*"el Colegio Mayor Cisneros, el equipo más potente entonces"*), ahead of San Pablo and Aquinas.

The union whose kit Architecture had worn was by then close to its end as the students' only voice. A decree of **5 April 1965** created professional student associations which, a historian of the union writes, "although integrated in the S.E.U., in reality came to replace it" (*"aunque integradas en el SEU, en realidad, venían a sustituirlo"*). The gazette later carried an order of 5 June regulating a commissioner's office, a *Comisaría*, for the union itself. The union was not abolished that spring. It was stepped around. A team called S.E.U. de Valladolid was still being photographed in 1963; what became of the union's fifteens after 1965, the sources do not say.

Madrid's rugby did not wait for the decree. In **1963–64** the Canoe Natación Club beat Santboiana 3–0 and brought the cup to the capital for the first time in ten years. FC Barcelona won it back, and Canoe took it again in 1965–66. In 1964, Architecture's history says, the club entered "the then newly created national federated competition" (*"la entonces recién creada competición nacional federada"*). Which competition it means, its history does not say; the federation's league did not resume until 1969–70. It describes what followed in one sentence: with new clubs appearing, "many of university origin, the federated competition overtakes the university one in importance" (*"muchos de origen universitario, la competición federada supera en importancia a la universitaria"*). The students had not left the game. They had left the union's ladder for the federation's, and taken their colleges with them.

In 1964 the regime's sports authority, the Delegación Nacional de Deportes, gave the rugby federation its Trofeo General Moscardó, the prize, the federation's yearbook explains, for the federation that most distinguished itself in its activities.

One more beginning belongs here, though nobody then could have known its weight. In 1961, at the Colegio El Salvador in Valladolid, a French priest named **Georges Bernés**, who had come to the city to study the pilgrim road to Santiago, put a proposal to the family that owned the school, and rugby began there.

### What this generation leaves behind

In July 1936 Spanish rugby had been divided between a presidency in Madrid and a Catalan federation with a seat of its own abroad. By 1965 the division had been closed from above. A decree had given the party's sports delegation the power to name every federation's president, and every president on rugby's list was from Madrid. The Catalan federation's independence had ended in 1940. The cup had been given the dictator's title, and the first final under it had been won by the Falange's student union.

And the field had gone the other way. Of the twenty-five finals from 1941 to 1965, clubs from Barcelona and Sant Boi won twenty. FC Barcelona kept the Generalísimo's trophy outright in 1946. When the national side returned after fifteen years it took some of FC Barcelona's regulars to Rome, and it played nine of its fifteen home matches in their city and four in the capital. The regime took the office and the voice. It did not take the game.

What it built instead was a second game in Madrid, inside the university: first under the union's protection, in the union's shirts, on the union's fields; then in the faculties' own names; and by the 1960s in the colleges. From 1954 to 1963 no Madrid side won the cup; Canoe ended that in 1964. The university game had grounds, a new intake of students every year, and by the middle of the 1960s clubs of its own in the federation's competitions. And in Valladolid a French priest's school had been playing since 1961.

The national side, meanwhile, had finished third in Europe in 1954 and had not played at all in three of the five years before 1965. On **27 March 1966** it met Portugal in Madrid in the first FIRA Nations Cup, in the second division, and lost 9–3. The season after that, the cup that still bore the Generalísimo's name was won, for the first time, by a hall of residence: the Colegio Mayor Cisneros, the college Franco had gone on to from the rugby pitch in 1943.

## Generation 3: The Colegios Mayores (1966–1990)

**May 1977.** In the last days of the month the cup final was played, and Cisneros scored first. Their full-back, Perote, finished "a very fast move" (*"una jugada rapidísima"*) that caught the other side too far up the field, and converted his own try: six points to nil. The reply came from an international, Cristín, whose try for the other side, the CAU, was worth four points and went unconverted. Then Apraiz kicked a penalty, and at half-time the CAU led by seven points to six.

"If in this first half the college men had shown themselves dangerous in their fast counter-attacks," *El País* told its readers on Tuesday 31 May, "in the second the greater strength and pressure of the CAU's tireless forwards ended by tilting the scoreboard" (*"Si en esta primera parte los colegiales se habían mostrado peligrosos en sus rápidos contraataques, en la segunda, la mayor fuerza y presión de la incansable delantera del CAU terminó por inclinar el marcador"*). A try by the fly-half, another penalty, a try from a scrum, a last try by Manero, and Apraiz's boot after most of them: **26–6**. The paper's headline called the CAU the just winners of the cup.

It was the forty-fifth final of the Spanish championship and the first of its kind. A year earlier the trophy had still been the *Campeonato de S.E. el Generalísimo*. Franco had died in November 1975, and with the season of 1976–77 the federation's roll carries a new heading: ***Copa de S.M. el Rey***, His Majesty the King's Cup. Spain's first free general election in forty-one years was a fortnight away. And the two sides that met for the first cup to carry the King's name were a university hall of residence and a second Madrid club, the CAU.

A generation earlier a Madrid side in the final had usually been there to lose to FC Barcelona. To see how the game's weight had crossed the country, go back ten years, to the first college that won it.

### The Colleges' Cup

The roll gives the line and nothing more: 1966–67, Colegio Mayor Cisneros 14, Unió Esportiva Santboiana 3. *La Vanguardia* carried no report of it that a search of its pages could find. What it meant can be read from the lines around it. The cup had been played twenty-six times under Franco's title, and this was the first time a college had won it. Santboiana, five times champions running only a few years before, would wait until 1989 to win it again.

Cafrune, who played in the Cisneros side of the 1970s and won two league titles with the college, told a Madrid magazine in 2018 how its side had grown: "In Madrid there was almost no rugby team, and the club began to gather in people who wanted to play this sport" (*"En Madrid no había casi ningún equipo de rugby y el club empezó a aglutinar gente que quería jugar a este deporte"*). Others, he said, came up afterwards, "like Canoe or the School of Architecture" (*"como el Canoe o la Escuela de Arquitectura"*). The last generation had left the university game with grounds, a yearly intake and clubs of its own. Now those clubs were winning.

It also had a federation that wanted them. In 1967 **Florencio Noriega** became president, and he held the office for ten years. The federation's records show him at every assembly from December 1967 to July 1976, and give him its silver medal and later its gold; they say almost nothing else about him. The Architecture club's history is warmer. With his arrival, it says, "a serious structure of national competition is created" (*"se crea una estructura de competición nacional seria"*). In 1969–70 a national league was played for the first time since 1954, and this time it lasted. In 1970, "at the request of the Spanish Rugby Federation" (*"a petición de la Federación Española de Rugby"*), the architecture students' side took legal form as a club in its own right.

The first champions of the new league were not from Madrid, or from Barcelona. They were the **Atlético San Sebastián**, a Basque athletics club that a rugby side had joined only in March 1964. It had won the second-category championship in 1966 and the cup in 1967–68, beating FC Barcelona 8–6. For a decade the titles moved between the Basque coast and the capital. San Sebastián won the cup four times in the ten seasons from 1966–67 to 1975–76. Canoe won it three times, Cisneros twice, and Architecture once, in the last final played under the Generalísimo's name. In the league Canoe took three titles running from 1970–71, Architecture the next two, Cisneros the one after.

The city that had held the cup for a generation is missing from that list. Catalan clubs reached the final four times in those ten seasons, Santboiana once, FC Barcelona twice and the Natació Barcelona once, and lost every time. In all, from 1965–66 to 1981–82, seventeen seasons, no club from Barcelona or Sant Boi won the cup. In the twenty-five seasons before, they had won it twenty times.

The federation's history offers its two models, a French one of clubs in Catalonia and a British one of university clubs in Madrid, and does not say why the second overtook the first. What the clubs' own accounts show is how seriously the Madrid students now took the game. Architecture travelled: to Paris and London in 1974, to Britain again in 1976 and 1977, coming home from the last tour with wins over London Scottish and London Welsh. In London in 1974 its players met a Welsh coach, **Ray Williams**, who agreed to train them that summer. After a second camp, which he ran at Elizondo in June 1975, the club's history says, it "worked hard on the preparation of coaches, sending many of them to the courses that the Welsh Rugby Union gave in summer" (*"se trabajó duro en la preparación de entrenadores, enviando a muchos de ellos a los cursos que impartía en verano la Welsh Rugby Union"*). A game that had come into Spain over the Pyrenees was now going to Wales for its schooling.

There was a third place on the map, still faint. El Salvador, the Valladolid school side that a French priest had started in 1961, finished second in the league in 1970–71 and again the season after, and lost the cup final of 1971 to Canoe, 6–3. A second Valladolid side, the university's, reached the final of 1975.

By October 1976, with the first season of the King's Cup under way, *El País* could describe the title as one "already all but booked for the capital" (*"un título ya casi abonado a la capital"*).

### The King's Cup

Seven months later the same paper put it in a headline: "Rugby and volleyball, in Spain, belong to Madrid" (*"El rugby y el voleibol, en España, son cosa de Madrid"*). The date was **12 May 1977**, and the occasion was the quarter-finals of the cup. Cisneros had beaten Santboiana 7–0 to overturn the first leg. The CAU had beaten a Valladolid side 29–3. Architecture had won in San Sebastián and Canoe at Cornellà. The four Madrid clubs, the paper wrote, "have eliminated the rest of Spain's teams from this competition" (*"han eliminado de esta competición al resto de equipos españoles"*). And it drew the conclusion without softening it: "Catalonia, until very recently the dominator of national rugby, has definitively lost any presence of weight" (*"Cataluña, hasta hace bien poco dominadora del rugby nacional, ha perdido definitivamente cualquier presencia de entidad"*).

The cup that those four clubs were playing for had changed its name and nothing else. The federation's roll runs straight on, final XLIV under the Generalísimo, final XLV under the King, the same numbering, the same competition, most of the same clubs. Six of the seven finals from 1975–76 to 1981–82 were between two Madrid sides. The other, in 1979, was between Cisneros and Valencia, and even that one was settled on a Madrid club's terms: the federation proposed Barcelona as the ground, Valencia agreed, and Cisneros refused. It was played instead on a Sunday at noon at **Tarazona**, in Aragón, eighty kilometres from Zaragoza, where a new pitch was being opened. "The superiority of the college men was total," *El País* reported, "despite the not very lopsided 26–14" (*"La superioridad de los colegiales fue total, pese al 26-14 no demasiado abultado"*).

Around the cup, the machinery of the old regime's sport was being replaced. The Delegación Nacional de Deportes, the party body that had held the power to name every federation's president since 1941, gave way in 1977 to a Consejo Superior de Deportes. That same year the new council chose Architecture as the best sports club in the country, and the club's history records who handed over the trophy: it was a "trophy received from the hands of H.M. the King Don Juan Carlos" (*"trofeo recogido de manos de S.M. el Rey Don Juan Carlos"*). In 1981 the King accepted the honorary presidency of the club's fiftieth-anniversary celebrations.

The men at the head of the federation changed too, and for the first time the record says something about where they came from. **Javier Cortázar**, who followed Noriega in 1977, had been honoured by the federation in 1970 as captain of the national side. **Alberto Pico**, president from 1981, was a Madrid man born in 1943 who had finished his playing days at the CAU and was also its director and its president. He led the federation until 1992, when, his obituary in *Libertad Digital* recorded, a decree limiting consecutive re-elections obliged him to go. Every president of the generation was still from Madrid.

The game they governed was wider than it had been. By 1976 Architecture alone was running twelve rugby teams a week, "including a women's team that made its debut in 1976" (*"incluyendo un equipo femenino que debutó en 1976"*). The federation's own account dates Spanish women's rugby from that decade and from two places, Architecture in Madrid and the physical-education institute, INEF, in Barcelona. The league changed shape: in 1978–79 it was played in four regional groups, and that season, for want of a final phase, there was no champion at all. For the three seasons after it the four group winners played off, and the winner each time was a Madrid club.

Two entries in Architecture's history belong to another chapter of this book as much as to this one. In May 1979, it says, the club received Northern Transvaal at the Vallehermoso stadium, and in 1987 it toured South Africa. Those were the years of the sporting boycott that the South Africa chapter describes. The club's history records the fixtures and makes no comment on them.

The university game had come through the transition without a break in its fixture list, and with a King's name on its cup and the King's hand on one of its trophies. What it had not done was make Spain a side that could live with the best of Europe. That was being tested every spring.

### The Lift

**3 June 1973.** The ground was the Foixarda, on Montjuïc, and it was a warm Sunday morning. *La Vanguardia* found "a full house" (*"un lleno completo"*), and called the sight of it "a brilliant and unusual spectacle of a numerous public" (*"un brillante e insólito espectáculo de numeroso público"*). Spain was playing France in FIRA's cup, in its first season in the top group, and France were the favourites for the tie and the tournament.

France won, 30–23. What the reporter, J. Pardo, wanted his readers to notice was how. The French, he wrote, had been "very disconcerted by the resistance and good play of the Spaniards" (*"muy desconcertada por la resistencia y buen juego de los españoles"*) and had needed their best players to get in front. Spain had drawn level. A much wider margin had been forecast, France being used to far bigger wins. And he ventured a judgment: "Until now Spanish rugby was considered internationally third-rate, but from this match at the Foixarda, and to the great surprise of many, the French included, Spain begins to count as one of the sides to be reckoned with in European rugby" (*"Hasta ahora el rugby español estaba considerado internacionalmente de tercera categoría, pero a partir de este encuentro en la Fuxarda —y con gran sorpresa para muchos, incluidos los franceses— España empieza a contar como uno de los conjuntos a tener en cuenta en el rugby europeo"*). Among the fifteen was a fly-half from Architecture, **Manuel Moriche**. His club's history quotes Ray Williams as saying he could have been first-choice fly-half for any of the Five Nations sides of the time.

It had taken seven years to reach that morning. Spain had entered FIRA's championship in 1966 in the second division and lost its first match, to Portugal. For five seasons running, from 1967 to 1971, it finished second. Then, in May 1972, it beat Italy **10–0** in Madrid and drew 6–6 with them at Ivrea a week later, and went up.

It stayed up for seven seasons. It finished third in 1973, third in 1974, and third again in 1977 and 1978. It beat Italy once more, 10–3 in Madrid in December 1977. It came within seven points of France twice, at the Foixarda and at Hendaye in 1976. But the top of the group was a long way off, and the distance showed on bad days: 76–3 to Romania in Bucharest in April 1978, and on **4 March 1979**, on the Île d'Oléron, France 92, Spain 0. That spring Spain finished sixth of six and went down.

What followed is the pattern of the generation. Spain won all four matches in the second division in 1979–80 and came straight back up. It lost all five in the top group in 1980–81 and went straight back down. It won the second division again in 1984, was relegated in 1985, won promotion in 1987 by beating the Netherlands in a final at Bordeaux, lost all eight matches of a two-year top-group season, and was relegated in 1989. It would go up again in 1990. Five promotions and four relegations between 1972 and 1990: too strong for one floor, not strong enough for the next.

In November **1982** two of the great names of the game came to Madrid within four days. On Saturday the 20th the New Zealand Māori played Spain at half past twelve "on the old ground of the Ciudad Universitaria" (*"en el viejo campo de la Ciudad Universitaria"*), the match going out live by satellite to New Zealand. *El País* had been frank beforehand: the interest would lie in "the rare chance to watch their ritual opening dance and their play, whatever the result" (*"la rara oportunidad de contemplar su danza ritual inicial y su juego, al margen del resultado"*). Moriche, the captain, kicked a penalty in the thirteenth minute. It was Spain's only score. The Māori won **66–3**, before a crowd that *El Independiente*, retelling the day forty years later, put at eight thousand. The man from the sports daily *As* reached for the bullring: the Spaniards, he wrote, had played the part of "a novice who is starting out and is shut in with six Miura bulls" (*"un novillero que se inicia y lo encierran con seis miuras"*). Forty years on Moriche remembered it differently. "For us, playing that match was a tremendous triumph" (*"Para nosotros, jugar aquel partido fue un triunfo tremendo"*).

Three days later, at Vallehermoso, Argentina won 28–19, with Hugo Porta, *El País* noted, living up to his fame; the paper thought the result excellent. Other visitors and other journeys followed through the decade: a Welsh fifteen that won 65–16 in Madrid in 1983 and 80–9 at Bridgend two years later, two tours of Zimbabwe, England's second string, and Scotland, who won at Cornellà in 1986 and at Murrayfield in April 1987, 25–7, a defeat the federation's history files among the decade's honourable ones. On **5 November 1988**, in Seville, the Māori came back and won only 22–12, Spain scoring tries through Azkargorta, Puertas and Claudio. *El País* called it a historic result.

That was the side, before and after, that the first World Cup did not send for.

### Not on the List

The first Rugby World Cup was played in New Zealand and Australia in May and June **1987**. There was no qualifying. Seven of the eight member unions of the International Rugby Football Board took part, South Africa being left out, and nine other countries were invited: Argentina, Canada, Fiji, Italy, Japan, Romania, the United States, Tonga and Zimbabwe. Spain was not one of them.

No source says whether Spain was considered. What can be set out is where it stood. It was not a member of the Board; World Rugby's own register dates the Spanish federation's affiliation to **March 1988**, the year after the tournament. Nor were any of the nine who were invited. Spain's international home for half a century had been FIRA, which the federation's history describes as founded "to put a counterweight to the power of the International Board" (*"por poner contrapeso al poder de la International Board"*). Two of the nine guests, Italy and Romania, were FIRA's strongest sides after France. Spain met both whenever it was in the top group; it had beaten Italy twice in this generation and Romania never. A third guest was Zimbabwe, whom Spain had met four times since 1983, winning two and losing two.

Spain's own 1987 can be followed in the federation's record. In April it lost at Murrayfield. A week later it beat Poland in Madrid, and on 10 May it beat the Netherlands at Bordeaux to win promotion. While the World Cup was being played, it had no fixtures. In August it crossed the Atlantic. On **2 August**, in Montevideo, it beat Uruguay 16–8, in the years when, as the Uruguay chapter tells, Uruguay too stood outside the game's top table; a fortnight later, at Mar del Plata, Argentina won 40–12.

The next World Cup would have a qualifying competition, and Spain was in it from the first whistle. In eight days in the autumn of **1989**, in Madrid, it beat the Netherlands 29–15, Poland 23–9 and Belgium 58–6. The round that would decide the matter was a year away, in Italy.

### The Map Comes Apart

At home, meanwhile, the map that *El País* had drawn in 1977 was coming apart. The league was played as a single national division again in 1982–83, eight clubs, two each from Catalonia, Madrid, the Basque Country and Valencia, and the title went to **Valencia**, a club founded in May 1966 by teenagers with no ground of their own, who played on a village football pitch or by the river. San Sebastián were second, Santboiana third, Architecture fourth. "The first four," the federation's account of the season says, "from four different points of Spain" (*"Los cuatro primeros, de cuatro puntos diferentes de España"*). That spring's cup final was contested by two clubs that were not in the top division. FC Barcelona beat the CAU, and the cup went to Barcelona for the first time in eighteen years.

Madrid did not fall away. Architecture won four leagues and five cups between 1980 and 1988, and by December 1986 it was appearing in the results columns as Xerox Arquitectura, with a sponsor's name in front of the faculty's. Cisneros won the league in 1984–85. Another Madrid club, the Olímpico de Pozuelo, won the cup in 1987. But Santboiana won the league three times between 1984 and 1989, and in 1988–89 the cup, its first since 1962. In Valladolid the university side reached another final, in 1985, and lost it to FC Barcelona, and former pupils of a second school founded their own club in 1986. In the Basque Country a club called **Getxo**, started in 1975 by three players from Gernika, climbed in the 1980s from regional rugby to the first division.

And the women's game got a championship of its own. In 1989 the first Spanish women's championship was played, won by INEF Barcelona, 10–6, against a Madrid university side, and that year a Spanish women's national team took the field for the first time, against France.

Architecture's history, looking back on what happened to it next, blames its own success: a decline, it says, "caused by the success of the rugby section and the abandonment of the other sports sections, the youth base and the links with the School and the University" (*"originado por el éxito de la sección de rugby y el abandono de otras secciones deportivas, la cantera y los vínculos con la Escuela y Universidad"*). The college sides had become clubs, and the clubs had drifted from the colleges.

On **3 June 1990** Getxo beat Santboiana 28–7 and took the King's Cup to the Basque coast.

### What this generation leaves behind

The generation began with a hall of residence winning a cup named for a dictator and ended with a Basque town winning the same cup named for a king. In between, the game crossed Spain. From 1966–67 to 1989–90 there were twenty-four finals. Madrid clubs won sixteen of them, San Sebastián four, Barcelona and Sant Boi, which had won twenty of the twenty-five before, three, and Getxo the last. No source explains the change. The record shows only who was building: students in Madrid with a federation behind them, a national league from 1969, and coaches sent to Wales.

The transition to democracy passed over this game lightly. The cup changed its name between one final and the next. The party's delegation became a council. The last president of the generation would leave under a rule limiting re-election. All of them were still Madrid men, and for the first time some of them are known to have been players.

The national side learned its level and could not rise above it. It beat Italy twice, ran France close twice, went up five times and came down four, and lost by ninety-two points on an island off the French coast. When the first World Cup was held it was not a member of the body that held it, and was not asked. It joined in 1988.

By 1990 the capital no longer had the trophies to itself: Valencia, Sant Boi and Getxo had all won trophies, Valladolid sides had reached three cup finals, and a women's championship had been played. The last cup final of the generation was won by a club fifteen years old. And in Madrid, in the autumn of 1989, Spain had won its first three World Cup qualifying matches. The rest of that road ran through Italy, from 30 September 1990.

## Generation 4: Galashiels (1990–1999)

**2 October 1999.** Netherdale, in Galashiels, in the Scottish Borders, was a long way from Murrayfield, and for Spain it was the ground that mattered. South Africa and Scotland were still to come, in Edinburgh, and *El País* had already written those two off as "mission impossible" (*"misión imposible"*). This one, against Uruguay, could be won. It was, the paper's headline would say the next morning, Spain's only opportunity.

Uruguay's forwards took the game away early. After twenty-three minutes its captain, **Diego Ormaechea**, forty years old, went over from a scrum, and a second try came the same way. Spain answered with the boot of its fly-half, **Andrei Kovalenko**, who landed five penalties out of six. Late in the match it was 17–12. Spain ran the ball, the move broke down, and Kovalenko kicked again: **17–15**. Then, as the paper's man put it, "at 17–15 Spain was finished" (*"con el 17-15 España se acabó"*). Chasing the game in the last minutes, it threw two passes that Uruguayans caught and carried to the other end. It ended 27–15.

The Uruguay chapter of this book opens a generation on the same afternoon, from the other dressing-room: a first World Cup, a first try, a first win. For Spain it was also a first World Cup, and the match its ten years of qualifying had led to. To see how much had to change for a Spanish side to be standing on that field at all, go back to the day the cup went to the Basque coast.

### Fadura

When Getxo beat Santboiana on 3 June 1990, it began what its own history calls a golden age. The club had been founded in 1975 by three players from Gernika. Its early squads, that history says, were drawn "especially from the rowers of the *traineras*" (*"especialmente de remeros de traineras"*), the racing boats of the Basque coast, and it played at the municipal sports ground at **Fadura**. Between 1991 and 1995, the club records, it went unbeaten there for more than forty-five league matches. It kept the cup in 1991, beating Gernika in an all-Basque final, and again in 1992. It won the league in 1993. It won the cup a fourth time in 1997.

Most of the decade's other trophies went to two places. One was Seville. The **Club de Rugby Ciencias** had been founded in 1972 in the Faculty of Sciences of the city's university and had detached itself from the faculty in 1977. It won the league in 1992 and 1994 and the cup three years running, from 1994 to 1996. The other was Valladolid, where rugby had started in a school in 1961. **El Salvador**, the school's club, won the league in 1991 and 1998 and the cup in 1993 and 1999. Across the city the senior players of a second school, the Colegio de Lourdes, had founded their own club in 1986, when the school stopped supporting a senior side. Its first sponsor was a farm, the Granja Conchita, and it took blue and white, its history says, out of admiration for the Scotland team of the 1980s. That club, the **Valladolid Rugby Asociación Club**, won the cup in 1998 and the league in 1999. Its history ventures that Valladolid became "probably the Spanish city where this sport has acquired most importance" (*"probablemente, la ciudad española donde más importancia ha adquirido este deporte"*). Santboiana, the oldest club of all, took the league in 1996 and 1997.

A rowing town, a science faculty and two schools: none of them was a Madrid hall of residence. In the generation before, Madrid clubs had won sixteen cup finals of twenty-four. In the nine finals from 1991 to 1999 they won none and lost three, Architecture in 1994 and Canoe in 1998 and 1999. Getxo won three of the nine, after the one of 1990; Ciencias three; the two Valladolid clubs three. Of the nine league titles of those seasons Madrid took one, Architecture's in 1995.

Architecture's own history names the clubs that took Madrid's place. Seville's Ciencias and one of the Valladolid sides, which it calls "Quesos", the Cheeses, were the ones "that displace the Madrid classics like Cisneros and Canoe" (*"que desplazan a los clásicos de Madrid como Cisneros y Canoe"*). "Among them all, however," it adds, "Getxo stands out" (*"Entre todos, sin embargo, destaca el Getxo"*).

The federation stayed where it was. Alberto Pico, obliged to step down in 1992, was followed by another Madrid man, Antonio Moreno, and by 1997 Pico was back.

### A Week in Italy, an Afternoon in Madrid

The national side missed two World Cups in the first half of the decade, each by one round. Its coach through the first of them was the Paris-born **Gérard Murillo**, who had held the post since 1986; the federation's account ends his term in 1992.

For the tournament of 1991 Spain had done the first part well, winning three qualifiers in Madrid in the autumn of 1989. The second part was a week in northern Italy a year later, against the two sides that had been invited in 1987 while Spain was not. At Rovigo on **30 September 1990** Italy won 30–6. Three days later, at Padua, Romania won 19–6. *El País* gave the second match four sentences. One of them was: "It again lacked a kicker for the penalties" (*"Volvió a carecer de un pateador en los golpes de castigo"*). Another: "The places, as expected, will be for Italy and Romania" (*"Las plazas, según lo esperado, serán para Italia y Rumania"*). Four days later, on 7 October, Spain beat the Netherlands 22–12 with nothing left to play for.

Sixteen months later Spain ran both of them close. On 9 February 1992 it lost to Italy in Madrid by a single point, 22–21. On **4 April 1992**, also in Madrid, it beat Romania **6–0**, the first time it had beaten Romania in fourteen meetings. It was a season too late, and it was not even enough to stay in FIRA's top group: Spain finished fifth of five and went down again. The pattern of the last generation held. Spain came back up through a play-off against Morocco at Toulouse in November 1993, lost all four matches of the following spring and was relegated in May 1994.

Within weeks it had missed a second World Cup. The route to South Africa in 1995 had begun in Lisbon in May 1993, where Spain beat Switzerland 40–0, Belgium 67–6 and Portugal 37–15 within a week. A year passed. On **21 May 1994**, in Madrid, Wales came for what *El País* called "the most important match in its history in this sport" (*"el partido más importante de su historia en este deporte"*). Wales won 54–0. A week later Spain beat Portugal 35–19, and that was the end of it.

Around the qualifiers the fixture list filled with opponents from the game's first rank, and Spain lost to them. Scottish sides won in Seville in 1990, in Edinburgh in 1991 and in Madrid in 1992 and 1995. An England side won 86–17 at Elche in 1994. Argentina won in Buenos Aires and in Madrid in 1992, the second time only by 43–34. There were better days elsewhere. A Spanish seven, which the federation dates from 1993, finished tenth at that year's first World Cup Sevens in Edinburgh, and Spain took a bronze medal at the 1993 Mediterranean Games. The women's national side, two years after its first match, finished sixth at the first women's World Cup, in Wales in 1991, and in 1995 it won a European championship.

Two qualifying campaigns had gone the same way: everything won against the countries below, everything lost against the countries above. The third would be different because the tournament was. But first the game itself changed.

### Open

On **26 August 1995**, in Paris, the International Board declared rugby union open: players could be paid. The England chapter tells that day from inside the room. Spain's national side spent it in Santiago de Chile, losing 28–23 to Chile. A week later, in Montevideo, it lost 47–10 to Uruguay, the country it had beaten there in 1987.

The federation's own history has a single sentence for what followed: "In 1995, when rugby becomes professional, Spain retreats by giant strides with respect to the powers it was beginning to treat as equals" (*"En 1995, cuando el rugby se convierte en profesional, España retrocede a pasos agigantados con respecto a las potencias a las que comenzaba a tutear"*). It offers no figures. The record has France winning **81–9** at Sant Boi, where the Spanish game had begun, in March 1996. It also has margins as wide before 1995: France by 53–4 in 1992, Wales by 54–0 and England by 86–17 in 1994. And it has Spain, in those same seasons, winning what was within reach: the second division of FIRA's championship in 1995, with a 90–8 win over the Czech Republic in Madrid, and a 52–6 win over Russia a fortnight after the French came to Sant Boi.

No source records a decision by the federation about professionalism, for it or against it. What the clubs' histories record is the weather. Getxo's speaks of relegations and rebuilding "in a context of growing professionalisation" (*"en un contexto de creciente profesionalización"*). Architecture's lists the visitors who came through its side in those years, beginning with a coach from Christchurch, in New Zealand, in 1989. The sharpest words belong to a columnist. On 7 December 1998 Juan José Fernández wrote in *El País* that the Spanish federation "has not evolved as it should have to make the leap to professionalism" (*"no ha evolucionado como debiera para dar el salto al profesionalismo"*), and that the game in Spain "remains a sport of amateurs, with few sponsors" (*"Sigue siendo un deporte de aficionados, con escasos patrocinadores"*).

He wrote that five days after Spain qualified for the World Cup.

### Murrayfield, with Fourteen

The door had been widened. The tournament of 1999 was to have twenty teams where the one before had sixteen, and the federation's account says so directly: after coming close in 1991 and 1995, Spain had its chance "after the increase in participants from 16 to 20" (*"tras el aumento de participantes de 16 a 20"*). The side was now coached by **Alfonso Feijoo**, with **Tomás Pardo** beside him; the sources do not date their appointment. It went through the rounds below without trouble. It won 62–3 in Andorra in November 1997 and beat the Czech Republic 39–8 at Santander three weeks later, before about 1,200 people. In the spring of 1998 it won 24–9 in Germany and beat Portugal 33–22 at Elche. That left a final round in Edinburgh, with Scotland and Portugal. (The federation's own account treats the two meetings with Portugal, at Elche and in Edinburgh, as one tie over two legs.)

On **2 December 1998**, at Murrayfield, Spain played Portugal with a place at the World Cup on the result. In the twenty-sixth minute its back-row forward José Díaz, an experienced man who had come from the French club Castres, was sent off. Spain played the rest of the match with fourteen. Portugal scored two converted tries and a penalty. Spain scored no try at all. Kovalenko, whom the paper described as "of Ukrainian origin" (*"de origen ucranio"*), kicked six penalties, the three-quarter Díez dropped a goal, and it was enough: **21–17**. A defeat, the paper noted, would still have left a repechage against Uruguay and Morocco. "Spain beat Portugal, 21–17," its report from Edinburgh began, "on the great Scottish stage of Murrayfield, and qualified, for the first time, for the final phase of a World Cup" (*"España ganó, por 21-17, a Portugal, en el gran escenario escocés de Murrayfield, y se clasificó, por primera vez, para la fase final de una Copa del Mundo"*). It was the eighteenth of the twenty teams to do so.

Three days later, in Edinburgh, Scotland beat Spain **85–3**. Fernández's column, on the Monday, was headed "A bit-part player at the World Cup" (*"Un comparsa en la Copa del Mundo"*). "Is such humiliation worth it once more?" he asked (*"¿Merece la pena tamaña humillación una vez más?"*). The federation, by his account, had taken qualification "as one of the key handholds to cling to for the improvement of the standard" (*"como uno de los asideros claves a los que agarrarse para la mejoría del nivel"*). It had ten months to get ready.

### The World Cup

Spain's preparation, as the record shows it, was four internationals in nine days in August 1999, on two continents, all lost: 30–7 to Japan in Tokyo on the 20th, 35–20 to Fiji at Avezzano in Italy on the 24th, 42–11 to Italy at L'Aquila on the 26th, and on the 28th, also at L'Aquila, **20–3 to Uruguay**. Five weeks later the same two sides would open their World Cups against each other.

The party that went to Scotland had Feijoo as coach, Pardo as his assistant, and as manager Alfonso Mandado, of Pontevedra, who two years later would take the presidency out of Madrid, where it had been since 1933. Pardo was himself a kind of answer to the question of where Spanish players came from: taken to France by his emigrant parents when he was two and a half, raised in Vichy, selected for Spain thirty-eight times. The captain was the flanker **Alberto Malo**. Twenty-one players went. *El País*, looking back when it was over, said Spain had arrived "with the label of the only amateur team" (*"con el cartel de único equipo aficionado"*).

The first match was the one at Netherdale. What the paper's account adds to the score is how it was lost. Spain "was clearly dominated in the forwards, overrun in the scrums" (*"fue dominada claramente en delantera, arrollada en las melées"*); Uruguay had more than seventy per cent of the territory at times and won three scrums to every one of Spain's. Spain "limited itself to getting points" (*"se limitó a conseguir puntos"*) from penalties, and did not dare to run the ball until it was behind. The two late Uruguayan tries came in the seventy-eighth and eightieth minutes.

Eight days later, on **10 October**, Spain met the world champions at Murrayfield. South Africa, the paper noted, sent out a line-up of reserves. Spain went out "with the aim of delaying as long as possible the inexorable rain of tries" (*"con el propósito de retardar en todo lo posible la inexorable lluvia de ensayos"*), and for half an hour it did better than that. It tackled, kept its shape, tried to hold on to the ball and, the report says, got to the far end of the South African half. "Spain held out for 31 minutes without conceding a single point. That was its reason for pride" (*"España aguantó 31 minutos sin encajar un solo punto. Ese fue su motivo de orgullo"*). Then the wing Breyton Paulse broke the line, the captain André Vos scored, and the afternoon went the way of the scrums. The full-back Francisco Puertas was left dazed on the grass by a tackle. Malo went off injured. South Africa scored seven tries, five of them from scrums, and won **47–3**, Ferran Velazco kicking Spain's penalty. The paper said a defeat by more than seventy had been expected.

On **16 October**, again at Murrayfield, Scotland, the reigning Five Nations champions, won **48–0**. Álvar Enciso led the side in Malo's place. Spain, the paper recorded, became the third team to finish a World Cup match without a point, and "tried to score a try until the last minute" (*"España intentó conseguir un ensayo hasta el último minuto"*). It did not get one. In three matches it had scored eighteen points, all from penalties, and conceded a hundred and twenty-two.

*El País* closed its account with the two aims the side had taken to Scotland: "Its aims were to beat Uruguay and to give a worthy account of itself. The first it did not achieve. But it played two acceptable matches against two colossi of rugby" (*"Sus propósitos fueron derrotar a Uruguay y ofrecer un papel digno. Lo primero no lo consiguió. Pero hizo dos partidos aceptables ante dos colosos del rugby"*).

### What this generation leaves behind

Spain reached a World Cup in the decade its domestic game finally belonged to the whole country. In nine finals the cup went to Getxo, Seville and Valladolid, and never to Madrid. The clubs that won it had grown out of a rowing town, a science faculty and two schools. The federation's presidents were still Madrid men, and in 2001 the presidency would leave Madrid for the first time since 1933, in the hands of one of the men on the World Cup staff, a Galician.

The national side missed 1991 in a week in Italy and 1995 in an afternoon in Madrid, and reached 1999 when the tournament grew from sixteen teams to twenty. It qualified with fourteen men and without a try, by four points, against Portugal, whom it had beaten in every qualifier of the decade. It went to Scotland carrying, in *El País*'s words, the label of the only amateur team, four years after the game went open, and came home without a try or a win.

Uruguay is the measure of the decade. Spain had beaten Uruguay in Montevideo in 1987. It lost to Uruguay there in 1995, in Italy in August 1999, and at Galashiels when it counted. Two countries that the first World Cup had left off its list both reached the fourth. Only one of them won a match there, and it won it against the other; Scotland and South Africa beat both.

The federation's history dates Spain's retreat from 1995. The same history calls 1999 the sporting peak, reached "when that international professionalisation of rugby had not yet made its mark" (*"cuando todavía no había hecho mella esa profesionalización internacional del rugby"*). The sentence that follows it there opens the next generation: "From then on it fell into an extraordinary slump" (*"A partir de ahí se cayó en un bache extraordinario"*).

## Generation 5: The Long Slump (2000–2018)

**17 April 2016.** The José Zorrilla is a football ground. It belongs to Real Valladolid, and on this Sunday it was full for a rugby match between two clubs from the same city. Before the kick-off there was a minute's silence for the dead of a building collapse in Tenerife and of earthquakes in Ecuador and Japan. The crowd, which the federation would put at twenty-six thousand, greeted **King Felipe VI** with what one report called "a unanimous ovation" (*"una ovación unánime"*), and then applauded the national anthem. With him in the box sat the deputy prime minister, the president of Castile and León, the leader of the Socialist opposition and the mayor.

The match was the final of the King's Cup: **El Salvador** against the holders, the **Valladolid Rugby Asociación Club**. It was close and low. Sam Katz kicked a penalty for El Salvador, the holders' kicker, Griffiths, landed two, and it was 6–3 to them at the interval. Katz levelled it. In the sixty-second minute Alberto Díaz scored the only try of the afternoon, Katz converted, Griffiths kicked one more, and El Salvador had won **13–9**. The king handed over the cup. When the losers went up for their medals, the winners formed a guard of honour and applauded them through it.

"It was not just any day in Valladolid," the report in *El Español* began (*"No era un día cualquiera en Valladolid"*), and it ended: "This Sunday, in Valladolid, a city that thrills to this sport won, and so, in general, did Spanish rugby, which is reborn through this final" (*"Este domingo, en Valladolid, ganó una ciudad que vibra con este deporte y ganó, en general, el rugby español, que renace de la mano de esta final"*).

Reborn from what is the subject of this generation. In October 1999 Spain had come home from its only World Cup. The federation's own history has three words for what followed its peak: *un bache extraordinario*, an extraordinary slump.

### Ninety-Two

The slump has a date and a score. Two months after Spain had won 16–14 in Montevideo, against the Uruguay that had beaten it at Galashiels, the world champions came to Madrid. It was **1 November 2001**. The match was at the Campo Central of the university city. Eight thousand people watched. Australia scored thirteen tries, three of them by Chris Latham, and won **92–10**. It was Australia's four-hundredth Test and the highest score it had ever made. The Irish reports of the day were kind about the losers: "a spirited Spanish side", which "thoroughly deserved" the try its lock José Miguel Villau scored in the sixty-sixth minute. The federation's history uses the match as its example of how far Spain had fallen: it lost, it says, *estrepitosamente*, resoundingly.

That was a friendly. The matches that counted were going the same way. The old FIRA championship had become the European Nations Cup, a league of six below the Six Nations and the European championship of this chapter from here on. Spain finished fourth in it in 2000 and 2001 and fifth in 2002. Qualifying for the World Cup of 2003 ran through some of the same opponents, and Italy. Spain beat Portugal 34–21 in Madrid in June 2002 to reach the round that mattered. In the autumn Italy beat Spain 50–3 at Valladolid and Romania beat it 67–7 at Iași, and those two went to the World Cup. Spain dropped into a two-leg tie against Russia, lost 36–3 in Madrid, won 38–22 at Krasnodar, and was out on aggregate.

Then it was back in. The Spanish federation had lodged a complaint about three of the Russian players, all born in South Africa. An independent judicial officer, the Irish press reported, found that Russian officials "were unable to provide evidence that the three were eligible to play", and Russia was expelled from the qualifying tournament and fined. Spain took Russia's place. On 15 March 2003 it beat Tunisia 33–16 on a neutral ground in France. One tie now stood between Spain and a second World Cup: the United States, home and away. The Americans won **62–13** in Madrid on 12 April and 58–13 in Florida on the 27th.

So the World Cup of 2003 was missed twice over, once on the field and once after a reprieve. A year later Spain lost in Prague, finished sixth of six in the European championship, and was relegated to its second division. The presidency had left Madrid in 2001, for the first time since 1933, with **Alfonso Mandado** of Pontevedra, the manager of the 1999 World Cup party. It would not go back in this generation.

### From the Bottom

Relegation set the terms of the next campaign. To reach the World Cup of 2007 in France, Spain had to start with the countries at the bottom of the European ladder and climb.

It did. Beginning in November 2004 it beat Hungary, Andorra and Slovenia, drew with Croatia, beat Poland, Moldova, Andorra again and the Netherlands, lost to Germany in Heidelberg and beat it by enough in Madrid, and in September 2006 beat the Czech Republic twice. Twelve matches in two years, ten of them won, brought it back into the top division of the European championship and into the last round of World Cup qualifying. The federation's bulletin before the next match allowed itself a moment of pride. Spain, it said, had reached "the decisive phase of qualifying for the 2007 World Cup, which no team coming from the preliminary rounds has ever reached" (*"Fase decisiva de la Clasificación para el Mundial 2007, a la que nunca ha llegado un equipo procedente de las rondas preliminares"*). What waited there, the same bulletin said, were "the two best teams in Europe, after the Six Nations, Romania and Georgia" (*"los dos mejores equipos de Europa, después del VI Naciones, Rumania y Georgia"*).

The coach was an Englishman, **Ged Glynn**. On **14 October 2006**, at the Campo Central in Madrid, Romania won 43–20. Two weeks later, in Tbilisi, Georgia won 37–23. Fourteen matches had come down to the last two, and the last two were lost. The federation's end-of-year bulletin put it without complaint: the Spanish fifteen "did not manage to win one of the places in play, but it did manage to return" to the first division (*"no llegó a conquistar una de las plazas en juego pero sí consiguió regresar"*).

One more place at that World Cup was still open, through a repechage, and it went to a country Spain had beaten in all five World Cup qualifiers the two had played. In March 2007 **Portugal** beat Uruguay over two legs and qualified for the first time. The Uruguay chapter tells that tie from the losing side. From Madrid it looked like this: when the federation printed its guide to the 2007 tournament, it had to list Portugal in a pool with New Zealand, and to write of 1999, "This was the only occasion on which Spain played the Championship" (*"Esta fue la única ocasión en la que España disputó el Campeonato"*).

After that the World Cup was reached, or not, through the league table. Places at the tournament of 2011 were decided in the European championships of 2009 and 2010. Spain finished fifth in both. The 2010 season ended on 27 March in Bucharest, with a 48–3 defeat that sent Romania on to a play-off and, through it, to New Zealand. That winter a side called **Olympus Rugby XV Madrid**, coached by Glynn and captained by the Spain player Pablo Feijoo, was entered in the European Challenge Cup against Connacht, Worcester and Montpellier. It lost all six matches, the last of them 66–0 at home.

The next coach was **Régis Sonnes**, from 2010 to 2012. The European championship of 2011 included a 60–0 defeat in Georgia and a 64–8 defeat in Romania. The one after it was the best season Spain had in the whole generation.

### The Broken-Down Vehicle

On **11 February 2012**, in Madrid, Spain beat Georgia 25–18. On **17 March**, in Madrid again, it beat Romania **13–12**, with a drop goal by Mathieu Peluchon two minutes after the Romanians had gone in front. Spain had beaten Romania once before, in 1992. It finished second of six.

The table that decided places at the World Cup of 2015 was the one for 2013 and 2014 together: the top two would go to England, the third to a play-off. Spain finished sixth in 2013 and fourth in 2014. With three rounds of 2014 left, the International Rugby Board reported that "Spain sit at the foot of the table", the table of the two years together. On 22 February 2014 Romania beat Spain 32–6 at Cluj, and with that result and Georgia's win over Russia the two places were filled. The fourth World Cup of the generation had gone.

The side that won in 2012 and lost in 2013 was not drawn from Spain alone. When the federation printed its squad for a tour of Namibia in November 2012, it arranged the players by the federation each was registered with. One of the headings was *Federación Francesa de Rugby*. Under it were men playing for clubs at Bourg-en-Bresse, Périgueux, Lille and Béziers. Spain had drawn on France before: Tomás Pardo was raised in Vichy, and the man sent off at Murrayfield in 1998 had come from Castres. By 2012 it was ordinary enough to be a heading on a list.

In the summer of 2012, between the win over Romania and the tour, the federation had changed hands. Mandado's eleven years ended, and on **14 July 2012** the assembly chose between two candidates. **Javier González Cancho**, of Valladolid, beat Alfonso Feijoo, the coach of 1999, by forty-two votes to twenty-nine, and promised "a Spanish rugby that is united and built on consensus" (*"un rugby español unido y de consenso"*).

He lasted twenty-one months. The account of what went wrong is Feijoo's, given to the assembly on **12 July 2014** after he had been elected to finish the term. The audit at the end of Mandado's presidency, he said, had shown a sound position. The year 2012 had closed with a deficit of about 185,000 euros. The year 2013 had closed with a deficit of **1,511,749 euros**. The government's sports council had ordered González Cancho to resign, and on 14 April 2014 he had. Most of the creditors, Feijoo told the delegates, were in no position to renegotiate. He read them the auditors' opinion: "These conditions indicate the existence of a significant uncertainty about the capacity of the Federation to continue its operations" (*"Estas condiciones indican la existencia de una incertidumbre significativa sobre la capacidad de la Federación para continuar con sus operaciones"*). And he found his own image for what he had been handed. His task was to "start rolling this broken-down vehicle that the federation now is, for it lacks many means, especially fuel" (*"empezar a echar a rodar este vehículo destartalado que es ahora la federación pues carece de muchos medios, especialmente de combustible"*).

In 2021, the Valladolid press reported, González Cancho accepted a six-month prison sentence and agreed to repay the federation more than 100,000 euros, in a case concerning his management of it. The report does not connect that sum to the deficit of 2013.

The vehicle kept moving. Spain was third in the European championship of 2015, beating Russia 43–20 in Madrid and winning in Lisbon; fourth in 2016; third again in 2017, when it beat Russia, won in Germany and beat Belgium 30–0.

### The Capital of Spanish Rugby

While the national side fell and the federation ran out of money, one city kept winning. The two Valladolid clubs had both begun in schools, El Salvador in the one where Georges Bernés had proposed the game in 1961, the other among the senior players of the Colegio de Lourdes. In the nineteen seasons from 1999–2000 to 2017–18 they won nine of the nineteen cups and thirteen of the nineteen league titles between them. From 2011–12 to 2017–18 no club from anywhere else won the league. Four cup finals were played between the two of them.

The Valladolid Rugby Asociación Club tells its own part of it with a club's pride. In 2001–02, its history says, it played in the European Shield, "the first Spanish rugby team to have played a European international competition" (*"siendo el primer equipo de rugby español que ha jugado una competición internacional europea"*). Then came "nine years of modest seasons without winning any title" (*"nueve años haciendo discretas temporadas y sin ganar ningún título"*), ended by a cup in 2010. In 2012, under a new play-off system, it won the league before a good crowd at the Pepe Rojo ground in Valladolid, led on the field by the New Zealander Dan Waenga; the next year its player-coach was an Argentine international; in 2013–14 it won the Supercopa, the cup and the league. It describes a side built on its own youth teams and "complemented with some foreign players, many of whom have ended up settling in Valladolid" (*"la complementa con algunos jugadores foráneos, muchos de los cuales han acabado estableciéndose en Valladolid"*). The city, it says, is recognised by many as "the capital of Spanish rugby" (*"la capital del rugby español"*).

The trophies that did not go to Valladolid went to Madrid and its edge (Canoe, Alcobendas, a club from Pozuelo), to Santboiana, which won the cup in 2000 and 2017 and two leagues between, to the Basque clubs Ordizia and Bera Bera, and once to La Vila. The Madrid halls of residence and faculties that had filled the finals of the 1970s were almost absent. Cisneros reached one final, in 2015, and lost it.

So when both Valladolid clubs reached the cup final of 2016, the city took it to the football stadium. The federation's history records the decision: seeing the feeling of that season, "the city bet on playing it in the José Zorrilla stadium" (*"la ciudad apostó por disputarla en el estadio José Zorrilla"*). "A historic day," it adds, "for what that full house in a big stadium represented" (*"Día histórico por lo que representaba ese lleno en un estadio grande"*). The press that weekend said no king had watched rugby in Spain for generations, and could not agree on how long; readers of this chapter have seen Alfonso XIII at the Metropolitano in 1923. The next year's final, the local press records, was played at the Zorrilla too.

### Rio

There was one other place where the slump did not reach. Rugby sevens had been voted into the Olympic Games for 2016, and at the end of **March 2010** the federation presented a plan for it at the sports council in Madrid. Its bulletin of the 29th headed the news "Sevens takes its first step towards Rio de Janeiro" (*"El rugby a 7 da su primer paso hacia Río de Janeiro"*). The government put in 240,000 euros at once. "In a period of crisis," the secretary of state for sport said, "we could have waited until 2012" (*"en periodo de crisis, podíamos haber esperado a 2012"*). The best sixteen men and the best sixteen women were to have a scholarship contract for three months of each year. "There is no doubt," Mandado said, "that if the resources are maintained the results will come" (*"No cabe duda de que si los recursos se mantienen los resultados llegarán"*).

Six years later both sides had one tournament left in which to qualify. The men's was at Monaco on **18 and 19 June 2016**, with a single Olympic place for the winner. Spain beat Ireland 12–7, beat Russia 17–12, and in the final met Samoa. It trailed 19–17 with time up. A try in the last play of the match made it **22–19**. "I am still trying to take it in," one of the players, Ángel López, said. "This is a dream for us. We are going to be Olympians." A week later, in Dublin, the women won their own last-chance tournament, beating Russia 19–12 in the final with two tries by Patricia García.

At Rio the women played the first match of the first Olympic sevens tournament, against France on 6 August 2016, and García took the first kick-off. They finished seventh, beating Fiji 21–0 on the way. The men, captained by Matías Tudela, finished tenth. The federation dates the professional era of sevens in Spain from those Games.

The women's fifteen had a harder generation than the sevens. Spain staged the Women's World Cup in Barcelona in May 2002, sixteen teams, and finished eighth. It had been playing since 2000 in the women's counterpart of the Six Nations, and by the federation's account had its best placing there in its second season. Then, in the federation's telling, the organisers decided that from 2007 the women's tournament would be played by the same countries as the men's, "and excluded Spain" (*"y excluyó a España"*). The side went on playing in Europe; in 2011, when the European women's championship was held in Galicia, it beat France 12–11 on the way to winning its group.

### What this generation leaves behind

Spain missed four World Cups in this generation, and each can be accounted for. In 2003 it lost on the field, was put back by a ruling against Russia, and lost again. In 2007 it climbed from the bottom of Europe through twelve matches and fell at the last two, and watched Portugal go instead. In 2011 and 2015 the table decided, and the one season in which Spain finished second fell between the two.

The game at home had a capital again, as it had in Barcelona in the 1950s and among Madrid's colleges in the 1970s, and this time it was a city in Castile whose two clubs had started in schools. The federation's presidents came from Pontevedra, Valladolid and San Sebastián. One of them, by his successor's account, left a deficit of a million and a half euros and the auditors' doubt that the federation could go on. The successor called it a vehicle without fuel.

And yet the generation's great days all came in 2016, within ten weeks: a football stadium filled for a club final in April, and two Olympic places won in June. The national side of a federation that could barely pay its way was third in the European championship in 2015 and again in 2017.

In February 2018 that side won in Russia and then beat Romania 22–10 in Madrid. "Spain," ran the headline in *El Español*, "caresses the Rugby World Cup for the first time in 20 years" (*"acaricia el Mundial de Rugby por primera vez en 20 años"*). In March it beat Germany 84–10. One match remained, on **18 March 2018**, in Brussels.

## Generation 6: Twice Disqualified (2018–2026)

**18 March 2018.** The ground was the Petit Heysel, beside the King Baudouin stadium in Brussels, and the arithmetic was simple. Spain had won in Russia, beaten Romania in Madrid and put eighty-four points on Germany. If it beat Belgium, it would go to the World Cup in Japan, into Ireland's pool. If it lost, the place would go to Romania.

The referee was a Romanian, Vlad Iordăchescu, and so were both his assistants. Rugby Europe, which said it had made all its appointments before the championship began, through an independent committee, had named them. Spain said it had asked on 19 February for a change and been refused. The Spanish camp, one Madrid paper noted, had gone on insisting beforehand that the referee was an honest man.

Belgium won **18–10**. At the final whistle, the reports of the day agree, Spanish players surrounded the referee. "They did not let us play," the coach, **Santiago Santos**, said afterwards; "the referee cut our play at every moment" (*"No nos han dejado jugar, en todo momento el árbitro nos cortaba el juego"*). The captain, **Jaime Nava**, said: "I do not want to think that rugby has prostituted itself to leave us out. When they assigned us the Romanian referees it struck us, but this is rugby, certain values are presumed" (*"No quiero pensar que el rugby se ha prostituido para dejarnos fuera. Cuando nos asignaron los árbitros rumanos, nos chocó, pero esto es rugby, se presuponen unos valores"*). Those were the words of the losing side that afternoon. No body that examined the match afterwards is recorded as finding anything against the referee, and the result was never set aside.

That afternoon Spain still expected to reach Japan the long way, through a play-off. What it did not yet know was that the match in Brussels would turn out not to matter. To see how a side lost one World Cup place, and its last road to another, on points taken away after the matches were played, begin with what happened after the whistle.

### Forty Points

Two processes followed, and they ran in different directions.

The first was about the match. World Rugby, as the International Board had been called since 2014, said the next day that it did not appoint officials for the European championship but was "in contact with Rugby Europe to understand the context of events". The Spanish federation sent it a video of nineteen refereeing decisions it disputed. On **17 April** the suspensions imposed by Rugby Europe's judicial committee, chaired by the Scot Lorne Crerar, were made known. Five Spanish players were banned for what had happened at the end of the match: Sébastien Rouet for forty-three weeks and Guillaume Rouet for thirty-six, for what the committee recorded as physical abuse of a match official; Mathieu Bélie for fourteen weeks, for threatening actions or words; and Pierre Barthère and Lucas Guillaume for fourteen weeks each, the reports giving no separate description of their offences. The federation said it was studying appeals with the players. Santos would say that summer that it was "something completely unheard of that the only people sanctioned in this whole mess are the Spanish players" (*"es algo totalmente inaudito que los únicos sancionados de todo este lío sean los jugadores españoles"*).

The second process was about who had been entitled to play at all. An independent committee looked at the two seasons of the qualifying table and gave its decision on **15 May 2018**. On the match, it refused the request, made by World Rugby and by Spain, to set aside the result, and "determined that the match should not be replayed"; *El Español*'s account of the decision has it recognising, even so, the conflict of interest created by appointing a Romanian referee to that fixture. On eligibility, it found that three of the six unions had fielded players who were not entitled to play for them: Belgium on seven occasions, Romania on eight, and Spain "on 9 occasions during the 2017 and 2018 Rugby Europe Championships (of which 8 matches related to Rugby World Cup 2019 qualifying)".

Spain's two players were **Bastien Fuster** and the same Mathieu Bélie, in a matter quite separate from his suspension. Both had played for France's under-20 side, in 2008 and 2012 by *El Español*'s summary of the written decision, and those appearances, the committee held, tied them to France. The Spanish federation's case was that France had not informed the players, and it said it had sought clearance from the French federation; "the (eligibility) rule is confusing," its statement ran, "and World Rugby should have created a procedure". The committee accepted that the unions "had not acted in bad faith". It applied the penalty all the same: five points for every qualifying match in which an ineligible player had appeared. Spain lost **forty**. Belgium and Romania lost thirty each. Each union was also fined, the fines suspended; Spain's was 50,000 pounds. Romania, which had taken the place in March, was out. Russia went to Japan as Europe's first qualifier, and Germany took Spain's place in the play-off.

Spain appealed, and its president, Alfonso Feijoo, went to London to argue the case. On **6 June 2018** the appeal committee dismissed the appeal "on each ground". It added that replaying the match in Brussels "would have proved academic in light of the points deduction". And in explaining why points had to be deducted, it invoked what it called a "long-established and consistent principle (including in 2003 and 2019 RWC qualifiers)". The one case of 2003 this chapter has told is Russia's, removed from the qualifiers for that World Cup after a complaint by the Spanish federation. Whether the committee meant that case it did not say.

So the World Cup of 2019 was missed. The federation's yearbook enters 2018 as a third place in Europe and says nothing of the deduction.

### Second in Europe

Santos stayed. In 2019 Spain was second in the European championship, beating Russia and Romania in Madrid. In 2020 it was second again. It won in Montevideo in 2019 and again in 2020, against the Uruguay it had not been able to beat in the year of its own World Cup, and lost the second Test of 2020 there five days later.

Qualifying for the tournament of 2023, in France, ran over the championships of 2021 and 2022 together. Spain began with three defeats, to Georgia, Romania and Portugal. Then it started winning. It beat Russia 49–12 in November 2021 and won 52–7 in Amsterdam a week before Christmas. That month one of its locks, **Kawa Leauma**, thirty-two years old, died of head injuries after a fall in the Netherlands. In February 2022 Spain beat the Netherlands again, won 41–37 in Russia and beat Romania 38–21 in Madrid.

On **13 March 2022**, a sunny Sunday morning at the Campo Central in Madrid, more than six thousand people watched it play Portugal for the place. The hooker Marco Pinto scored two tries in the first half. Portugal, in the words of one report, "threw everything at the Spaniards in the closing stages". Spain held on, **33–28**, and the crowd invaded the pitch. "This one," Pinto said, "I dedicate to our brother Kawa Leauma, who died wanting to play with us." Spain had qualified as Europe's second team, for the first time since December 1998 and without a fourteen-man escape. Its pool had South Africa, Ireland and Scotland in it.

It lasted forty-six days.

### Ten Points

The second case concerned one player and two matches. **Gavin van den Berg**, born in South Africa, had appeared for Spain in both games against the Netherlands, on 18 December 2021 and 5 February 2022. Spain had fielded him on the basis of residence. The Romanian federation complained to World Rugby that he did not meet the requirement. The committee that heard the case imposed no sanction on the player, and the Spanish federation said there was no evidence that he had any part in what it alleged had been done to his papers.

On **28 April 2022** an independent judicial committee, chaired by Nigel Hampton QC, announced that Spain had "fielded an ineligible player during two Rugby World Cup 2023 qualification matches". The sanction was five points for each match, ten in all, a fine of 25,000 pounds, and the 50,000 pounds suspended four years earlier now to be paid. Ten points took Spain out of the qualifying places. "Romania will qualify as Europe 2 into Pool B replacing Spain," the announcement said, "and Portugal will replace Romania in the Final Qualification Tournament." Romania, that is, took Spain's place in South Africa's pool, and Portugal went to the last-chance tournament.

A legal commentary on the written decision gives the committee's reasoning. The player had been out of Spain for more than a hundred days in one of the years in question, and he told the hearing that it was only at the end of 2019 or the start of 2020 that he had begun to make Spain his home. The federation had not interviewed him before picking him. "In that sense," the committee is quoted as saying, "the FER is the author of its own misfortune."

What the federation said next is an allegation, and it has to be read as one. Its vice-president told Reuters the day after the ruling that the copy of the player's passport supplied to the federation had been altered, that members of the Alcobendas club had changed the dates of exit and entry, that three of them had acknowledged it in writing, and that "there was no evidence the player was involved in falsifying the copy of the passport". The club told Reuters that "all those responsible have left the club", and that Van den Berg was not among those who had gone. The player said the stamps in the copy the federation had been given "did not match with the actual dates of my trip". On 9 August 2022 the Madrid public prosecutor filed a complaint alleging the falsification of an official document against a former president of the club, two former coaches and a former player. By the summer of 2026 no outcome of that complaint had been reported, and no court had found against anyone.

Feijoo spoke within a day of the ruling. "We have been deceived," he said, "and I had never thought that anyone could allegedly falsify a document in the world of rugby" (*"hemos sido engañados y nunca había pensado que alguien pudiera presuntamente falsificar un documento en el mundo del rugby"*). He called himself "the one responsible, but not the one to blame" (*"ser el responsable, pero no el culpable"*), and said he would go. The players issued a statement of their own the same day; the Valladolid paper that carried it headed its report with the players turning on Feijoo. One of them spoke of having had "our participation in the World Cup ruined twice" (*"arruinado dos veces nuestra participación en el Mundial"*).

The appeal was dismissed on 27 June 2022, and that decision was final. Feijoo resigned at the federation's assembly on 2 July. On 30 July **Juan Carlos Martín**, of Valladolid, was elected in his place with fifty of the sixty-seven votes. The federation had first expelled Alcobendas from the league; in August, the legal press reported, the government's sports tribunal annulled that, holding that the club was not answerable for what individuals had done. On 16 September, on a new file, the federation's disciplinary committee fined the club and relegated it to División de Honor B. The decision was open to appeal, and no outcome of one had been reported by the summer of 2026.

The World Cup of 2023 was played without Spain. The yearbook records why: a sanction, it says, "for an improper line-up in the match played against the Netherlands".

### The Road to Australia

Santos left in April 2023, by agreement with the federation, which sent him off as "the coach with the greatest number of victories in the history of the national side" (*"el entrenador con mayor número de victorias de la historia de la selección"*). That August Argentina beat Spain 62–3. On **2 November 2023** the federation presented his successor: **Pablo Bouza**, an Argentine and a former Puma, on a four-year contract whose stated object was the World Cup of 2027 in Australia. He came from the Uruguayan staff that had qualified for the World Cup in France.

The rules changed, and towards Spain. The World Cup was growing from twenty teams to twenty-four, and in the European championship of 2025 the top two sides in each pool would reach the semi-finals and, with them, the World Cup. Spain's pool had Georgia, the Netherlands and Switzerland in it. On 2 February 2025 Spain beat the Netherlands 53–24. On **9 February 2025**, away to Switzerland, it won **43–13**, and that was enough.

"Spain," World Rugby announced that day, "will take part in their second Men's Rugby World Cup, their first in the 21st century." More than twenty-five years had passed since Galashiels. By the summer of 2026 no ruling had touched the place.

What followed, to July 2026, was a run of results. Spain beat Portugal 42–31 in the semi-final of that championship and lost the final to Georgia, 46–28. In July 2025 it toured North America and beat the United States, 31–20, and Canada, 24–23, victories the federation called unprecedented; it recorded that Spain had risen to fifteenth in the world ranking, equal to the best position it had ever held. In November 2025 Fiji won 41–33 at Málaga. On **3 December 2025**, in Sydney, the draw placed Spain in Pool C with Argentina, Fiji and Canada, in a tournament to run from 1 October to 13 November 2027. In March 2026 Spain lost its European semi-final to Portugal, 26–7, and beat Romania 29–23 for third place. In July 2026, in the first window of World Rugby's new Nations Cup, it drew 42–42 with Canada in Edmonton, beat Tonga, and lost 29–22 to the United States.

At home the league final of 7 June 2026, at the Pepe Rojo, was won by the Valladolid Rugby Asociación Club against Burgos, 25–24, with a long penalty on the last play of the match. In June 2023 Madrid was awarded the final tournament of the world sevens series for three seasons from 2023–24. The women's fifteen, winners of seven European titles running to 2024, went to the World Cup in England in 2025 and lost its three matches, to New Zealand, Ireland and Japan.

### What this generation leaves behind

Twice in four years a ruling on who had played removed Spain from the road to a World Cup: in 2018 by a deduction that cost it its place in the play-off, and in 2022 after it had won its place on the field. The two cases were not alike. In 2018 the question was whether two men who had played for France's under-20s were tied to France, and the committee found against the federation while accepting that it had not acted in bad faith. In 2022 the question was where one man had been living; the federation itself said the document that answered it had been altered by others; and the committee's judgment on the federation, as a legal commentary quotes it, was that it was the author of its own misfortune. What the two had in common was the penalty: five points a match, taken from results won on the field.

The match that opened the generation was never set aside. The result in Brussels stood: Belgium 18, Spain 10.

And then Spain qualified. It qualified under an Argentine coach who had come from Uruguay, in a tournament enlarged, for the second time, just far enough to admit it, by beating Switzerland on a February afternoon.

The chapter began with a French game crossing the Pyrenees in a veterinary student's luggage. Between that and this lay a cup named for a dictator and renamed for a king, a game carried by Barcelona, then by Madrid's colleges, then by two school clubs in Castile, and one World Cup, reached with fourteen men.
