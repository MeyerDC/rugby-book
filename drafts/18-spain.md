# Spain

## Generation 0: Over the Pyrenees (1894–1928)

**February 1901. The Bonanova velodrome, Barcelona.**

The footballers have the ground. Players of the **Foot-Ball Club Barcelona** and of **Català FC** have come to the cycle track to play the game they are known for, and at some point in the afternoon they put the round-ball game aside and try another. The sports correspondent who wrote it up signed as "A Forward" (*Un Delantero*), and gave it a single line: some trials of rugby football were also held on the same ground (*"Efectuáronse asimismo en dicho terreno algunos ensayos de foot-ball Rugby"*).

The line names no score and no sides, and passes no verdict. It would be a footnote if not for who was on the ground.

### "Too Rough and Brutal"

In 1902 a profile of FC Barcelona's first and second teams set out where the club's men had learned their sport, and a surprising amount of what it listed was rugby. **Arthur Witty** had gone to school in Liverpool at eleven and played in its first XV from 1893 to 1895. **Jaime Alberto Eckes** had been at school in Liverpool in 1893. **Alfonso Albéniz** was "a member of Racing Club in Paris, where he has played rugby football" (*"miembro del 'Racing Club' de París en donde ha practicado el foot-ball rugby"*). **Cyril Alexander** had played in Scotland, and the profile was precise about it: "rugby only" (*"rugby solamente"*). The club's founder, **Joan Gamper**, had played at Lyon.

Gamper's Lyon year is the one the club itself tells. In the year before he founded FC Barcelona he joined **Union Athlétique de Lyon**, the town's rugby club, and took to a code he had never played. The paper *Lyon Sport* noticed, in the club's English translation: "Although he had only played association football … he quickly learned the rules of rugby football, contributing to our first team his excellent dribbling qualities. His kicks and dribbling have placed him among the top players in the region." By the club's own account he had walked into a feud: the French rugby union of the day banned anyone who played association football from rugby for a year. Gamper was not a specialist of any kind. Besides football, he did athletics, cycling, rugby and golf.

The rugby may even be on the shirt. Where FC Barcelona's blue and maroon came from is not certain, and the club itself backs, as a theory, the idea that they came from the rugby team of **Merchant Taylors' School** in Crosby, outside Liverpool, which Arthur Witty and his brother attended. The school has confirmed that blue and maroon was its rugby strip in the late nineteenth century.

So FC Barcelona was founded by a man who had played rugby for a season, in a side where rugby was part of the education of several of its first players, and it tried the game out at the Bonanova in 1901. It chose football. Nothing forced that choice, and for the next decade the Spanish press explained why the country was right to leave rugby alone.

The game was not unknown in print. As early as **1894** a professor of gymnastics, **José Esteban García Fraguas**, had described and illustrated its rules in a Spanish treatise. In **1903** there was talk in Barcelona of two sides from **Toulouse** coming to play a match at the Hipódromo, and of the Barcelona, Español and Hispania clubs raising a team to meet them. The same year a columnist gave the view that would keep recurring: neither the Spanish temperament nor the game's effect on the body made it worth acclimatising here (*"ni por nuestro temperamento ni por las condiciones higiénicas de ese juego, no creemos que convenga que aquí se juegue mucho ni se trate de aclimatar el tal Rugby"*).

The news from America did not help. In 1904 the Spanish press was reporting the casualties of the American college game, whose injured were not always told apart from rugby's. One report ran under the heading "Victims of football" (*"Víctimas del foot-ball"*): a student had lost his reason "from the blows he had taken" (*"a consecuencia de los golpes recibidos"*). Rugby had a defender that year, a writer signing as Huso, who granted that it was brutal and argued for it anyway on the grounds of what it replaced. Better, he thought, that the young spent their holidays at it than in the café, at the gambling table, at the bullfight or out carousing (*"Creo que es preferible esta distracción a pasar los días festivos en el café, en la timba, en los toros o de juerga"*).

Six years later Barcelona's own city council tried to stage it. For the **Mercè festival of 1910** the council planned a match between two French sides, to show the city "for the first time in Spain this exciting and evocative game" (*"por primera vez en España este emocionante y sugestivo juego"*). It never took place. Why is not known.

The rugby Spaniards did get to watch was played by the British. That same year, 1910, two teams from the English squadron played at the ground of the **Real Club Fortuna** in **Vigo**, the first match of its kind in the region. The report was unimpressed: the small crowd had not liked the new sport, finding it inelegant and very violent (*"Al público escaso que presenció la lucha no le gustó el nuevo deporte, por lo poco elegante y muy violento"*). What the match was good for, the reporter decided, was admiring "the composure and cold blood of Britain's sons" (*"la serenidad y la sangre fría de los hijos de la Gran Bretaña"*).

On **12 and 13 February 1911**, at **Monelos park in A Coruña**, the officers of two Royal Navy warships, the *Liverpool* and the *Gloucester*, who had been playing football against the **Real Club Deportivo**, put on a rugby match. The Coruña paper's verdict was the one this whole period deserves: the new sport had failed to win over the public. "It is too rough and brutal" (*"Resulta demasiado fuerte y brutal"*).

The report travelled. In Barcelona it was retold as a game of "trips, wallops and other barbarities" (*"zancadillas, trastazos y demás barbaridades"*), at the very moment a rugby team was being planned in the city, and the objection it met was the old one from 1903: the game was not made for the Spanish temperament.

By 1911, then, Spain had seen rugby played by its own footballers, by British sailors in two Galician ports, and on paper for seventeen years. FC Barcelona had been founded by a rugby player and may have been wearing a rugby school's colours. What it did not have was anyone who wanted to carry the game, and every time the idea came up, the same verdict put it down.

### The Veterinarian from Toulouse

On **16 October 1911**, eight months after the Coruña verdict, the veterinary school at Toulouse enrolled a seventeen-year-old from Sant Boi de Llobregat. His matriculation sheet survives, made out to Baudilio Aleu, the Castilian form of his name. His father had encouraged him to go, and the reason was a practical one. In that era, as his club would later put it, anyone who wanted to study veterinary medicine properly had to study at Toulouse, "the nearest city, and the focus of culture in southern Europe" (*"la ciutat més propera i focus de cultura al sud d'Europa"*).

Nobody sent **Baldiri Aleu Torres** to Toulouse for rugby. He found it there because, in Toulouse, the veterinary school was one of the places the game had come from. In 1899 a player named Coulon had broken away from the students' club, the Stade Olympien des Étudiants de Toulouse, to found the **Union Sportive de l'École Vétérinaire**, which played in grey shirts and black shorts. In 1905 it joined the Sport Athlétique Toulousain to form Véto-Sport. In May 1907 Véto-Sport merged with the students' club Coulon had left, and in 1908 the combined club took a name that was, in the words of the vets' own rugby association, easier to digest: **Stade Toulousain**. The vets did not vanish into it. Octave Léry, François Moura, François-Xavier Dutour and Pierre Jauréguy all came from the school, and they starred when the Stade became champion of France for the first time, in 1912, while Aleu was in his first year there.

His club believes that is where his enthusiasm came from: from the university matches, and from a Toulouse side that had grown out of student groups and would be, by the end of the 1910s, "a model for all of France" (*"un referent a tot França"*). The club says "probably" (*segurament*), and that is as far as the record goes. The Catalan encyclopedia is plainer: at Toulouse he learned the rules.

He finished in 1913 and moved on to Paris, to the veterinary school at Alfort, then did his practical training in Normandy during the Great War. After that he went home to Sant Boi to practise as a vet, and brought the game with him.

What he did with it first was talk. The café **Cal Ninyu** was the centre of the town's social life, and Aleu worked on the regulars of its conversation circle (*tertúlia*), praising a sport that, as his club's history puts it, was unknown in the country until then. His recruits were not students. Sant Boi's municipal register gives their trades: peasants, bricklayers, carpenters, mechanics, shopkeepers and industrialists, young men "of every stratum and social condition" (*"de todos los estratos y condición social"*). The pitch, as the club remembered it fifty years later, was that this was a game in which intelligence counted for "much more … than brute force" (*"mucho más … que la fuerza bruta"*). A decade of Spanish newspapers had called rugby brutal. The first man to carry it in Spain sold it as the opposite.

Training began in the first months of 1921, on a piece of ground on the bank of the Llobregat that the players turned into the **Camp del Riu**, the river ground. By **18 April 1921** the group was in the Barcelona press. *La Veu de Catalunya* called on anyone who wanted to try the new sport to join the twenty-five members already signed up to the Unió Esportiva Santboiana.

The man who would teach them the technique arrived by letter. On **12 May 1921** **Michel Reynard** wrote to Aleu that "many French, English and Spanish sportsmen" (*"muchos sportmen franceses, ingleses y españoles"*) wanted to play the oval game; the letter survives in Santboiana's archive. Reynard was born at Arles in 1890 and worked in Barcelona as an engineer for the Energía Eléctrica de Cataluña. Three days after the letter the two men met. On **15 and 16 May** Union Sportive Perpignan and Racing Club de Narbonne played two exhibition matches in Barcelona for the victims of the Great War, which the club remembers as being at Espanyol's ground. Aleu and his friends came away, in the club's words, enthused (*entusiasmats*). By club tradition they also came away with their colour. Perpignan's second strip was the blue of the uniforms of the soldiers who had gone to the war and died there, and Sant Boi chose blue in their memory. That too the club offers as probable, not certain.

From that meeting Aleu and Reynard became the two men at the centre of the club's organisation, Reynard as player, coach and administrator. A tribute in *La Jornada Deportiva* in 1923 would call Reynard Aleu's "fellow student" (*"compañero de estudios"*), and his principal mentor in the technique of the game. The club's own papers have them meeting for the first time that May, in Barcelona.

The club was incorporated on **4 July 1921**. Its founding act gave its object as "the practice and spread of amateur sport, especially rugby" (*"la pràctica i difusió de l'esport amateur, especialment el Rugby"*), and its first minute book opens the same day. The sport historian Xavier Torrebadella-Flix, who has gone back through the period's press, calls it the first sporting association in the country given over entirely to rugby. In September it formally leased its field. Aleu would be its president until 1936, and the ground was later named after him.

What the club lacked was anyone to play against. Its first match came on **13 November 1921**, against the **Club Natació Atlètic**. *La Jornada Deportiva* wished the beginners the reward their effort deserved, and looked forward to the day when the Estadi Català would see the embrace of the two Unions: Perpignan's, the champions of France, and Sant Boi's (in the paper's own forms, the *Unión Sportiva Perpignaise* and the *Unión Sportiva Samboyana*). After that match Aleu wrote in *La Veu de Catalunya* himself, calling for new clubs to be formed, so that Sant Boi would have rivals and the game could take root in Catalonia and in Spain. Sant Boi remembers a manifesto of his in the same paper, "About Rugby Football" (*"Entorn el Futbol-Rugby"*), and dates it to 1922; whether that was the same article is not settled.

The clubs came, and the men who ran them had learned the game where Aleu had. **David Cuyás**, who built the rugby section of the **Club Natació Barcelona**, the third club in Catalonia, founded at the end of 1921, had been educated in France, and had learned there to play and to referee. The men who taught on the field were French as well. When the first competition was played, in the spring of 1922, Santboiana fielded Reynard, Vinçon and Dubois. The Natació Atlètic had Renaud and Hoche, the Natació Barcelona had Lafitte, and the Catalunya Sporting Club had Ducrós and Julien.

On **2 May 1922**, on the central ground of the old Barcelona hippodrome, Santboiana beat the Club Natació Barcelona 3–0 to win the **Copa Real Sociedad de Carreras**, the first rugby competition in Catalonia. The three points were a single try, not converted, scored by Dubois, who survives in every account only as a French surname. *La Jornada Deportiva* reported that Santboiana had taken the trophy brilliantly (*brillantemente*).

Reviewing the year for *El año deportivo*, Lluís Gibert singled out the thing that most needed saying: "the influence French players have had on our rugby" (*"la influencia que los jugadores franceses han tenido en nuestro rugby"*). The first Catalan players, he wrote, would remember Dubois, Julien, Reynard, Lafitte and Vinçon forever, "and especially Reynard and Vinçon were the masters" (*"y especialmente Reynard y Vençon fueron los maestros"*).

The game that British sailors had shown to Galicia, and that Barcelona's footballers had tried and put down, had come back into Spain over the Pyrenees, carried by a vet from a river town and a handful of Frenchmen. Barcelona, which had turned it down for twenty years, now had clubs of its own playing it.

### Nineteen Teams

On **20 and 21 May 1923** the Catalan federation closed its season at the hippodrome of **Can Tunis**, the racecourse built by the Compañía Francesa de Carreras de Caballos. The rugby clubs played a great many of their matches there, because they had so few proper grounds of their own. That weekend the visitors were **Toulouse Lalande Olympique**. On the first day they played Santboiana; on the second they played a **Catalan selection**, with Baldiri Aleu in its side, and won 9–0. *La Jornada Deportiva* ran the result the next day under a plain headline: in the match against the Catalan selection, Lalande had triumphed 9 to 0. Four days later its correspondent Delsol came back to the two matches at the hippodrome with some further thoughts. His point was that the French had seen how far the Catalans had come, and across the border *La Dépêche*, *La France* and *L'Auto* all covered the matches.

Two years before that weekend there had been one club, in Sant Boi. By then there was a federation calling itself Spanish, a Spanish championship, and more teams than anyone had space for.

The federation came first, and it came from a swimming club. It was the **Club Natació Barcelona** that put it to the others that rugby needed an association to look after the game's interests beyond any one club, and on **13 April 1922** the **Federación Española de Rugby** was constituted in Barcelona. Five clubs made it: the Natació Atlètic, the Natació Barcelona, Santboiana, the Catalunya Sporting and a side called Universitary. **David Cuyás**, the Natació Barcelona's rugby man, was its first secretary. By the end of August its presidency was with **Jaime Camino** of the Natació Atlètic. It was a national body in name. In fact it was five clubs from Barcelona and Sant Boi.

Clubs kept coming. By the end of 1922, Torrebadella counts thirteen: Santboiana, the Natació Atlètic, the Sociedad Deportiva de Sant Andreu, the Catalunya Athlètic, the Natació Barcelona, the Catalunya Sporting, the Barcelona Internacional Rugby Club, the rugby section of the **CADCI**, the Olimpic Rugby Club, the Universitary, the Faculties of **Medicine** and **Pharmacy**, and the Agrupació Atlètica Vida. The two faculties met on **18 December 1922** in what Torrebadella records as the first school match. The CADCI was something else again. The **Centre Autonomista de Dependents del Comerç i de la Indústria** had been founded in 1903 by shop and office clerks of Catalanist leanings, and its rugby committee announced itself publicly in the Centre's magazine, *Acció*, in January 1923. Almost every one of these sides had a Frenchman to teach it, and the French coached for nothing: the former player Laurent Etxeverry took the CADCI, a man named Grenier ran Santboiana's forwards, Vida had three former French players, and Michel Reynard, the most prominent of them, coached Santboiana and the Catalan and university selections.

A Catalan federation followed the Spanish one, in late 1922 or early 1923, with **Cuyás** as its first president. It was already organising a match in December 1922.

The first **Campeonato de España**, the championship of Spain, was played at the start of **January 1923**, organised by the Spanish federation, and only Catalan clubs entered it. There was no one else to enter. As Torrebadella puts it, the championship of Catalonia was the championship of Spain as well, since there were no teams outside the city. Santboiana won it, beating the Catalunya Athlètic 13–3 in the final, and the Spanish federation counts that as the first title in the history of what became the King's Cup. By February 1923 the Spanish federation listed six affiliated clubs: Santboiana, the two Natació clubs, Sant Andreu, the CADCI and the Olimpic. In two years, Torrebadella writes, Barcelona rugby had laid "the first institutional stone of Spanish rugby" (*"la primera piedra institucional del rugby español"*).

Whose game was it? Torrebadella's answer is a class one. In a city where football was turning proletarian and drifting toward a much-criticised professionalism, rugby answered to a different model, one found among young men "of the petite bourgeoisie and the liberal professions" (*"de la pequeña burguesía y de las profesionales liberales"*), and it stood as a counterpoint. Pasarello, Pujadas and Martín, historians who worked from Santboiana's archive, draw the pioneers the same way: young, white, from comfortable families, men who had found their passion for the game while studying in France, working alongside Frenchmen who were making their careers in Barcelona. The faculty sides and the clerks of the CADCI fit that picture. The peasants and bricklayers whom Aleu had signed up in the café at Sant Boi fit it less well, yet all of them played in the same competitions.

There was a politics to it too. After the Lliga Regionalista's campaign for an autonomy statute in 1918 and 1919, Torrebadella writes, Catalan sporting associations began to take on a Catalanist sign that within a few years had turned openly to the idea of independence, and in 1922 that sharper politics found two new parties, **Acció Catalana** and Francesc Macià's **Estat Català**. Rugby, in Pasarello, Pujadas and Martín's reading, took on a marked Catalanist tone from the start. They note that Perpignan, the French champions whose visit had enthused Aleu, carried the Catalan flag on their crest, and that this was valued as a symbol of pan-Catalanism. The CADCI's politics would in time cost it some of its sports sections, its first rugby team among them. The specialist press, meanwhile, *La Jornada Deportiva*, *El Mundo Deportivo*, *El Campeón*, *Stadium* and *L'Esport Català*, gave the new game its unconditional support.

It did not always get a game worth supporting. In April 1923 the Copa Real Sociedad de Carreras was played for a second time, and *Aire Libre*'s account of the final that spring was of a match in which the tension got to the players and there were moments of marked violence. The magazine's rugby writer, signing as "Drop-Goal", took it seriously. Brutal play, or at least violent play, had always been the greatest enemy of a strong and manly sport like rugby (*"El juego brutal, o cuando menos violento, ha sido siempre el mayor enemigo que ha tenido un deporte fuerte y viril como es el rugby"*), and if it went unchecked the city would see "the sport we love disappear from our sporting world" (*"desaparecer de nuestro mundo deportivo el deporte de nuestros amores"*). It was the old verdict of 1911, this time from the game's own side.

The weekend at Can Tunis closed that season, and the next one began at once. Six more clubs took up the game in 1923: the Unió Deportiva de Sants, the Club Deportiu Europa, the Helènic, a Català Rugby Club, and the two great football clubs of the city, the **Real Club Deportiu Espanyol** and **Fútbol Club Barcelona**, twenty-two years after its footballers had tried the game at the Bonanova and put it down. By the end of 1923, Torrebadella concludes, Barcelona had nineteen rugby teams, clubs or sections. In August Santboiana and the Natació Barcelona took the game to **Valencia** for two promotional matches, refereed by Cuyás, by then president of both the Catalan and the Spanish federations, and at the Tona town festival the Natació Barcelona played the CADCI. Not everyone liked the summer fixtures. In a piece in *La Veu de Catalunya* titled "An outrage against sportsmanship" (*"Un atentat de lesa esportivitat"*), the chairman of the CADCI's rugby committee, R. Sarsanedas, objected to playing in the middle of August. In the 1923–24 season the Catalan championship had seven teams in it, Espanyol among them.

By then the game had gathered in the best-known sporting clubs of Barcelona, which Torrebadella calls the most football-minded and sporting city in Spain, and those clubs had promised to spread it to other Spanish cities. The Spanish federation, its championship and nearly all of its players were still in Barcelona.

### The King at the Metropolitano

On **20 May 1923**, the Sunday on which Toulouse Lalande Olympique played Santboiana at Can Tunis, Madrid was shown the game. The match was staged at the **Stadium Metropolitano**, a ground so new it had only just opened, and it was played on grass, before about 10,000 people. The sides were French, **Biarritz Olympique** and the **Stadoceste Tarbais**, brought down to show the game, and the referee was the Spanish federation's president that spring, **Jaime Camino**. **Alfonso XIII** was there. The king was no stranger to the game; he had watched it several times in London and in the French Basque Country, and the following spring he would be photographed at Bordeaux, watching Stade Toulousain play Perpignan in the final of the French championship. Torrebadella calls him a very special spectator at the great rugby matches of those years, when he was going to more and more sporting events.

The match was promotion, and it was business too. The company that owned the Metropolitano had its own reasons for putting it on: it thought rugby might come to offer as much spectacle as football. The ground had been prepared for it by the press. On 29 April the Madrid daily *La Libertad* had announced a campaign to introduce the game (*"campaña de iniciación"*), with training sessions so that the first matches against Spanish teams could be played that autumn (*"para poder efectuar los primeros encuentros con equipos nacionales en el próximo otoño"*). Madrileños who wished to take part were warned that the game called for a complete physical development, and were asked to send their names in writing to Joaquín Álvarez Puebla.

More matches followed at the same ground on 31 May and on 2 and 3 June, and the king came to those as well. This time the Catalans were invited: the Natació Atlètic and the Natació Barcelona played, along with Biarritz and a Basque-French selection. The king's presence was what Madrid read about. It was reported prominently in the news dailies and in the illustrated and society magazines of the capital, and in Torrebadella's reading that gave Madrid rugby, from its first weeks, a marked distinction of social class. In Barcelona the game had been played by clerks, students, professional men and Sant Boi's bricklayers. In Madrid it arrived in the society pages.

Not every reviewer was won over. Some wrote that the game could never take root in Spain because of "the dominant impulsive character of the Spanish man" (*"el dominante carácter impulsivo del hombre español"*). Ten days after the first match, in *Vida financiera* of 30 May, Gómez Puig put his own doubts in writing: "this game demands an absolute command of the nerves" (*"Este juego requiere un dominio absoluto de los nervios"*), a firm equanimity, a constant thinking about the play that forgets what happened in the last movement before starting the next. Spanish players, he predicted, would end up at the police station before the match was over, "though not before giving a real show on the field" (*"no sin antes dar un verdadero espectáculo en el campo"*). Even so, he conceded, "Madrid's first rugby team is already being formed" (*"se está ya formando el primer equipo madrileño de rugby"*).

The French coast was sending the game in by a second road. On 24 June 1923, to open the Estadio de Berazubi at **Tolosa** in Guipúzcoa, players from Boucau, Biarritz, Bayonne and Hendaye put on an exhibition. At **San Mamés** in Bilbao, on 25 May 1924, the Association Sportive Bayonnaise played the Stade Hendayais in a benefit for the widow and daughter of the footballer Pichichi. Bilbao's writers were polite and unmoved: they loved football like nobody else, and did not believe rugby could ever interest them (*"somos amantes, como ninguno del fútbol; pero jamás creemos que pueda interesarnos el rugby"*), and most of the crowd at San Mamés, they added, agreed. Rugby, Torrebadella concludes, did not really begin in Basque sport until the 1930s.

In Madrid the game found a home in the football clubs, though not among their footballers. By the autumn of 1924 a team had formed of English enthusiasts and some "Britannicised Spaniards" (*"españoles 'britanizados'"*). At the Athletic Club the game was taught by a young Czech, Bereny. By 18 December 1924 the **Sociedad Gimnástica Española** had a team of its own, formed with the idea of "nationalising rugby" (*"nacionalizar el rugby"*), of making the game Spanish. By the end of that year four Castilian clubs had teams: **Real Madrid**, **Athletic de Madrid**, the Gimnástica, and the **Academia de Infantería** at Toledo. Real Madrid's rugby side, which went by the name of Britania R.F.C., played its first match, an exhibition, at the Metropolitano on 10 January 1925, and beat the Athletic 27–0, thanks to the play of its foreign members. It was Real Madrid's in name only, Torrebadella notes: most of its players were foreigners.

The Toledo side came out of the army. Outside Catalonia, the first Spaniards to try the game for themselves were at the **Escuela Central de Gimnasia**, the army's central gymnastics school at Toledo, which ran from 1919 to 1936 and was attached to the infantry academy there until 1930. The academy's cadets played the Athletic Club de Madrid in their first match, beat them, and proclaimed themselves champions of the Centro, as *Armas y Deportes* reported in 1925. The school took the game seriously enough to print its own rulebook, *Foot-ball Rugby: Reglamento*, at Toledo in 1927.

Madrid now had clubs, and it wanted a body of its own. On 7 December 1925 delegates of the Gimnástica and the Agrupación Deportiva Ferroviaria, the railwaymen's sports club, met at the Gimnástica's rooms, with the Catalan referee Carmelo Davalillos Artigas present, and founded the **Federación de Centro de Rugby**. Its president was Ángel Leoz Cendoya, president of the Ferroviaria. Its technical director was **Dr Enrique Gutiérrez**, a former captain of the French national side and a member of the French selection committee. The Spanish federation's own history describes the new body as meeting the needs of the clubs of the centre of the peninsula. Early in 1926, as the *Heraldo de Madrid* reported, **Baldiri Aleu** was named president of the Spanish federation and its seat fixed in Barcelona. At the end of March the whole committee of the Centro federation resigned, and its new president was Fernando López-Quesada y Bourbón, a former Real Madrid footballer. That year the Centro federation also made **Captain José Hermosa Gutiérrez**, a sports teacher at the gymnastics school in Toledo, president of its referees' committee.

On the evening of Thursday **13 May 1926**, at Sant Boi de Llobregat, the Toledo cadets, champions of Castile, met **FC Barcelona**, champions of Catalonia, in the final of the Campeonato de España. It was the federation's second championship, the first since January 1923, and the first final in which a side from outside Catalonia played. David Cuyás of the Natació Barcelona refereed, and Barcelona won 19–0. In Barcelona it was read as more than a match. *L'Esport Català* of 17 May wrote of a team that "united the aspirations of all Catalans around a sporting supremacy" (*"hermanaba las aspiraciones de todos los catalanes alrededor de una supremacía deportiva"*). For Torrebadella it was a symbolic triumph for Catalan nationalism: behind FC Barcelona's victory stood a victory for Catalonia.

The cadets did not play on. After their success, as Hermosa told it in *Gran Sport* in October 1930, the unexpected happened, and the team was dissolved. Some of the academy's superiors and professors considered rugby "barbarous" (*bárbaro*), "though there had not been a single serious injury" (*"a pesar de no haber ocurrido ni una lesión de importancia"*). "But those gentlemen," Hermosa wrote, "thought of rugby what was said of football twenty years ago" (*"pero esos señores pensaban del Rugby lo mismo que hace veinte años se decía del fútbol"*).

The break between the two cities came over a match with France. On **19 February 1927**, after disagreements over organising a Spain–France fixture, the Centro federation separated from the Spanish federation and set up the **Rugby Unión Española**, organised and presided over by Gutiérrez with the backing of Manuel Ordóñez, and it meant to ask for the title of Royal (*Real*). The Madrid clubs' own argument, in the federation's later history, was that they needed more attention. Barcelona's press saw it otherwise. It accused Madrid's federation men of doing nothing to spread the game and pursuing only their private interests, of going about "sowing rancour and intrigue" (*"sembrando rencores e intrigas"*). The planned match, against a French side far stronger in body and in technique, was a reckless escapade (*calaverada*), an unnecessary display of French power, a disaster (*descalabro*) that would ruin everything Spanish rugby had achieved.

The match went ahead at the Metropolitano on **26 May 1927**. The Spanish side was picked by the Centro federation alone; the Spanish federation's own history counts a single player from the Catalan federation among them. It had no sanction from the Spanish federation. The pro-government *La Nación*, the daily that served Primo de Rivera, regretted that for reasons of his diary, "this first international match cannot be presided over, as it otherwise would have been, by the first sportsman of Spain" (*"este primer encuentro internacional no podrá ser presidido, como lo hubiera sido en otro caso, por el primer deportista español"*). It meant the king. France won 66–6. The Madrid press still called it the first official match of the Spanish national side, and the criticism of the "Spanish selection" (*"Selección española"*), Torrebadella writes, was unsparing.

Torrebadella reads the whole quarrel as politics carried on in sport. The rivalry between Barcelona and Madrid, he writes, lay beyond sport itself, and rugby reflected the schism between two nationalisms. In his account Primo de Rivera's dictatorship obstructed Catalan rugby and supported Castilian rugby, and the Castilian leaders who formed the Centro federation meant to sustain the dictatorship's ideological frame: "the Castilian Spanishisation of rugby" (*"la españolización castellana del rugby"*), which required disarming the power of a federation held mainly by Catalan players. Captain Hermosa, as a sports teacher at the army school, was in his reading one of those charged with the nationalisation of sport.

Aleu, unable even to attempt a mediation, resigned. In **January 1928** a new Spanish federation was constituted in Madrid, merging the directorates of the Rugby Unión Española and the old federation, with Enrique Gutiérrez as its president. It kept the old name, Federación Española de Rugby, and its seat moved from Barcelona to Madrid. The reconciliation was sealed at the Metropolitano, with a match for the Copa Lord Derward between selections of Castile and Catalonia. The Catalans won 17–0. The quarrel had cost the championship three seasons: the Campeonato de España was not played from 1926–27 to 1928–29, and when it came back in 1929–30, FC Barcelona beat Real Madrid 39–3 in the final. Five years after the king first watched it on the Metropolitano's grass, the game Barcelona had made was run from Madrid.

### What this generation leaves behind

Spanish rugby came out of its first generation marked by the road it had taken in. British sailors had played it in two Galician ports and been told it was too rough. Barcelona's footballers had tried it and chosen football. It took root only when it came back over the Pyrenees with a vet from Sant Boi, and Frenchmen taught it for nothing. Even the man now at the head of the federation had captained France.

What the generation built was a federation with two homes, and a quarrel that each city told its own way. Five clubs had founded it in Barcelona and called it Spanish from its first day. Madrid's clubs had broken away from it saying they needed more attention, and one of them had formed its team to make the game Spanish. Barcelona's press called the Madrid men intriguers, and wrote of FC Barcelona's 1926 champions as a team that had united the aspirations of all Catalans.

The settlement of January 1928 did not end the quarrel. The federation's own list of its presidents has Enrique Gutiérrez at its head, from Madrid, in 1928, and Baldiri Aleu back in the chair, from Barcelona, in 1929. For the next few years the two cities would pass the federation back and forth. Spanish rugby still had to decide whether it belonged to the city that had made it or to the capital that now ran it.

### The Settlement

On the morning of **Easter Sunday, 8 April 1928**, the Côte d'Argent committee, acting for the French federation, gave a reception in the salons of the Hôtel de Bordeaux for the visitors from Spain, the French players and the officials. The Spanish consul raised a glass, and so did the president of the Spanish federation, **Dr Enrique Gutiérrez**. They drank to Franco-Spanish friendship, to "the king of sports" (*"au sport-roi"*), and to the greater growth of Spanish rugby. Gutiérrez was among people who knew him. He had captained France, and *La Petite Gironde* called him an old boy of the Bordeaux-Étudiants-Club (*"un ancien du Bordeaux-Étudiants-Club"*). Three months after the settlement that had made him president, he had brought a Spanish side to his old club's city.

The settlement had moved the federation's seat to Madrid and put a Madrid man at its head. The side it sent to Bordeaux came from Barcelona. Three days before the match Manuel Ordóñez, who had backed Gutiérrez's breakaway the year before, previewed the trip in *Heraldo Deportivo*. His headline was about another match, the reconciliation at the Metropolitano in March: "The Catalan selection beats the Madrid one by 17 points to 0" (*"La Selección catalana vence a la madrileña 17 puntos a 0"*). The national side came further down. "We can state," he wrote, "that the Spanish national selection that will represent us on 8 April at Bordeaux will be made up of all the Catalans, with the Madrid man Vázquez included in the side" (*"Podemos asegurar que la selección nacional española que nos representará el 8 de abril en Burdeos estará formada por la totalidad de los catalanes, incluyéndose en la formación al madrileño Vázquez"*).

A year earlier it had been the other way round. The side Madrid picked for the Metropolitano in May 1927, the federation's history records, was all from the Centro federation, with only one player from the Catalan federation. Now the Catalans made up the side, and Madrid had one man in it. The federation's register of capped players shows who went. Thirteen men won their first cap on 8 April 1928: José Aguilar Cortiella, Domingo Aiximeno Moyano, Rafael Artigas, José Baides Facios, Vicente Bisbal Climent, Juan Blanquet Fondevilla, Amadeo Blasco Yus, Fernando Carreras Parset, Jaime Garrigosa Julia, Manuel Isart Subirach, Rafael Rues, Eduardo Ruiz Méndez, and the one Madrid man, **Ricardo Vázquez Granjell**. Spain had played only one Test before, so the other two of the fifteen had played in the 66–6 of 1927. The register does not say which two.

The Madrid press had called the 1927 match the first official match of the Spanish national side. When the federation came to write its own history, it gave the title to Bordeaux instead: "First official match of the Spanish men's national side, 1928" (*"Primer partido oficial de la Selección española masculina – 1928"*). So Spanish rugby ended up with two first official matches, a year apart. The first was picked in Madrid without the federation's sanction. The second had the sanction of a federation now seated in Madrid, and was played by Barcelona's men.

The opponent was not France, as in 1927, but its second string. *La Petite Gironde* described "a France B team made up of our international hopefuls" (*"une équipe de France B composée de nos espoirs internationaux"*). They played in the afternoon at the Parc des Sports at Lescure, in ideal weather, before 10,000 people. The paper thought that a very big crowd for Easter, when the townspeople usually left the city. France B won by 53 points to 5, with thirteen tries, five goals and a drop goal. Spain defended well, for a time with fourteen men after Aiximeno left the field. Their one score came with France 45 points up. A Spanish dribbling rush broke through the French defence, and the captain, Blanquet, scored the try and converted it himself. It was "wildly applauded" (*"follement applaudi"*).

What was certain, the paper's critic wrote, was that "the Spanish players have been brought up in a good school. They know only what is good and attractive in rugby" (*"les joueurs espagnols sont élevés à bonne école. Ils ne connaissent du rugby que ce qu'il contient de bon, de joli"*). They had been taught to open the play and keep the ball moving, and kept away from the tricks and the dirty play that France's best players could not resist. "So they opened their eyes wide in astonishment when they found themselves jostled in the line-outs" (*"C'est ainsi qu'ils ouvraient de grands yeux étonnés lorsqu'ils se voyaient bousculés dans les touches"*). Only Blanquet, the captain, had a thorough knowledge of the game, and he had played in leading French sides. The forwards had the most to learn: "rarely were all eight of them together to go into a scrum" (*"rarement ils se trouvèrent les huit pour se mettre en mêlée"*). Behind the scrum, Vázquez on the wing and Bisbal in the centre could not be shaken off by the men opposite them, and Garrigosa and Blanquet, taking turns at full-back, tackled well. The Spaniards had a great deal to learn, the critic concluded, "but in the unanimous opinion of those who saw them last year, they have made great progress" (*"mais de l'avis unanime de ceux qui les ont vus l'an passé, ils sont très en progrès"*). The side seen the year before had been Madrid's. Thirteen of the men at Lescure had never played for Spain.

The match report was signed by G. Chappert, and he placed the visitors among the game's new arrivals: "like the Italians, the Spaniards, newcomers to rugby" (*"comme les Italiens, les Espagnols, nouveaux venus au rugby"*). He also wrote that the subjects of Alfonso XIII had played the game for only two years. They had played it for longer, since Santboiana was founded in 1921. He claimed a kinship too. The Spaniards' qualities, he wrote, were those "we find in our Basques and our Catalans, still closer brothers of the Iberian Basques and Catalans" (*"que nous trouvons chez nos Basques et nos Catalans, frères encore plus proches des Basques et des Catalans ibériques"*). The game had reached Barcelona over the Pyrenees, and Chappert saw the tie running back the same way: the Spaniards in front of him were kin to France's own Catalans.

The settlement's first season gave half an answer to the question of who owned Spanish rugby. The federation's office sat in Madrid, under a president from Madrid, and its national side was Barcelona's. On the ground the two cities were close to level: by the end of 1928, Torrebadella counts six established rugby clubs in Barcelona and five in Madrid. The only Test Spain had played at home was still Madrid's, the 66–6 against France that the federation had never sanctioned.
