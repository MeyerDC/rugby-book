# South Africa

## Generation 0: Winchester Football and the Two Beginnings (1861–1889)

Two churchmen. Two schools. Two beginnings, two years apart.

That's the thing nobody tells you, and it's where I want to start — because if you only know the mainstream story, you know one churchman and one school, and the other one has been quietly deleted from the national memory for a hundred and sixty years.

---

### The wrong game

**1861.** A clergyman from Wiltshire named **George Ogilvie** — born 1826, known to everyone as **"Gog"** — arrives in Rondebosch to take up the headmastership of the **Diocesan College**. Bishops. He'll stay until 1885.

And he introduces football to South Africa.

Except — and this is the first great joke of our rugby history — **it isn't rugby.**

The game Gog taught was **Winchester College football**, learned at his old school in Hampshire. A handling game, yes, but not the Rugby School code. A different beast entirely, with its own laws, its own shape, its own culture.

**23 August 1862. Green Point Common, Cape Town.**

The **Officers of the Army** against the **Gentlemen of the Civil Service**.

**Nil–nil.**

That is the first recorded football match in South African history, and it was a scoreless draw in a game that no longer exists, played by soldiers of the garrison and colonial bureaucrats, on a common in Cape Town.

*(Some sources say 21 August, some say 23 August. The 23rd is better attested. And note the opponents — **the Army**. British garrisons, playing to keep fit and keep morale, are one of the great vectors of this game across the entire Empire, and you'll remember they turn up in Buenos Aires and Valparaíso too.)*

And then the young gentlemen of Cape Town piled in, and the local press started reporting scratch matches with names that tell you *exactly* what kind of society this was: **Town versus Suburbs. Civil Servants versus All Comers.** And, best of all — **Home versus Colonial-born.**

*Home versus Colonial-born.* The Englishmen against the men born here. In 1860s Cape Town, that was the fault line that mattered, and they made a rugby fixture out of it.

---

### The war between the codes

Here is the part that will genuinely surprise you: **rugby nearly died in South Africa before it ever really began.**

Around **1875**, the actual Rugby School code starts being played in the Cape Colony. That same year the first club is formed — **Hamilton**, in Sea Point. And Hamilton, initially, **played the Winchester game.**

By 1876, Hamilton and **Villagers** are the two leading clubs — and they're playing **Winchester football** against each other, not rugby.

And by the late 1870s, rugby football was **battling to survive** against the Winchester game. The Western Province club had **ceased to exist entirely** through lack of support.

In about 1877, the game that would become the most powerful cultural force in white South African life was **losing** — and losing to a version of football played nowhere else on earth, taught by a schoolmaster from Hampshire.

There is a South Africa in which rugby simply never takes, and Gog Ogilvie's Winchester game becomes the national sport, and there are no Springboks at all.

---

### The man who saved it

And then, in **1878**, a man steps off a boat in Cape Town.

**William Henry Milton.**

He is a **former England back**, capped in **1874 and 1875** — a real, recent international, arriving in a colonial backwater where the football fraternity is still arguing about which game to play.

He joins **Villagers**, and he starts, in the lovely phrase the histories use, **playing and preaching the rugby code.**

*Preaching.*

**By the end of that single year, Cape Town had all but abandoned the Winchester game in favour of rugby.**

One man. One season. That is how close it was, and that is how fast it turned.

And here's the coda that makes Milton a very South African figure indeed. He didn't stay a rugby man. **He became a colonial administrator** — a major one — and his career runs straight through the machinery of imperial expansion in southern Africa, into the orbit of Cecil Rhodes and the settlement of the territory that became Rhodesia. There's a doctoral thesis about him whose title says it all: *Sir William Milton: A Leading Figure in Public School Games, Colonial Politics and Imperial Expansion.*

The man who gave South Africa rugby also helped give Britain a colony.

**The game and the Empire arrived in the same suitcase.** They always did — you've seen it in Montevideo, in Buenos Aires, in Valparaíso. But nowhere else is the link *quite* this literal.

---

### The Boers take it

**1883. Stellenbosch.**

A rugby club is formed in what the sources describe as a **predominantly Boer farming district** outside Cape Town.

And the game is **enthusiastically adopted by the young Boer farmers.**

Now — you know what Stellenbosch becomes. Every South African does. But hold on to how *strange* this is at the moment it happens.

This is an **English** game, brought by an **English** schoolmaster, preached by an **English** international, played by the officers of the **British** Army and the gentlemen of the **colonial** civil service, in fixtures literally named *Home versus Colonial-born*.

And the Boers pick it up and love it.

Sixteen years before the Anglo-Boer War. Before the farms are burned, before the concentration camps, before roughly twenty-six thousand Boer women and children die in British custody.

The Afrikaner takes the Englishman's game **before** the Englishman gives him the greatest reason he will ever have to hate him.

And that fact — that rugby was already *theirs* by 1899 — is the seed of everything that happens in Gen 1 and Gen 2. It's why the game becomes an instrument of reconciliation in 1906. And it's why, when Afrikaner nationalism went looking for a vehicle in the twentieth century, there was one already built, already loved, and already theirs.

You beat the English at the game the English brought you.

---

### The spread

From there it goes everywhere, and it goes along the **money**.

British and Boer migrate into the interior together, and they carry the game with them — from the Cape Colony through the **Eastern Cape**, into **Natal**, and then **along the gold and diamond routes to Kimberley and Johannesburg.**

The mineral revolution *is* rugby's distribution network in this country. Where the diggings went, the game went.

And there's a detail here you'll enjoy, because it is emphatically *not* the mechanism you saw in Villa Peñarol or at the Iquique nitrate fields — where British enterprise carried the game out into the world and the men who owned the enterprise kept it: **rugby was carried into South Africa in part by miners from Cornwall** — a rugby stronghold in England. Cornish miners, following the diamonds and the gold, bringing a working-class rugby culture with them into the interior.

That is **Tucumán's** mechanism, not Carrasco's. Sugar mills, not polo lawns; the diggings, not the cricket club.

Not gentlemen. **Miners.**

The unions form fast, and follow the mines:

- **Western Province** — 1883
- **Griqualand West** (Kimberley — diamonds) — 1886
- **Eastern Province** — 1888
- **Transvaal** (the Rand — gold) — 1889

---

### Kimberley, 1889

And then, in **1889**, in **Kimberley** — a mining town that barely existed twenty years earlier — the national governing body is founded.

Two details about it that almost nobody knows.

**First: it wasn't called the rugby board.** It was founded as the **South African Football Board**. The word "**Rugby**" was only inserted in **1893** — and only because a *soccer* body, the South African Football Association, had been formed in 1892 and they needed to tell each other apart.

For its first four years, South Africa's rugby union was simply "the football board," because it hadn't yet occurred to anyone that there was more than one football.

**Second: Kimberley.** Remember that town. It founded the white board in **1889** — and it is going to found something else, seven years later, that matters just as much.

That same year, 1889, the first nationwide tournament is held at Kimberley. **Western Province** beat Griqualand West, Eastern Province and Transvaal.

The machine is built.

---

### And now the other beginning

Everything above is the story you already half-know. Here is the one you don't.

**1864.**

Two years after Green Point. Twenty-five years before the Board.

A clergyman named **Canon Robert John Mullins** becomes headmaster of an institution in Grahamstown for Black students — an institution that had begun as a branch of the all-white **St Andrew's College**, where Mullins had been a teacher.

And **he teaches his students rugby.**

He is usually credited as **the first person to introduce rugby to Black South Africans.**

**1864.** Not 1964. Not 1994. **Eighteen sixty-four** — while Ogilvie was still teaching the *wrong game* to white boys in Rondebosch, and eleven years before Hamilton RFC was founded, and fourteen years before William Milton got off the boat.

Black rugby in this country is not younger than white rugby. **It is essentially the same age**, and depending on how you count the codes, it may even have got to the *real* game first.

*(One more thing about Mullins, because it's almost too neat: his son, **Cuth Mullins**, played as a forward for the **1896 British touring team to South Africa.** The man who taught rugby to Black students in Grahamstown raised a British international.)*

---

### How it grew — and why the Eastern Cape

The mechanism was the **mission school**.

White missionaries used sport deliberately, as a tool — to encourage what they called **"respectability"** in the emerging Black and Coloured middle class. Cricket, tennis, croquet, soccer, and rugby. Sport as a technology of civilisation, applied to a colonised people.

That is not a flattering origin. It's a *paternalistic* one, and it should be named as such.

**But look what happened to it.**

**Rugby dominated the non-white sporting scene** in the Cape Colony and the **Eastern Cape** in particular — right through **to the late 1960s.**

The Eastern Cape, which benefited most from those mission schools, **remained the stronghold of Black rugby from that day to this.** It still is. Every great Black Springbok you can name traces back, through family or club or school, into that geography — and the reason is a churchman in Grahamstown in 1864.

And the game was **taken** — properly taken, made into something the missionaries never intended. Rugby became an element of Black **identity**. Matches drew women, men and children from different religious backgrounds. Communities raised money for their clubs by holding **dances**. And in a Cape where Black religious communities were sharply divided — where club membership itself was **strictly divided along religious lines** — the rugby field became one of the few places those divisions **weakened**, because opponents developed mutual respect for each other's toughness and bravery.

Which inverts the story entirely. In the 1880s, in the Cape, **rugby was a force for unity among Black South Africans** — bringing together communities that religion kept apart.

---

### 1886

And then the institutional proof.

**The Western Province Coloured Rugby Union is founded in 1886.**

**1886.**

Three years *before* the white South African Rugby Football Board was founded at Kimberley.

Coloured rugby in this country **organised itself into a union before white rugby did.**

Ten years later, in **1896**, the **South African Coloured Rugby Football Board** is founded — and where do you think it was founded?

**Kimberley.**

The same town. The same diamond dust. The white board in 1889, the Coloured board in 1896, seven years and a few streets apart.

That is not a coincidence of geography — it is the mineral revolution again, pulling every South African, of every colour, into the same mining towns, where they all did the same thing: **they formed a rugby union.**

*(And keep that fact in your pocket, because in **March 1992**, when those two bodies finally merge, the venue chosen for the signing will be a hotel **in Kimberley** — deliberately, explicitly, because it was the founding city of **both**.)*

---

### What Gen 0 leaves behind

**A game that nearly wasn't.** Winchester football was winning. One England back, in one season, turned it.

**A game the Boers had already claimed by 1883** — sixteen years before they had any reason to hate the men who brought it.

**A distribution network made of diamonds and gold**, and Cornish miners, and British soldiers.

**A board founded at Kimberley in 1889** that didn't even have the word "rugby" in its name.

**And a second rugby, exactly as old as the first** — begun by Canon Mullins in **1864**, grown in the mission schools of the Eastern Cape, organised into a union in **1886**, and dominant in Black and Coloured sporting life for the better part of a century.

Two rugbies. Two churchmen. Two schools. Born two years apart, in the same colony, playing the same game.

One of them will be given the state, the stadiums, the money, the jersey, the springbok, and the memory of a nation.

The other will be given the Rhodes Cup, the townships, the makeshift fields, and a silence so complete that a South African can grow up here, love this game, and never once be told it existed.

That's Gen 1.

## Generation 1: The Springboks, and the Rugby Nobody Tells You About (1889–1947)

Two rugby boards. Both founded in **Kimberley**. Seven years apart. And for the next ninety-six years, only one of them will be allowed to exist in the national memory.

That's the story of this generation. Let me tell you both halves, in parallel, because they were happening at the same time on the same soil — and the reason you've only ever heard one of them is not an accident.

---

### The tour that shouldn't have happened

**1891.** The first British team to tour South Africa.

And then there is the funding.

The tour was **financially underwritten by two men**: **Cecil John Rhodes** — Prime Minister of the Cape, arch-imperialist, diamond magnate, the man whose name is a curse and a scholarship — and **Paul Kruger**, President of the South African Republic.

**Rhodes and Kruger. Jointly. Funding a rugby tour.**

Eight years before they go to war with each other.

The Uitlander question is already boiling. The gold is already under the Rand. The Jameson Raid is four years away. And these two men — who between them will produce the most catastrophic conflict in South African history — reach into their pockets *together* to bring a British rugby team to Africa.

It tells you something the rest of this generation will keep proving: **rugby, in this country, was never merely a sport.** From the very first tour, it was a **political instrument**, and both sides knew it.

The British won every match. South Africa was played off the park.

Then **1896**: the British come again — and in the final Test at Cape Town, **South Africa beat them 5–0.** The first international victory in our history.

And **1903**: the British tour again and can only manage **11 wins, 8 losses, 3 draws.** South Africa takes the series.

---

### The green jersey

Here's a detail almost no South African knows, and it's a good pub fact.

**For its first five years, South Africa had no jersey.**

They played in **white**, or in the colours of whichever union or club happened to be hosting the match, with **no badge on the chest at all.**

*(And white, to this day, remains our alternate kit — a straight inheritance from that improvised era.)*

Then came the fourth and final Test of **1896**, at Newlands, and a captain named **Barry Heatlie**.

Heatlie had left Bishops in 1894 and helped found a club called **Old Diocesans** the year after. Old Diocesans played in **myrtle green** — a colour chosen, the story goes, by **Heatlie's wife**, for no better reason than that the dye was cheap and going spare. He still had a cache of the shirts. He handed them out.

And that is the match I told you about four paragraphs ago. **5 September 1896. South Africa 5, the British Isles 0.**

That first victory — South Africa's seventh Test, and the first they had not lost — was won in a set of leftover club jerseys, in a colour a woman had picked because it was on hand.

The team decided the green was lucky.

Seven years later, on **12 September 1903**, Heatlie was recalled to captain the third and final Test — again at Newlands. Old Diocesans had folded by then, but the outfitters still had stock. He put South Africa in green a second time.

**South Africa 8, the British Isles 0.** The first series this country ever won.

And the colour stuck.

The most sacred garment in Afrikaner cultural life is myrtle green because a captain's wife liked a cheap dye, and because the team happened to win in it twice.

---

### 1906: how you make a nation

Now the big one.

**1906–07.** South Africa tours the British Isles for the first time. It's an **all-white** team — Afrikaners and British colonial South Africans, side by side.

And then the timing:

**The Anglo-Boer War ended in 1902.**

Four years. Four years after the farm-burnings. Four years after the scorched earth. Four years after roughly 26,000 Boer women and children died in British concentration camps, and thousands of Black South Africans died in camps nobody bothered to count properly.

**Four years later, Boer and Briton pulled on the same green jersey and sailed to England together.**

And the tour **worked**. It is credited, explicitly, with helping to **erase the ill-will** between the two white groups. Just half a decade after the British had thrown Afrikaners into camps, the two peoples played alongside one another under a single banner.

Rugby was the **reconciliation technology of white South Africa.** That is not a metaphor. It is what the game was *for*, and the men running it knew it.

**The name.** The team gave itself an Afrikaans nickname — the **Springbokken** — and the English press anglicised it. **Springboks.**

Captained by **Paul Roos**, a Stellenbosch man. There's serious historical work on him titled *"Captain Diplomacy"* — which tells you exactly how that tour was understood at the time. Not as sport. As **statecraft**.

---

### What the Springbok was becoming

To understand what the Springbok became, you have to start with what it *meant* — and the usual version of this story skips that part, which is exactly why it convinces nobody who lived it.

**1902.** The Afrikaner is a defeated people. The republics are gone. The farms are burned. Twenty-six thousand of their women and children are dead in British camps, and the men who put them there now govern them. Within twenty years a great many of them will be off the ruined land and in Johannesburg, at the bottom of a labour market, in a country they had believed was theirs.

And they have this game.

An English game. Brought by an English schoolmaster, preached by an English international, played by the officers of the British Army — and taken up by Boer farmers at Stellenbosch **sixteen years before the war**, before there was any reason on earth to want revenge on the men who brought it.

So when the reason came, there was somewhere to put it.

**1906:** Paul Roos's Springbokken sail to Britain and play the country that burned the farms — not with a rifle, on a field, under British rules, in front of the British.

**1937:** they go to New Zealand and win the series **2–1** — 13–6 at Christchurch, 17–6 at Eden Park. It is the only Springbok side that has ever won a Test series in New Zealand. The tactics arrived by telegram from **Paul Roos himself**, thirty-one years after he had led the first tour, and consisted of three words: **"skrum, skrum, skrum."** They came home as **the Invincibles**, and it was the New Zealanders who called them the best team ever to leave New Zealand.

**That is the first half of this, it is true, and it is not sinister.** A people who had lost a war, a country and a generation of their children turned out to be the best in the world at one thing, and the thing was the conqueror's game. If you do not take that seriously you will not understand a single thing that follows — including why, ninety years later, this is still the sport that can stop the country dead.

---

Now the second half. Because a game that can carry a wounded people's dignity can carry other things too, and it is the same institutions that carry both.

The **Broederbond** — a secret society of Afrikaner men, formed to advance Afrikaner interests — was significant in mobilising the Afrikaner vote before the **1948 election.** Its members ran through Afrikaans public life and, as Gen 2 will show in detail, through the upper reaches of rugby administration. And when the National Party took power, it treated the Springbok as the emblem of one nation inside the country.

Not South Africa's.

**The Afrikaner's.**

That is the hinge, and it is worth being exact about it — because the accusation usually made here is the wrong one. The problem was never that Afrikaners loved rugby. Everybody in this book loves rugby; that is what the book is about. The problem is that the institutions carrying the love were the same institutions carrying the ideology, run by the same men, and there was nothing in the game to stop them.

An English game → taken up by Boer farmers at Stellenbosch in the 1880s → carrying a defeated people's dignity for forty years → used in 1906 to reconcile Boer and Briton → adopted by a nationalist movement as its emblem → and administered, by 1948, by men who belonged to the same brotherhood as the governing party.

**Rugby didn't merely reflect Afrikaner nationalism. It was one of the institutions that carried it.**

And it is why, from the other side of the fence, that same green jersey looked like something else entirely. Whatever it meant in Stellenbosch, to Black South Africans it was the sport of the **police**, the **army**, and the **government.**

Both of those are true, at the same time, about the same shirt. That is what makes this the hardest chapter in the book.

---

### And now the other rugby

Everything above is the half of the story that got read.

Here is the other half. It was happening at the same time, in the same towns, on the same weekends.

#### Kimberley, 1896

You have the fact already from Gen 0: the **South African Coloured Rugby Football Board**, founded in **1896**, **in Kimberley** — seven years and a few streets from the white board, in the same diamond dust.

Here is what they did with it.

They went looking for a trophy **equal in stature to the Currie Cup.**

They approached **Cecil John Rhodes.**

**And Rhodes said yes.**

He donated a trophy. The **Rhodes Cup**.

The inaugural tournament kicked off on the Kimberley Grounds on **20 August 1898** and ran for a week. Admission: **three shillings** for a pavilion seat, **two shillings** for temporary seating, **one shilling** to stand. **P. Maher of Western Province** scored the first try in the tournament's history.

On the final day, Western Province beat Transvaal 8–0, and Griquas beat Eastern Province 11–6, and the Mayor of Kimberley handed out medals.

And one of the referees that day? **J.T. "Chubb" Vigne — Springbok number 3** — who had played centre in the first Test series against the British tourists in 1891.

A white Springbok, refereeing the inaugural final of Coloured South Africa's national championship, in 1898.

**Between 1898 and 1980, twenty-eight Rhodes Cup tournaments were played.**

Eighty-two years. A national inter-provincial competition, running parallel to the Currie Cup, for the entire lifespan of segregated South Africa.

Ask a hundred South Africans what the Rhodes Cup is. See how many know.

---

#### The Eastern Cape and the mission schools

Remember **Canon Robert Mullins** from Gen 0 — teaching rugby to Black students in Grahamstown from **1864**, two years after Green Point?

That seed grew, and it grew in a specific place.

The **missionaries** were the vector. They introduced the game into their schools for indigenous peoples, and the region that benefited most was the **Eastern Cape** — which **remained the stronghold of Black rugby from that day to this.**

Not a coincidence. Not a mystery. **A churchman, a mission school, and a hundred and sixty years of inheritance.**

**1905:** Black clubs form the **Eastern Province Native Rugby Union**, president **Tobias Mvula.**

**1935:** the **South African Bantu Rugby Board** breaks away from the Coloured Board — discussions beginning at an inter-town rugby tournament in East London and concluding at an inter-provincial *cricket* tournament in Port Elizabeth. Black sporting South Africa was small and interconnected; the same men ran everything.

Its first officers: **J.M. Dippa** of Port Elizabeth as president, and as secretary — **Halley Plaatje**.

**The son of Sol Plaatje.**

The son of the founding secretary-general of the ANC, the author of *Native Life in South Africa*, the man who fought the 1913 Natives Land Act — was the first secretary of Black South African rugby.

That is the calibre of person who was running the game you were told Black people didn't play.

**1936:** the Bantu Rugby Board holds its first inter-provincial tournament in Kimberley. Transvaal and Eastern Province share the title after a **goalless draw**. The tournaments are sponsored by the **Chamber of Mines**, competing for the **NRC Cup** — the Native Recruiting Corporation Cup.

*(The Chamber of Mines — the body that recruited Black migrant labour for the gold mines — sponsoring Black rugby's national championship. Same pattern as Rhodes and the Coloured board. The mining houses funded Black rugby, and it is not remotely difficult to work out why they might have wanted a contented, organised, "respectable" workforce.)*

**1938:** the Coloured Board picks its **first national side.** And the Bantu Board then initiates **Test matches against them** — deliberately, in the sources' words, **to forge a sense of unity.**

Black and Coloured South Africa playing international-style Tests **against each other**, in the 1930s, as an act of solidarity, because nobody else would play either of them.

---

### What it actually was

So by the 1940s, this country had **three rugby nations** living on top of each other — and a fourth still to come:

- The **South African Rugby Board** — white, state-backed, Currie Cup, Springboks, Newlands and Loftus and Ellis Park.
- The **South African Coloured Rugby Football Board** (1896) — Rhodes Cup, national side from 1938.
- The **South African Bantu Rugby Board** (1935) — the Eastern Cape, the NRC Cup. It would rename itself in 1959, and its national side would be called the **Leopards** — but that belongs to Gen 2.
- And later again, the **South African Indian Rugby Football Association.**

Separate boards. Separate championships. Separate sets of national colours.

And the difference between them was not talent, and it was not passion, and it was not history — because as we've established, the Black and Coloured game is **as old as the white game**.

The difference was **facilities and money.** Black and Coloured rugby ran on **makeshift township fields with no state support**, while white rugby was **funded by the state**. Black and Coloured players were **systematically denied access to white pitches and training grounds.** No integration at any level. No pathway. Ever.

That's it. That's the entire mechanism.

Same game. Same country. Same age. **One of them got the state.**

---

### And the world was already helping

One last thing, because it complicates the comfortable story that the world was always against us.

**1919:** the **New Zealand Army team** — the side that had just won the King's Cup in Britain — tours South Africa. The South African board stipulates that **no player with Māori blood may be included**. Two Māori players, **Parekura Tureia** and **Charles Tepene**, are left behind. So is **Ranji Wilson** — an All Black of West Indian descent, and one of the stars of that King's Cup side. He never gets on the boat.

**1928:** the All Blacks tour South Africa for the first time — and **George Nēpia and Jimmy Mill are excluded.** Nēpia, who is very possibly the greatest fullback who has ever lived.

And **this attracted little comment at the time.**

Nobody objected. Not in New Zealand, not in Britain, not anywhere.

**Even before 1948** — before the National Party, before the apartheid statute book — foreign rugby nations were **voluntarily** stripping their own best players out of their touring squads to keep South Africa comfortable.

Apartheid rugby did not need to be imposed on the world. **For decades, the world simply agreed.**

---

### What Gen 1 leaves behind

**A green jersey**, chosen by accident in 1903.

**A name** — Springbokken — and a tour in 1906 that reconciled Boer and Briton four years after the camps, and in doing so made rugby the **founding sacrament of white South African nationhood**.

**A game grown into Afrikaner nationalism** so completely that by 1948 the same men ran the board, the brotherhood and the party.

**Four rugby boards**, of which one had the state and three had township fields.

**A Rhodes Cup**, played twenty-eight times over eighty-two years, that almost nobody now living can name.

**And Halley Plaatje**, son of Sol, taking the minutes.

In **1948**, the National Party wins the election.

And **Danie Craven** — a man whose name is on a stadium you have probably sat in — takes over South African rugby and turns it into the most feared force on earth.

That's Gen 2.

## Generation 2: The Afrikaner Capture, and the Best Team on Earth (1948–1969)

In **1948** the National Party won the election.

And within two years, they had made it **illegal** for a Black South African to play rugby on a decent field.

That's not a figure of speech. That's a legislative sequence, and it's worth laying out in order because it shows you exactly how deliberate this was:

**1950 — the Group Areas Act.** Defines the separate geographic areas in which each racial group may live. Black and Coloured South Africans are removed from developed areas.

**1953 — the Reservation of Separate Amenities Act.** Segregates *all* public facilities in South Africa.

**Including rugby pitches.**

Segregation along racial lines now **denied Black South Africans access to the top-level facilities and training that would enable them to represent South Africa in a Springbok jersey.**

Was that effect intended? You do not need to prove it, and it is better not to pretend you can read minds at seventy years' distance. **A government that has just spent three years legislating where people may live and which benches they may sit on does not need a separate secret policy to keep them off the rugby fields.** The fields were public amenities. The Act covered public amenities. That was the whole point of the Act.

Remember what you learned in Gen 1: the Coloured Board had existed since **1896**. The Rhodes Cup since **1898**. The Eastern Province Native Rugby Union since **1905**. Black and Coloured rugby was **as old as white rugby.**

The apartheid state did not prevent Black South Africans from *taking up* rugby.

**It found them already playing — and legislated them into township dust.**

---

### And the Broederbond was in the room

The **Broederbond** — the secret Afrikaner brotherhood — had been crucial in mobilising the Afrikaner vote in the months before the 1948 victory. And it did not then go home.

The reporting on this is stark. **Virtually everyone else involved in the higher echelons of South African rugby was a member of the Broederbond — including the referees for Test matches.**

The referees.

And it reached into selection. Which brings us to **Des van Jaarsveldt**, and to a point this chapter should make plainly, because it is the one most often left out.

Van Jaarsveldt was a Rhodesian, and the only Rhodesian ever to captain the Springboks. He went to the trials in **1951** and again in **1956**, and was passed over both times. He believed the reason was **Broederbond pressure on the Board not to pick him — because he was Rhodesian.**

He finally got the captaincy in **1960**. He led South Africa to victory.

He was **never picked again.**

The reasons handed down are that he **could not speak Afrikaans**, that there was Anglophobia among the selectors, and that he had declined to lead the traditional team prayer — which went down badly.

Not Black. Not Coloured. **A white, English-speaking South African, given the captaincy of his country, winning with it, and discarded for not being the right sort of white man.**

Hold on to him, because he is the answer to the easy reading of this chapter. This is not a story about one people doing wrong to another. The Broederbond was not only keeping Black and Coloured South Africans off the field — it was policing **which whites** were allowed on it, and it broke a man who had done nothing but win.

An institution that will do that to its own is not expressing a people's character. It is doing what closed institutions do: to whoever is nearest.

---

### Mr Rugby

And presiding over all of it, for **thirty-seven years**, was one man.

**Daniël Hartman Craven.** Born Lindley, Free State, 1910. Doc. **Mr Rugby.**

You know the name. There's a stadium with it on. There's a schools week with it on. Let me tell you who he actually was, because he is the most complicated figure in the history of our game and I'm not going to hand you a hero or a villain.

**The credentials are absurd.** He played **16 Tests** between 1931 and 1938, and captained the side. He is remembered as **one of the greatest dive-passing scrumhalves who ever lived** — and, extraordinarily, he was also selected for the Springboks at **centre, fly-half, No. 8 and fullback.** Four other positions.

Academically: a **BA** in 1932, an **MA** in 1933, and then **three doctorates** — ethnology in **1935**, his thesis titled *Ethnological Classification of the South African Bantu*; psychology in **1973**; physical education in **1978**. Three doctorates, forty-three years between the first and the last. He became Stellenbosch's **first professor of physical education** in 1947.

He joined the Union Defence Force in 1938 as director of physical education and was sent to study in **Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, Italy, France and Britain** — returning only when war broke out.

*(One detail he liked to raise: his paternal grandfather **John Craven came to South Africa from West Yorkshire as a diamond prospector**, and Craven himself **claimed not to be an Afrikaner.**)*

He was appointed national coach in **1949**.

He was president of the South African Rugby Board from **1956 until his death in 1993** — the longest-serving president in the history of the game anywhere. He sat on the **International Rugby Board from 1957** and chaired it **three times** — 1962, 1973, 1979.

For most of the twentieth century, **Danie Craven was South African rugby.** There was no meaningful distinction.

---

### What he built

He started his coaching career by winning **ten matches in a row.**

**1949: New Zealand tour South Africa. Springboks 4–0.**

A whitewash of the All Blacks. It remains New Zealand's heaviest Test series defeat to that point.

**Undefeated from 1949 to 1952.** **17 wins from 23 Tests** — a **74% record** that makes him one of the greatest coaches this country has produced.

**1951–52: the tour of Europe.** Considered among the finest Springbok sides ever to leave these shores. Captained by **Hennie Muller**. They won the **Grand Slam** and beat France as well.

**31 matches. 30 wins. One defeat** — to London Counties.

And the highlight, the one your grandfather would have known by heart:

**Scotland 0, South Africa 44.**

Forty-four to nil, at Murrayfield, in 1951. In an era when a try was worth three points.

**1960: the All Blacks tour again. Springboks win the series 2–1 with a draw.** And that same year the Springboks tour Europe and beat **all four Home Unions** — a **fourth Grand Slam.**

And behind all of it sat the number that defined the era: **from 1896 until 1958, not one touring side won a Test series in South Africa.**

The British Isles managed it in 1896. Then nobody managed it again for **sixty-two years** — and we will come to the men who finally did.

They were, quite simply, **the best rugby team on the planet**, and everybody knew it.

---

### What Hennie Muller said about him

There's a line from his captain that tells you why the players loved him:

**He knew them so well — their strengths and weaknesses, their fears and their innermost thoughts. He could put himself into the shoes of the player.**

That's a great coach. It is also, when you think about who else he was working for, a genuinely troubling sentence.

---

### The other side of Craven

Now the part I promised I wouldn't simplify.

**The charge against him is severe and it is largely deserved.** He is hated by many for having **done little to challenge segregation** in a sport central to white South African identity, at the precise moment when he had more power to do so than any human being alive. He **reluctantly complied with government demands** to have **Māori players excluded** from visiting All Black teams — in 1949, and again in 1960. He asserted that the Springbok was **intrinsically tied to the white identity of the national team.**

In **1968** he argued that the racial groups were **"separate nations… [who] won't ever play in the same side"** — before adding, almost as an afterthought, *"but maybe… one day, we would have such a team."*

And in **1969** came the line that follows him into every obituary: that there would be a **black Springbok "over my dead body."**

Craven **denied he ever said it.** And here's the honest position, which I'd rather give you than a comfortable one: **nobody has ever been able to establish where, when, or to whom that statement was made.** It has no traceable source. It may well be apocryphal. It is also entirely consistent with what he *did* say on the record in 1968.

**And yet.**

A fact little known to outsiders: **Craven provided coaching to a Coloured rugby team in the 1940s and '50s.** Not after the "dead body" remark. **Before it.** Two decades before the government moved an inch, he was quietly running multiracial coaching.

**Errol Tobias** — who becomes the first non-white Springbok, and whose story is Gen 4 — recalls Craven coming to watch the non-white sides and telling them: **"you guys are on the right track."**

In **1964**, Craven launched **Craven Week**, the schoolboy tournament that still carries his name — an idea brought to him fifteen years earlier by the Springbok flanker **Piet Malan**, as a way of marking the **South African Rugby Board's seventy-fifth anniversary.** Kimberley in 1889, plus seventy-five years, is 1964. The board threw itself a birthday party and the party never stopped.

And it was, like everything else in that country, **whites only.**

Hold that against him. Then hold this against that: in **1980**, with no law compelling him and to a good deal of fury, **Craven forced the organisers to open Craven Week to every race** — a change first proposed in 1978 that had gone nowhere until he insisted on it. That story belongs to Gen 4. But it is worth knowing here that the week with his name on it got there fourteen years before the country did.

He tried to shield rugby from Broederbond interference in selection, and he was despised in some quarters for it. During the 1981 New Zealand tour, protesters produced a **"WANTED — dead or alive" poster with Danie Craven's face on it.**

And in **1988** — this is Gen 4, but you should know where it's heading — Craven flew to **Harare to meet the ANC**, in defiance of his own government, and began the negotiations that produced unification in 1992.

He died on **4 January 1993**, two and a half years before Ellis Park.

---

### So what do we do with him?

Here's my honest read, and you can throw it out.

Craven was not a secret liberal, and he was not a monster. He was **an institutionalist** — a man whose deepest loyalty was to *rugby*, and who spent forty years making the calculation that the way to protect the game was to **stay inside the system and steer it**, rather than break with it and be replaced by someone worse.

That calculation bought him a magnificent rugby machine and an unbeaten home record. It also meant that for four decades, the most powerful man in South African sport **used his power to preserve the institution rather than to challenge the injustice it was built on.**

He coached Coloured teams in the fifties and told them they were on the right track — and then presided over a Board that kept them off the fields for another thirty years.

Both are true. That's the man. And frankly, that's the position an enormous number of white South Africans occupied in that era, which is precisely why he remains so hard to look at squarely.

---

### Meanwhile, the other rugby

Because it hadn't stopped. It never stopped.

The **Bantu Rugby Board** — founded 1935, with **Halley Plaatje**, son of Sol, as its first secretary — changed its name in **1959** to the **South African African Rugby Board**. Not for administrative tidiness. Because the word **"Bantu"** had been **poisoned by the apartheid state**, which used it to segregate Black South Africans, strip them of land and economic opportunity, and remove their civil and political rights.

They renamed themselves **in rejection of the regime's vocabulary.**

Their national team was called **the Leopards.**

And in the townships, on makeshift fields with no funding, the **Rhodes Cup** kept being played — as it had since 1898.

Two rugbies. One of them was the best team on earth. The other one wasn't allowed on the grass.

---

### The world starts to notice

The turn comes in **1960**, and it comes fast.

**Harold Macmillan** delivers the **"Wind of Change"** speech in Cape Town.

And at **Sharpeville**, police kill sixty-nine people.

From that moment, international criticism of apartheid **rises steeply** — and the Springboks, as the most visible representatives of white South Africa, become **the target.** When the All Blacks toured in 1960, they came **despite a petition of 150,000 signatures** opposing it.

**1963:** the **South African Non-Racial Olympic Committee (SAN-ROC)** is formed, a year before the Tokyo Games, protesting that only whites could represent South Africa. Its members are targeted and persecuted by the regime.

The cracks are showing in white rugby too. In **1956** the All Blacks won their first-ever series against the Springboks — in what was described at the time as **the most bitterly fought series in history.** In **1958**, France came to South Africa expected to be cannon fodder, drew the first Test 3–3, and then **won the series.**

The unbeatable machine was, quietly, becoming beatable.

And in **1959**, in a committee room rather than on a field, non-white rugby broke in half. **Fourteen unions — more than ten thousand players** — walked out of the **South African Coloured Rugby Football Board** behind a man named **Cuthbert Loriston.**

That sounds like an administrative event.

It is the beginning of the most painful civil war in the history of South African rugby — and it is Gen 3.

---

### What Gen 2 leaves behind

**The greatest Springbok teams ever assembled.** A 4–0 whitewash of the All Blacks. 30 wins from 31 in Europe. 44–0 against Scotland. Two Grand Slams. And a home series record that stood unbeaten from 1896 until France broke it in 1958.

**A legislative machine** — Group Areas Act, Separate Amenities Act — that took a Black and Coloured rugby culture as old as the white one and pushed it onto dirt.

**A secret society in the referees' room.**

**A Springbok emblem** that the governing party had claimed for one nation inside the country — and which, to Black South Africans, meant the police, the army, and the government.

**And one man**, holding all of it in his hands for thirty-seven years — who coached Coloured teams in secret in the fifties, launched a whites-only schools week in 1964 and then forced it open to every race in 1980, told Errol Tobias he was on the right track, allegedly said *over my dead body*, complied with the exclusion of the Māori, and finally flew to Harare in 1988 to meet the ANC.

**Gen 3** is where it all begins to come apart — and where non-white rugby **tears itself in half** over the most agonising question available: *do you take the state's hand, or do you refuse it?*

Cuthbert Loriston takes it. Abdullah Abass refuses.

*No normal sport in an abnormal society.*

## Generation 3: The Split, and the Word "Collaborator" (1959–1979)

There is a stadium in Kimberley called the **AR Abass Stadium.**

You have almost certainly never heard of it. Neither had I.

It is named for **Abdullah "Dullah" Abass** — and it is, in one writer's devastating phrase, a place that now **stands like a silent tumour, growing with every death of the heroes who once gave it purpose.**

This generation is about the man that stadium is named for, and about the men who chose differently from him. And I want to be honest with you upfront: **it is the hardest chapter in South African rugby**, and it is the one that has been most thoroughly erased — not by the apartheid state, but by the *new* South Africa.

Because the great tragedy of Gen 3 is not that white rugby crushed Black rugby.

**It is that Black rugby was forced to tear itself in half.**

---

### The trap

Start with the date, because the tidy version of this story gets it wrong.

**The break came in 1959.** Not in the mid-sixties, and not as an answer to anything the government had offered — because in 1959 the government had not yet offered anything. **Fourteen unions, representing more than ten thousand players, walked out of the South African Coloured Rugby Football Board** and formed the **South African Rugby Football Federation** under **Cuthbert Loriston.**

And here is the part the morality play leaves out. It was not, at the start, a clash of principle at all. The sources are blunt: the walkout followed **a bitter power struggle between Loriston and the Board's general secretary, Abdullah Abass.** Two men who could not work with each other, and a movement that tore along the seam they made.

The principle came *afterwards*. And then it swallowed everything.

Because through the sixties the world was closing in. **Sharpeville. The Wind of Change. SAN-ROC** — formed 1963, its first president **Dennis Brutus jailed on Robben Island**, several of its later leaders **killed or imprisoned.** South Africa suspended from the **1964 Tokyo Olympics.**

And the apartheid government made a calculation. It could not hold the line completely. So it would give **just enough.**

Not integration. Something they called **"multi-nationalism"** — the idea that South Africa contained *several nations*, each of which could have its own sports teams, which might occasionally play each other. Separate development, in shorts.

*That* is what turned a quarrel between two men into a question of conscience for a whole people. There was now something on the table, and you had to decide whether to take it. Loriston's Federation was willing. And in **1966**, the men who had stayed behind — the rump of the old Coloured Board, under Abass — renamed themselves the **South African Rugby Union** and set about refusing it.

Seven years after the split, the two halves finally had their names. And the question that would define them for the next thirty years:

**Do you take the state's hand, or do you refuse it?**

Take it, and your players get onto real fields. Real coaching. Maybe, one day, a Springbok jersey. Your kids get *something*.

Refuse it, and you keep your integrity — and you condemn your own players to township dirt for another twenty years, and you watch them retire without ever having been seen.

There is no clean answer. There has never been a clean answer.

---

### The men who took it

Loriston's **South African Rugby Football Federation** — the Federation, the "Fed" — chose to work **with** the white board. Their representative side was called the **Proteas**.

And alongside them, the Black board — the **South African Rugby Association (SARA)**, descendant of the old Bantu Board that Halley Plaatje had served — with its side, the **Leopards**.

And in **November 1977** it happened: the coloured **SARFF**, the black **SARA** and the white **SARB** amalgamated to form a nominally **non-racial South African Rugby Board**, affiliated to the **IRB**.

On paper: unity. Players of colour could now enter the mainstream competitions. In that same year, **all grounds where SARB matches were played were opened to all races.**

**Hennie Shields** was chosen for the Gazelles. **Errol Tobias** and **Turkey Shields** for the SA Country XV. **Timothy Nkonki** and Hennie Shields were sent to Argentina to play in an Invitation XV for the **centenary of Club Atlético San Isidro.**

*(CASI. The San Isidro club from Gen 0 of the Argentina story — the one founded in 1902 at the Vignoles hotel, whose great schism in 1935 was about whether women could attend post-match drinks. **A Black South African and a Coloured South African, flown to Buenos Aires in 1977 to play at CASI's centenary.** These threads never stop touching.)*

---

### The men who refused

And then there was **Dullah Abass**.

The **South African Rugby Union** — the *original* SARU, non-racial, nothing to do with the body that runs the game today — **refused to be part of it.**

They said no. And they said why.

In **March 1973**, nine sporting federations from oppressed communities came together under the presidency of **Norman Middleton** and founded the **South African Council on Sport — SACOS.**

And SACOS gave South Africa a sentence that outlived apartheid:

**"No normal sport in an abnormal society."**

It means precisely what it says. **You cannot have a fair game inside an unfair country.** Sport is not a neutral space where politics is suspended for eighty minutes. A "mixed" fixture between a white club and a Black club means nothing when one side goes home to Sandton and the other goes home to a location with no running water. Integrate the field all you like — you have not integrated the *society*, and so the field is a lie.

SACOS argued explicitly that the reforms were **a guise, intended to appease the international community while maintaining separate racial development.**

And they built a whole doctrine on it. The **1978 Normal Sport Charter**: single non-racial federations, the outlawing of racially exclusive clubs, the integration of all school and youth sport. The **"double standards resolution"** — forbidding *any* participation in race-based sport, in any form, ever.

If you played for the Proteas, you were, in SACOS's eyes, **a collaborator.**

---

### What it cost them

And it is worth being precise about what these men actually did.

**SACOS affiliates suffered from a chronic lack of sponsorship**, because big business — **South African Breweries** among them — **backed apartheid.**

Their inter-provincial competition involved **arduous trips by road** and was **entirely self-funded.**

They organised fixtures, agitated for facilities, chased sponsorships that never came, and built sport programmes **from community level to national level with no government and no corporate support whatsoever.**

Meanwhile — and I'm quoting — **white sport prospered.**

They were persecuted. Their officials were labelled **"sports terrorists"** — the equivalent of enemies on the border. They were accused, with magnificent gall, of **politicising sport.**

And **Hassan Howa**, the SACOS president, said the thing that explains the whole movement. People came to him and said: *what you fought for has arrived, why aren't you satisfied?*

And he answered:

**"I can't be satisfied if I was fighting for the wrong things."**

That is a man who understood that being *let in* to an unjust house is not the same as tearing the house down.

---

### Two brothers, one field

So picture South Africa in 1978.

A Coloured winger in the Boland is playing brilliant rugby. And there are now **two roads** open to him.

**Road one:** join the Federation. Play for the **Proteas**. Get selected for an SA Country XV. Maybe — just maybe — get a Springbok jersey. Be seen. Be paid attention to. Be told by Danie Craven himself that **"you guys are on the right track."** Be denounced by your own community as a **sell-out** who put on the jersey of the police and the army.

**Road two:** stay with **SARU** and **SACOS**. Play on a field with no posts and no grass, in front of your own people, self-funded, in a competition nobody outside the townships will ever report. Retire at thirty-four having never once been watched by a selector. And know that **you never wore the cloak of the men who denied you.**

**Randy Marinus** played against the **1976 All Blacks at the age of nineteen** — and then decided to **continue his career under SACOS-affiliated SARU.**

He walked *away* from the mainstream. At nineteen. Knowing exactly what it would cost him.

And there's **Eric "Bucs" Damons** of Kimberley — a player of incredible talent, a legend of Black rugby, who died in 2021. Nobody in the current Griqualand West union knows his name. It was always a contentious issue that Bucs was **never selected for SARU**. And the man who wrote his eulogy found a bitter consolation in it — that his omission **lifted from his shoulders the burden of having to wear the cloak of those who denied him in the first place.**

*In South Africa, rugby justice is provided by allowing those who were suppressed by apartheid policies to adorn the colours of the proud sporting symbol of apartheid.*

That is the wound. It has never healed, and I don't think it ever will.

---

### What the world did

Meanwhile, outside, the isolation was tightening — and it was rugby, specifically, that did it.

**1969–70:** the Springboks tour Britain and Ireland, and are met by demonstrations at every ground. Marchers on the road to **Twickenham**.

**1976: New Zealand tour South Africa** — months after the **Soweto uprising**, in which schoolchildren were shot in the streets.

The response is one of the most consequential acts in the history of sport: the **African bloc boycotts the Montreal Olympics** in protest at New Zealand's presence — **twenty-nine nations by the most commonly cited count**, almost all of them African, with **Guyana and Iraq** joining them. Twenty-two walked out on the eve of the opening ceremony and the rest followed within days. The sources differ on the exact number. They do not differ on the scale: most of a continent left the Olympic Games because of a rugby tour.

**1977:** the Commonwealth signs the **Gleneagles Agreement**, committing member states to discourage sporting contact with South Africa.

And in **December 1976**, SACOS was recognised by the **Supreme Council of Sport in Africa** — which meant that from then on, **South Africa's entire international sporting relations were effectively hostage to SACOS's influence.**

Those self-funded men, driving to fixtures on bad roads with no sponsorship, now held a veto over whether the Springboks could play anybody at all.

And Norman Middleton, the founding SACOS president, understood exactly where the pressure point was. He said it better than any analyst ever has:

**The government could not care less about cricket and soccer** — those sports don't really mean much to the true Afrikaner, so expulsion from them doesn't mean much either. **But rugby is different.**

**"Rugby is the Afrikaner's second religion."**

They knew precisely where to press. And they pressed.

---

### And the Rhodes Cup dies

One last thing, and it's the detail I keep turning over.

**In 1969, the Rhodes Cup was discontinued.**

Not by the government. Not by the white board.

**By Black rugby itself.**

Think about what that decision *was*. That cup had been played for since **1898** — seventy-one years. It was the closest thing Coloured South Africa had to a Currie Cup: the trophy their grandfathers had gone and *asked Rhodes for*, and which he had *given* them.

And the reason they gave it up is more specific, and more damning, than a general distaste. They rejected **Rhodes the imperialist** — and they rejected **his company's exploitation of Black workers in the mines.**

Which closes a circle this chapter opened in Gen 0. The mineral revolution is what pulled every South African, of every colour, into Kimberley. It is why the white board was founded there in **1889** and the Coloured board in **1896**, seven years and a few streets apart. The diamonds built the town that built their union — and the man whose company dug those diamonds out of the ground on Black labour had put his name on their trophy.

In 1969 they looked at the most prestigious object they owned and said: **we will not compete for a thing with that man's name on it.**

And here is the part that usually gets left out — the part that is better than the version where they simply lose something.

**They didn't give up their history. They replaced it.** In **1971** they instituted the **South Africa Cup**: the same competition, the same men, the same makeshift fields, and nobody's name on the trophy. The Rhodes Cup flickered back for a single season — and then, in **1980**, they shelved it for good, on precisely the principle they had killed it with eleven years before.

Twenty-eight tournaments, 1898 to 1980. Two decisions to end it, both taken by the people it belonged to.

That is the same instinct that made the Bantu Board rename itself in 1959, and the same instinct that made Dullah Abass refuse the state's hand in 1977, and the same instinct that made Randy Marinus walk away at nineteen.

**It is the most principled thing anybody did in South African rugby in the twentieth century, and it cost them everything, and almost nobody remembers it.**

---

### The verdict nobody wants

So who was right? Loriston or Abass?

I'm not going to hand you an answer, because I don't think there is one — but I'll tell you where the argument now sits, and it will surprise you.

**SACOS's warnings were ignored by the incoming ANC government after 1994.**

The critique — made by SACOS veterans, and by historians — is that the ANC chose to leave **apartheid sports structures relatively intact**, in the belief that it would win them goodwill and votes from the white electorate. In **May 1990** the **National Sports Congress** was launched as the sports wing of the ANC, and the SACOS mantra was, in one writer's phrase, **turned on its head.**

**SACOS disbanded in 2005.**

And the men who spent thirty years insisting that you cannot have normal sport in an abnormal society look at South African sport today — at the elitism, the corruption, the nepotism, the exclusion — and say, quietly: *we told you.*

The stadium in Kimberley still carries Abass's name. Almost nobody knows why.

---

### What Gen 3 leaves behind

**A civil war inside Black and Coloured rugby**, between men who wanted their players *seen* and men who would not shake the hand that was strangling them — and a word, **collaborator**, that still cuts in Boland and Kimberley today.

**A slogan** that outlived apartheid and is now being turned back on the people who inherited the country.

**A boycott with teeth** — twenty-nine nations out of Montreal, Gleneagles, and SACOS holding a veto over the Springboks' entire international existence.

**A discontinued cup**, abandoned on principle in 1969 at seventy-one years old — and replaced, in 1971, by one that carried nobody's name.

**And Norman Middleton's diagnosis**, which was the key to the whole strategy and which every white South African should sit with: they didn't boycott rugby because rugby mattered most to *them*.

They boycotted rugby because **it mattered most to us.**

---

**Gen 4** is where it detonates. **Errol Tobias** pulls on the green jersey in 1981 — the first Black Springbok — and is called a hero by one half of his country and a traitor by the other. The Springboks tour New Zealand and very nearly break that nation in half: flour bombs from a light aircraft over Eden Park, and **40% of the entire New Zealand police force** at a single rugby match. South Africa misses the first World Cup, goes seven years without a Test against an established nation, and — locked out of the world — starts **inventing opponents**.

Including a team from South America that officially did not exist, captained by a man named **Hugo Porta**.

## Generation 4: The Man in the Middle (1980–1991)

A thirty-one-year-old bricklayer from a farm called **Klipdrift**, outside Caledon in the Overberg, digs up his own back yard and floods it.

He turns it into a **mud-bath**.

Then he goes out into it, day after day, and practises — because he has just been told, over the telephone by **Danie Craven**, that he is going to New Zealand with the Springboks, and he knows the fields there will be wet, and he intends to be ready.

His name is **Errol George Tobias**, and he is about to become the loneliest man in the history of South African sport.

---

### Who he was

Born **18 March 1950**. A farm in the Western Cape. He worked as a **bricklayer**, and later ran his own construction company.

He played for **Boland**. And he played for the **Proteas** — the representative side of the Coloured Federation, the body Cuthbert Loriston led, the one that took the state's hand. He toured **Britain with them in 1971.**

Which means, in the language of Gen 3, Errol Tobias had *already chosen his road.* He was Federation, not SARU. He was on the side of the men who said: **get on the field, be seen, take what's offered.** And SACOS had a word for that.

**August 1978.** He turns out at fly-half for a **multi-racial South African Country Districts XV** against the American Cougars, at the Border grounds. He scores **two tries** and makes two more. **5,500 people** watch them win 44–12.

**October 1980.** He's part of a Springbok touring party to South America — **which is refused visas to enter Argentina.** So they play **Paraguay, Uruguay and Chile** instead, in tours wrapped in secrecy, in front of crowds **as small as one hundred people.**

*(One hundred. The Springboks. The team that put 44–0 on Scotland. Playing in front of a hundred people in Asunción because nobody would let them in.)*

And then the call comes.

---

### 30 May 1981. Newlands.

**South Africa v Ireland.**

Errol Tobias runs out at **outside centre**, aged thirty-one, in front of **37,000 people**.

**The first player of colour ever to play a Test match for the Springboks.**

Before the game, Craven had warned him: *the match will be over before you even realise you're representing your country.* And Craven told him something else, before New Zealand — that he would **always** be known as the first Springbok of colour, that the tour would be brutal, and that he should remember he was **in the team on merit, not as window-dressing.**

Tobias broke the line and put **Rob Louw** through for a try. South Africa won **23–15**. Danie Gerber scored what Tobias still calls one of the finest tries ever seen at Newlands.

A week later, at **Kings Park**, they beat Ireland again, 12–10. Series won.

He'd end with **six Test caps** between 1981 and 1984 — **21 games for the Springboks in all** — scoring **22 points**: a try, five conversions, three penalties, a drop goal. In 1984 he moved to fly-half and, at **Ellis Park**, scored a try and a conversion in a 35–9 demolition of England.

He was, by every account, a **genuinely excellent rugby player.**

---

### And he was hated by everyone

**His selection was condemned from both directions at once.**

**White South Africans who backed apartheid** attacked it as a **cynical stunt** by rugby officials to placate the protesters.

**Anti-apartheid campaigners** attacked it as **political window-dressing** — a token, a fig leaf, a Black man held up to prove the system worked.

**Halt All Racist Tours**, the New Zealand protest movement, put his name on placards. Their man **Dick Cuthbert** called him **"an Uncle Tom."**

And inside his own team, it wasn't clean either. **Naas Botha** later suggested the players had accepted him. But **Johan Claassen**, the tour manager, admitted that team management **possibly had an "anti-Errol" bias.**

His own management.

So picture him. Thirty-one years old. A bricklayer from the Overberg. Flying to New Zealand as the **only** non-white man in a green jersey — attacked by the apartheid right as a sop, attacked by the liberation left as a sell-out, and quietly resented by some of the men in his own changing room.

**Within weeks of arriving in New Zealand, he was ready to pack his bags and fly home.**

He called the tour a **"bizarre, never-ending nightmare."**

---

### What New Zealand did

And it *was* a nightmare — for everybody.

The 1981 Springbok tour drove New Zealand, by the reckoning of the people who lived it, **to the brink of civil war.** It split families down the middle. Fifty-six days.

- **Hamilton:** protesters **invaded the pitch** and the match against Waikato was **abandoned.**
- **First Test:** kick-off **delayed** while police and officials walked the length of the field picking up **thumbtacks** strewn on the grass. South African viewers, watching live in the early hours, saw fields being combed for **glass shards, fishhooks and nails.**
- Protesters got into venues and **shone mirrors into the players' eyes.**
- Demonstrations outside the team hotels ran **through the night**, until the Springboks had to be **housed in function rooms inside Athletic Park and Eden Park** — the team nicknamed it the **"Grandstand Hotel."** They slept in the stadium.
- **Molesworth Street, Wellington:** police batons on protesters.
- Gangs — **Black Power** and the **Mongrel Mob** — were in the streets. Peaceful protesters dressed as clowns were **beaten with batons.**

And then, **12 September 1981, Eden Park, third Test.**

A **Cessna 172**, piloted by **Marx Jones** with **Grant Cole**, flew low over the stadium **dropping flour bombs onto the field during a Test match.** The All Black prop **Gary Knight** was **hit in the head** by one — a 450-gram bag of flour, from a light aircraft, in the middle of an international. They dropped smoke bombs too, and protesters in the ground fired flares onto the grass.

**The game continued.**

South Africa's captain, **Wynand Claassen**, asked whether New Zealand had an air force.

Outside: **201 arrests.** More than **90 police officers and protesters injured.** In Warkworth, Dunedin and Timaru, protesters **stormed the TV transmitters and cut the broadcast.**

**Forty percent of New Zealand's entire police force** was on duty at that one rugby match.

Marx Jones went to prison.

**Hone Harawira** — later a Member of Parliament — was one of the leaders of the mostly Māori and Pasifika **Patu Squad**, and was charged with seven offences carrying, by his count, something like **98 years.**

They called them the **Barbed Wire Boks.**

---

### And Tobias's verdict, forty years later

**Errol Tobias — the man they abused, the man they called an Uncle Tom, the man whose tour they tried to destroy — later said the protests were right.**

He came to see them as having shown South Africa that **apartheid had to end.**

And at a formal welcome during that tour, the Māori leader **Graham Latimer** — while telling the Springboks to their faces that they would never be welcome again under those conditions — gave Tobias a warm *mihi*, and said of him:

**"I believe he represents hope for 18 million South Africans, for whom there appears to be little hope."**

Both things were true. **He was a fig leaf, and he was a door.**

And the door opened. His selection **paved the way** for **Avril Williams** — and later for Avril's nephew, a boy named **Chester Williams**, who in 1995 would score four tries in a World Cup quarter-final and become the face on the poster.

*(And there's a lovely detail from Abe Williams, the 1981 team manager — himself appointed specifically to represent non-white players. He describes the coaching clinics run in the Coloured communities, where juniors were taught by visiting Springboks. **Some of those juniors became provincial and Springbok players — Chester Williams among them.** And the first fully open **Craven Week** was held at **Stellenbosch in 1980**, because **Danie Craven forced the organisers to open it to all races** — a change first proposed in 1978 that had gone nowhere until he insisted.)*

---

### The lockout

**1984** was the last time an established rugby nation played a Test series in South Africa for eight years. England came, and lost both Tests — one of them the 35–9 at Ellis Park where Tobias scored.

And then, from **1985 to 1991**, the Springboks did not play a single Test against an established country. Seven years.

But here is the detail that cuts against the comfortable story, and it deserves to be better known: **rugby never expelled South Africa.** Athletics did — the IAAF threw them out in **1976**. The Olympic movement did. But **South Africa remained a member of the International Rugby Board right through the apartheid era**, and the president of its own board, **Danie Craven**, had chaired that body in 1962, 1973 and 1979.

The isolation was real and it was near-total. It was also imposed from *outside* the game — by governments, by Gleneagles, by most of a continent walking out of Montreal, by SACOS's veto through the Supreme Council of Sport in Africa. **Rugby's own world body never once put South Africa out.**

They missed the **first Rugby World Cup in 1987.** They missed **1991.** They were left off the guest list.

But being left off a guest list is not the same as being put out of the house. And in **1989**, as you are about to see, the IRB demonstrated the difference.

And the last match of that 1981 tour is the saddest fixture in Springbok history. The Test against the **United States** was moved, played **in secret, a day early**, at **Glenville** in upstate New York.

**Twenty-nine spectators.**

Twenty-nine. **The lowest attendance ever recorded at an international rugby match, anywhere, ever.**

The greatest team on earth, playing in a field in front of twenty-nine people, because nobody would watch them.

---

### Inventing opponents

So, locked out, South Africa did what desperate people do. **It invented fixtures.**

Rebel tours. Invitational sides. Made-up teams.

**1986: the New Zealand Cavaliers** — an unsanctioned rebel tour, not approved by the NZRU or the New Zealand government.

And — as you already know, from the other end of the telescope — **the Jaguars.**

**Sudamérica XV.** The shadow team invented so that Argentina could evade its own government's ban. Thirty of the forty-two players Argentine. All-Argentine Test XVs. Captained by **Hugo Porta**.

And on **3 April 1982, at Bloemfontein**, an unchanged Springbok side — the same men who had been flour-bombed at Eden Park seven months earlier — lost **21–12**, and **Hugo Porta scored every one of those twenty-one points.**

South Africa awarded those Tests **full cap status**. Argentina did not.

Two pariah rugby nations, playing each other under a flag of convenience, because the world would not have either of them.

*(And on the 1980 Springbok tour that played the Jaguars in Montevideo and Santiago — the one Tobias was part of, the one that got refused visas for Argentina — the squad included Errol Tobias. **The first Black Springbok and the greatest Argentine of all time were on the same isolated, illegitimate, ghost circuit at the same time.** That's not a coincidence. That's what the boycott *did*.)*

---

### Oom Danie flies to Harare

And then, at the very end, the turn.

By **1988**, with the SARB's centenary approaching, **Danie Craven and Louis Luyt knew change was coming whether they liked it or not.**

It started quietly and at home. **On 7 May 1988**, representatives of SARB and SARU met at the **Cape Sun in Cape Town** to discuss the way forward.

And then, in **October 1988**, Craven and Luyt went to **Harare** and met the **African National Congress** — a banned organisation — along with representatives of the non-racial **SARU**. The ANC delegation was led by **Alfred Nzo**, and among the men across the table was **Thabo Mbeki.**

The National Party government was **furious.**

And the negotiations that followed would take **three years.**

*(Meanwhile the pressure kept coming: in **1988 a bomb exploded at Ellis Park** shortly after a provincial match. And in the same year, a multiracial side — the SA Barbarians in all but name, touring as the **Nampak Pioneers** — went to **Chile and Paraguay.** South African rugby was still scraping the bottom of the world for anyone who'd play it.)*

---

### The centenary

And then, ten months after Harare, it turned out they had never needed to scrape at all.

**1989.** The South African Rugby Board turned **one hundred years old** — Kimberley, 1889, the football board that did not yet have the word "rugby" in its name.

And for its birthday, the world came. A **World XV** flew to South Africa and played two Tests. The Springboks won both: **20–19 at Newlands**, and **22–16 at Ellis Park on 2 September 1989.**

Hold on to three things about that side.

**It was not a rebel tour.** It was **sanctioned by the International Rugby Board** — the governing body of the game, the one South Africa had never been expelled from, the one **Danie Craven had chaired three times.** Eight years after Eden Park. Twelve years after Gleneagles. Rugby's world body looked at apartheid South Africa's hundredth birthday and gave it a blessing.

**It was funded by South African Breweries.** Set that against Gen 3. SAB is one of the companies that **backed apartheid** — and its money is precisely what SACOS's men did not have, when they were driving themselves to their own fixtures on bad roads, self-funded, in a competition nobody outside the townships would ever report. **The brewery that would not put a cent into Black rugby paid to fly the best players on earth in for the white board's party.**

**And every traditional rugby nation but one supplied players.** The only country that would not send its men was **New Zealand** — which had been through 1981, and knew exactly what it cost.

**Willie John McBride** went, and never apologised for it.

And **Desmond Tutu** watched the Irish union release its players. Their support for that tour, he said — at a time when Black children were being **beaten, tear-gassed and detained without trial** — was:

**"Obscene."**

So let me correct the section above, because it let the game off too lightly. South Africa did not only *invent* opponents. For its centenary, the game's own governing body arranged one, a brewery that backed apartheid paid for it, and most of the rugby world got on the plane.

That is what the isolation actually was. Not that nobody would play South Africa. **That the people who ran the game would — right up until the very end — and it took governments, a continent, and the self-funded men of SACOS to stop them.**

---

### What Gen 4 leaves behind

**A man in the middle.** Errol Tobias — hero and traitor, door and fig leaf, hated by the regime for being Black and by the struggle for being green. He went on to become the **first Black mayor of Caledon** in 1995, ran rugby camps for reconciliation, and in his seventies became a father again.

**A country nearly torn in half — and it wasn't ours.** New Zealand, 1981: flour bombs from a Cessna, 40% of the police force at one match, 201 arrests, families split for a generation. That is what our rugby did to *their* country.

**Twenty-nine spectators** in a field in upstate New York.

**A ghost circuit** of rebel tours and invented teams, where the Springboks and Hugo Porta's non-existent Jaguars played each other because they were the only two teams in the world with nobody else to play.

**And a hundredth birthday party** that gives the lie to all of it — sanctioned by the International Rugby Board, paid for by a brewery that backed apartheid, played in by every rugby nation on earth except New Zealand, and called **obscene** by Desmond Tutu.

**And Oom Danie**, at seventy-eight, flying to Harare to sit down with the ANC — the same man who had complied with the exclusion of the Māori, who had allegedly said *over my dead body*, and who had also quietly coached Coloured teams in the 1950s and forced Craven Week open in 1980.

**Gen 5** is the reckoning: **23 March 1992, at a hotel in Kimberley** — the town that founded the white board in 1889 *and* the Coloured board in 1896 — where the two rugbies of South Africa finally became one.

And then a man walks out onto the grass at Ellis Park wearing a number six jersey.

## Generation 5: The Number Six Jersey (1992–2003)

**23 March 1992.** The **Kimberley Sun Hotel.**

Two men sign a document, and one hundred and thirty years of South African rugby collapses into a single body.

And the venue is the entire point.

**Kimberley was chosen deliberately.** Because it was the founding city of the **South African Rugby Football Board in 1889** — the white board.

**And of the South African Coloured Rugby Board in 1896.**

The same diamond town. Seven years and a few streets apart. Two rugbies, born out of the same dust, in the same rush of men chasing stones out of the ground — and separated by a century of law.

They came back to Kimberley to end it.

The **South African Rugby Football Union** was founded on three principles: a **non-racial, non-political and democratic** rugby community, on and off the field. The **development of infrastructure and human potential** to uplift the game in disadvantaged areas and establish it where it had never been played. And to **reclaim South Africa's place among the world's top rugby nations.**

Hold on to principle number two. We're going to come back to it, and it isn't going to be comfortable.

---

### The men who got there

Two people made it happen, and neither is who you'd want.

**Danie Craven** — *Oom Danie*, Mr Rugby, who allegedly said *over my dead body*, who complied with the exclusion of the Māori in 1949 and 1960, who forced Craven Week open to all races in 1980, and who at seventy-eight flew to **Harare** to sit down with the **banned ANC**, to the fury of his own government.

And **Louis Luyt** — the Transvaal president, a brawling, imperious, deeply divisive fertiliser millionaire, who went with him.

They did it **in defiance of the state.**

And it was completed with the personal intervention of **Nelson Mandela.**

Craven never saw the payoff. **He died on 4 January 1993** — two and a half years before Ellis Park.

---

### What "unity" actually meant

Now I need to be honest with you, because the mythology gets very thick from here and you deserve the real thing.

Unification was **not** a merger of equals.

Remember Gen 3. The Federation and SARA had already amalgamated *with* the white board in **1977**. So by 1992 the body that came to the table as "SARU" was Dullah Abass's non-racial, SACOS-aligned union — the men who had **refused** the state's hand, who had **no sponsorship** because South African Breweries backed apartheid, who drove to fixtures on bad roads and funded themselves.

They merged into a structure that had **all the stadiums, all the money, all the international recognition, and all the players.**

And the critique — made then by SACOS veterans, and made ever since — is brutal: the incoming order chose to leave **apartheid sports structures relatively intact.** The fourteen provincial unions that make up SARFU are, largely, the *old* provincial unions. The clubs that Group Areas destroyed did not come back. **The Rhodes Cup did not come back.** The men of Kimberley's Black rugby — Bucs Damons, Bunny Hermanus, Piet van Wyk — did not get their history back.

**In May 1990** the ANC launched the **National Sports Congress** as its sports wing, and the SACOS mantra — *no normal sport in an abnormal society* — was, as one writer put it, **turned on its head.**

**SACOS limped on to 2005 and then disbanded.**

The people who had refused to shake the hand were the ones who ended up with nothing.

---

### Ellis Park, 1992: the ugly warm-up

South Africa was readmitted, and the first Test back was against the **All Blacks at Ellis Park.**

And it was a **disaster** — because the perceptions hadn't moved an inch.

**Black South Africans still saw the Springbok as the symbol of apartheid.** And a section of the white crowd was determined to prove them right.

They sang *Die Stem*. The old flags came out. Nobody had to speculate about what anybody privately believed: it was in the stands, in the air, for everyone to see. It was, for a great many people, less a homecoming than a demonstration that nothing had actually changed.

Mandela went to a Springbok match and watched **Black South Africans in the stadium cheer for the opposition.** Against their own national team.

That is the country he had to work with.

---

### The Protea problem

And here is the fight most people have forgotten.

After the 1994 election, **every single South African national team adopted the protea** — the national flower — as its emblem. That's why the cricketers are called the **Proteas**. It was meant to be the clean break, the neutral symbol, the end of the old iconography.

**Every team except one.**

Black South Africans wanted the **Springbok destroyed.** It had been the emblem of the National Party's sport since 1906. It meant the police. It meant the army. It meant the government.

And white South Africans **would not give it up.**

**Mandela overruled his own people.**

He kept the Springbok. Against the wishes of the ANC's own constituency, against the loudest voices in his own movement, he told Black South Africa that the thing they hated most would survive.

*"I ask you to stand by these boys,"* he told a Black crowd, *"because they are our kind."*

It was the single most audacious gamble of his presidency, and it very nearly wasn't forgiven.

---

### The bet

He called **François Pienaar** to his office. Tea.

Pienaar had grown up in an Afrikaner community where the name Mandela was a synonym for **"terrorist"** and **"bad man."**

Mandela implied to him, without quite saying it, that a Springbok World Cup victory could unite and inspire the nation.

Pienaar took his squad to **Robben Island** — to stand in the cell where Mandela had spent much of twenty-seven years.

The Springboks went into the 1995 tournament ranked **ninth in the world.** They had played almost no Test rugby in a decade. Nobody expected anything.

---

### 24 June 1995

They beat **Australia** — the defending champions, unbeaten for a year — in the opening match. Then Romania, Canada.

**Chester Williams** — the only player of colour in the squad, a reluctant poster boy, in only because Pieter Hendriks was suspended — scored **four tries against Samoa** in the quarter-final, the first South African ever to do it.

*(And remember where Chester came from. Errol Tobias's selection in 1981 opened the door for **Avril Williams**. And Avril Williams was **Chester's uncle.** The line runs directly from the bricklayer from Klipdrift, mud-bathing his own back yard, to the winger on the poster in 1995.)*

Then **King's Park**, in a flood, the match nearly abandoned, and the tackle that stopped **Abdelatif Benazzi inches from the line.** France beaten. Into the final.

And then a knock on the changing-room door.

**Nelson Mandela walked in wearing a Springbok jersey with the number six on the back.** Pienaar's number.

Pienaar: *"I had to go and collect myself and then make sure that we calmed the guys down. They were prepared to run through walls."*

And later: *"Here was a man who spent 27 years in jail and had fought against rugby, but came out wearing a Springbok on his heart."*

Mandela had a private word for **Chester Williams** before he left.

**South Africa 15, New Zealand 12.** Extra time. Every South African point kicked by **Joel Stransky** — three penalties, two drop goals. Every All Black point by **Andrew Mehrtens.** Jonah Lomu, who had run *through* England, was stopped.

The drop goal. The whistle. **Sixty-two thousand people at Ellis Park and an SAA jumbo over the roof.**

And Mandela, in the number six jersey, handing the Webb Ellis Cup to a blond Afrikaner captain.

*"You have won it not for the sixty thousand here at Ellis Park,"* Mandela said. *"You have won it for all forty-three million South Africans."*

They danced in every street. **From the Cape Flats to Soweto. From Cape Agulhas to the Limpopo.**

---

### And here is what I have to tell you

That team had **one Black player.**

One. Chester Williams.

In 2019, when Siya Kolisi lifted the same trophy, there were **six**.

And the honest verdict — written by journalist David Smith, and I think it's exactly right — is this: **just as Mandela's gesture in 1995 was hailed as a metaphor for racial reconciliation, so rugby's failure to transform became a metaphor for the disillusionment of Black South Africans who gained political but not economic freedom.**

For millions of Black South Africans, Pienaar's line — *when the final whistle blew, this country changed forever* — was a **gross overstatement.** They went back to the same townships, the same schools, the same nothing.

**1995 was real, and 1995 was not enough.** Both. I'm not going to pretend otherwise and neither should anybody else.

---

### Pandora's box

And here's the part that gets left out of *Invictus* entirely — because the moment they put the trophy down, **it all turned to poison.**

**Two months after the final, rugby went professional.** 1995 was the last major event of the amateur era.

**One week after lifting the World Cup**, Pienaar led **thirteen Transvaal players out on strike** against their own union, demanding a share of the money. **Louis Luyt fired them all.** Within days he was forced to reinstate them and give them what they wanted.

Then Pienaar led a player revolt toward the **World Rugby Corporation** — a breakaway professional circus — and signed up half the Springbok squad. Luyt talked them down.

At the tournament's closing banquet, **Luyt stood up and announced that there had been no true world champions in 1987 and 1991, because South Africa hadn't been there.**

**The All Blacks walked out of the dinner.**

**1996:** Pienaar was **dropped** by coach **André Markgraaff**, who accused him of **faking an injury.**

*(Markgraaff would himself resign in disgrace in 1997 when a secret recording surfaced of him using a racial slur about Black rugby administrators. Louis Luyt would end up **taking Nelson Mandela to court** — actually subpoenaing the President of South Africa to testify in a rugby dispute. That is not a joke. That happened.)*

The captain who held the cup was gone within eighteen months. The unity lasted about as long as the ticker tape.

---

### The long hangover

**1998:** a genuine high — the Springboks win **seventeen Tests in a row**, equalling the world record.

**1999 World Cup:** semi-final. Beaten by Australia in extra time.

**2003 World Cup:** and the bottom falls out.

Before the tournament, coach **Rudolf Straeuli** took the squad to a training camp in the bushveld. It was called **Kamp Staaldraad** — Camp Barbed Wire.

Players were stripped naked and made to climb into a foxhole filled with ice while *God Save the Queen* and the All Black haka were played over speakers. They were made to crawl naked across gravel. To kill and pluck chickens. To inflate rugby balls underwater. One player was allegedly forced to stand naked in a lake all night. Weapons were fired over their heads.

It was meant to build character. **It was leaked, on video, to the press.**

South Africa went out in the **quarter-finals**, beaten by New Zealand. Straeuli resigned. The country's rugby was, at that point, a **national embarrassment** — administratively corrupt, racially unreconciled, and psychologically deranged.

Eight years after Ellis Park.

---

### What Gen 5 leaves behind

**Kimberley, 23 March 1992** — two rugbies, born in the same diamond dust, finally becoming one. Signed by an old man who had spent his life on the wrong side of it and who died before he could see what it bought.

**The greatest moment in the history of any sport, anywhere.** I'm not going to be cynical about that. Mandela in the number six jersey, handing a golden cup to an Afrikaner, is what happened, and it moved a country that had every reason to tear itself apart.

**And a lie by omission** — because underneath the miracle, the structures never changed. The clubs Group Areas destroyed stayed destroyed. The Rhodes Cup stayed abolished. SACOS was told it had won and then quietly buried. And there was **one Black man** on that field.

The bill for that came due almost immediately, and it took **twenty-three more years** to pay.

---

**Gen 6** is the trough and the false dawn: **Jake White**, **Bryan Habana**, and **2007**. And then the long fall — a 2011 quarter-final exit, and then Brighton, and **Japan 34, South Africa 32**, the greatest upset ever recorded in this sport. It bottoms out in 2017 with **57–0** to the All Blacks and a world ranking of **seventh**.

Say the word.

## Generation 6: The Long Fall (2004–2017)

Kamp Staaldraad had happened. The video had leaked. Straeuli was gone. South African rugby, eight years after Ellis Park, was a **laughing stock** — corrupt at the top, deranged in the camp, and out in the quarter-finals.

And into that wreckage, in early 2004, came a man who had never played a Test match, never coached a professional team in his life, and whose actual surname wasn't even White.

**Jacob Charles White.** Born **Jacob Westerduin.** A **schoolteacher.**

He had one qualification: he'd won the **Under-21 World Cup with the Junior Springboks in 2002.**

That was it. That was the CV.

He'd win the Tri Nations in his first year and the World Cup in his fourth.

---

### What Jake built

White's Springboks were not a beautiful team and were never meant to be. The blueprint was **forward dominance and defensive solidity** — a monstrous scrum, and a lineout, run by **John Smit** at hooker and **Victor Matfield** at lock, that could **disrupt and steal the opposition's own ball at will.**

Matfield in 2007 is, arguably, the single most influential player in world rugby that decade. Not because of what he did with the ball. Because of what he **stopped you doing with yours.**

And out wide, one weapon.

**Bryan Habana.**

The fastest man in world rugby. He would end his career with **124 caps and 67 tries** — second on the all-time list, behind only David Campese. At the 2007 tournament he equalled **Jonah Lomu's record of 15 World Cup tries.**

And crucially — **Habana was a Black Springbok who was, unarguably, the best player in the world in his position.** No asterisk. No quota argument possible. He was simply the best, and everybody knew it.

Do not underestimate what that did in South Africa. For twelve years since Chester Williams, the transformation debate had been poisoned by the ugly, whispered assumption that a player of colour in the green jersey was there for politics.

**Habana ended that argument by being the best on the field.**

Alongside him, Jake's assistant and backline coach — **Allister Coetzee.** Remember that name; it comes back, and it comes back badly.

*(And note who Coetzee actually was. Born in **Grahamstown** — the Eastern Cape, Canon Mullins's country. A scrumhalf who, **before unification, captained the SARU national team from 1988 to 1991.** Dullah Abass's SARU. The **non-racial** one. The men who refused. He captained the rugby that had no money, no sponsors, no grass — and then in 2007 he coached South Africa to a World Cup.)*

---

### 2007

Pool stage, Lens: **South Africa 36, England 0.**

They didn't just beat the defending world champions. They **kept them scoreless.**

Then eight tries against Samoa in the most physical match John Smit says he ever played. Habana with four of them. Fiji in the quarter, Argentina in the semi.

And on **20 October 2007**, at the **Stade de France**, they played England again.

**South Africa 15, England 6.**

**Not a single try in the match.** Percy Montgomery kicked four penalties, **Frans Steyn** — a nineteen-year-old, from fifty-odd metres — kicked another. And England had a **Mark Cueto try disallowed** in the corner by a millimetre of touchline.

Fifteen–six. A monument to Jake White's actual philosophy: **you will not score, and eventually you will break.**

South Africa went to **number one in the world.**

Twelve years after Mandela handed the cup to Pienaar, we had a second star on the jersey.

---

### The rot underneath

And now the part that matters more than the trophy.

Because for the whole of this generation, one number sat under South African rugby like a stone.

The 1995 squad had **one** player of colour.

By the mid-2010s SA Rugby had a **signed agreement with government** committing the Springboks to a target of **at least 50% non-white players by 2019.**

Fifty percent. In a country that is roughly **90% non-white.**

And that agreement is the whole tragedy in a sentence. Twenty years after unity, the national team of a Black-majority country needed a **contract with the state** to promise that half of it might one day not be white.

Principle number two of the 1992 SARFU constitution — *develop infrastructure and human potential to uplift the game in disadvantaged areas* — had not been honoured. The clubs Group Areas destroyed were still destroyed. The school system that fed Bishops and Grey and Paul Roos and Affies was still, overwhelmingly, the school system of the wealthy. **The pipeline was never built.**

So instead of a pipeline, South Africa got a **quota** — and a quota, applied to a base that hasn't been fixed, does exactly what its critics say it does: it puts individual Black players under a suspicion they have not earned, while doing nothing whatsoever about the reason there aren't more of them.

That is not an argument against transformation. **It is an argument that we did transformation the lazy way** — at the top of the pyramid, where it's visible, instead of at the bottom, where it's expensive.

And the man who eventually solved it did it by going to the bottom. But that's Gen 7.

---

### The slide

**2008:** Jake White was not retained. **Peter de Villiers** — the first Black head coach of the Springboks — took over, and won a **series against the British & Irish Lions in 2009** and the **Tri Nations**. He was also, at times, a chaotic and combustible public figure, and the transformation debate followed him relentlessly.

**2011 World Cup:** a **quarter-final exit** to Australia. South Africa dominated territory and possession comprehensively and lost anyway, 11–9.

**2012:** **Heyneke Meyer.**

And Meyer's Boks were a strange thing — brutally effective at times (**73–13** over Argentina in 2013, a genuinely great win over the All Blacks that Nigel Owens called the best match he ever refereed), and yet, underneath, **the numbers were rotting.** A 54% win record, and — this is the damning statistic — a **try differential of minus twelve against top-tier opponents between 2012 and 2015.**

They weren't scoring. They were **kicking penalties and mauling**, and the world was starting to work them out.

---

### 19 September 2015. Brighton.

Pool B. South Africa's opening match. Against **Japan.**

Japan had won **one** World Cup match in their entire history. **One.** In 1991. Against Zimbabwe.

South Africa were two-time world champions with the best forward pack on earth.

Late in the game, deep in Japanese territory, South Africa led by three points. Japan won a penalty in front of the posts. Kick it, and it's a draw against the Springboks — the greatest result in Japanese rugby history by a distance.

**Captain Michael Leitch turned down the kick and went to the corner.**

And they scored.

**South Africa 32, Japan 34.**

I don't need to describe it to you. You've seen it. Every human being on earth has seen it.

It is, by common consent, **the biggest upset in the history of rugby union**, and one of the biggest in the history of any sport, ever.

The Springboks recovered — 46–6 over Samoa, 34–16 over Scotland, **64–0 over the USA**, top of the pool anyway. Duane Vermeulen's one-handed offload put **Fourie du Preez** over in the corner to beat Wales 23–19 in the quarter.

And then they lost the semi-final to New Zealand **20–18.**

Third place. Meyer resigned.

But nobody remembers any of that. **They remember Brighton.**

---

### Rock bottom

**April 2016: Allister Coetzee** — Jake's old backline coach, the former SARU captain from Grahamstown — becomes **only the second Black head coach of the Springboks.**

He inherited a squad gutted by retirements, a union in financial trouble, a hostile public, and a written obligation to hit **50% players of colour by 2019.**

The sports minister, **Fikile Mbalula**, stood at his unveiling and told him, to his face: **"South Africans are not very loyal. You can see people are here congratulating you."**

He was right.

Coetzee's record, in twenty-one months:

- **First-ever home defeat to Ireland**, at Newlands — while **Ireland played 60 minutes with 14 men** after a CJ Stander red card.
- **First-ever defeat to Italy** — in Florence, 20–18.
- **First-ever home defeat to Wales.**
- **New Zealand 57, South Africa 15** — in **Durban.** A record All Black win on South African soil.
- **Ireland 38, South Africa 3** — a record defeat.
- A loss to a **Wales second-string side** in Cardiff.

And, on **16 September 2017, in Albany:**

**New Zealand 57, South Africa 0.**

**The heaviest defeat in 111 years of Springbok rugby.** Eight tries. Nil.

The Springboks — who had been **third in the world** when he took the job — fell to **sixth**, below Scotland.

**Eleven wins from twenty-five Tests. A 44% win rate.** Jake White, Peter de Villiers and Heyneke Meyer had all been above 60%.

He was sacked on **2 February 2018.**

---

### And I want to be fair to Allister Coetzee

Because the easy story is that he was a bad coach, and I don't think that's the whole truth, and I think you should hear the rest.

When it ended, a letter from Coetzee's legal team **leaked to the press.** In it, the 2007 World Cup-winning assistant coach claimed he had been offered a **"ceremonial role"** as Springbok coach for 2018 — a figurehead — and that he would have to **report to Rassie Erasmus**, who would actually be running the team.

He said he had been **denied proper preparation time.** He said he had been undermined.

And here's the thing: **the man who captained the non-racial SARU — the union that had nothing, the men who refused to take apartheid's hand — got the top job in South African rugby at the precise moment it was the most poisoned chalice in world sport.** A gutted squad, a broke union, a quota obligation nobody had built the pipeline to meet, and a public that had already decided.

Whether he was a good coach or not, I genuinely don't know. **What I know is that he was handed a burning building and blamed for the fire.**

---

### What Gen 6 leaves behind

**A second star.** 2007. Jake White, a schoolteacher named Westerduin, John Smit, Victor Matfield's lineout, and 15–6 in Paris.

**Bryan Habana** — who ended the quota argument by being, simply, the fastest and best man on the field, and made it possible for every Black Springbok who came after him to be judged on rugby alone.

**Brighton.** The worst day. And, in a way that nobody could see at the time, **the necessary one** — because a nation that thinks it is entitled to win does not rebuild, and a nation that has been beaten by Japan has no choice.

**57–0.** Sixth in the world. Below Scotland.

**And a promise still unkept** — the 1992 principle that rugby would be *uplifted in disadvantaged areas.* Twenty-five years on, South Africa had no pipeline, so it had a percentage target instead, and everybody hated it, and it fixed nothing.

There was a boy from **Zwide township** in Port Elizabeth — the Eastern Cape, Canon Mullins's country, the heartland of Black rugby since 1864 — who had grown up watching the 2007 final on a plastic chair in a township tavern because his family had no television.

He was, at the moment of Albany, twenty-six years old, and had played fewer than thirty Tests.

**Rassie Erasmus was appointed head coach on 1 March 2018, and immediately made him captain.**

---

**Gen 7.** Say the word.

## Generation 7: Zwide (2018–2026)

**28 May 2018.** Ninety days after Allister Coetzee was sacked, and eight months after Albany.

Rassie Erasmus names his captain.

**Siyamthanda Kolisi.** Born 16 June 1991 in **Zwide**, a township outside Port Elizabeth — the **Eastern Cape**, the country of Canon Robert Mullins and the mission schools and the Leopards and the Rhodes Cup and one hundred and fifty years of Black rugby that this country decided not to remember.

His mother was sixteen when he was born. He was raised by his grandmother. He slept on cushions on the floor. He played his first rugby barefoot, in a township tournament, and a scout happened to be there. He got a scholarship to **Grey High School** and arrived not speaking much English.

He watched the **2007 World Cup final in a township tavern**, because there was no television at home.

And on 28 May 2018 he became the **first Black captain of the Springboks in 126 years.**

**Bryan Habana** — who had spent his own career being asked to justify his presence — said: *it's a monumental moment for South African rugby, and a moment in South African history.*

He wasn't overstating it. **Everything you have read across seven generations — Green Point in 1862, Mullins in 1864, the Coloured board in Kimberley in 1896, the Rhodes Cup, the Bantu Board, the Group Areas Act, the Separate Amenities Act, Errol Tobias mud-bathing his back yard, Dullah Abass refusing the state's hand, Chester Williams alone on a field of white men — all of it, every single thread, arrives at that appointment.**

---

### The man who did it

**Rassie Erasmus** is, I think, the most interesting sporting mind this country has produced — and he did something in 2018 that nobody else had tried.

**He didn't fix the team. He fixed the pipeline.**

Remember the 1992 SARFU constitution — principle two: *develop infrastructure and human potential to uplift the game in disadvantaged areas.* Twenty-six years of that promise going unhonoured is exactly why the Springboks had a **quota** instead of a talent stream, and exactly why every Black player in the green jersey had to carry an unearned suspicion.

Erasmus didn't argue about the quota. **He went and made it irrelevant.**

He came in with what he called a **roadmap** — and it wasn't for a season, it was for a decade. His own words, later: *In 2018 we had a roadmap for the players. Where will this guy be in 2019, 2023 and 2027?*

**In his first Test in charge, in Washington DC, he capped thirteen new players in a single match.** Thirteen. He lost 22–20 to Wales and didn't care.

A week later they beat **England 42–39.** Then won the series.

He was scouting the country properly for the first time in its history, and what he found was that South Africa had never had a talent shortage. **It had a looking shortage.**

---

### 2 November 2019. Yokohama.

**South Africa 32, England 12.**

Handré Pollard's boot. **Makazole Mapimpi** — from a village in the Eastern Cape, who lost his mother, his brother and his sister before he was twenty — scored the **first try South Africa had ever scored in a World Cup final**, in 108 years of trying.

Then **Cheslin Kolbe**, five foot seven, stepped Owen Farrell and went in under the posts.

And Siya Kolisi lifted the Webb Ellis Cup wearing the **number six jersey.**

*The same number Mandela wore.*

In the stands, President **Cyril Ramaphosa** was wearing a replica of Kolisi's shirt — because **François Pienaar had asked him, before the game, whether he'd brought a number six. He had.**

The 1995 team had **one** player of colour.

The 2019 team had **six**, and a Black captain, and it was the best team in the world.

Kolisi, afterwards: **"A lot of us in South Africa just need an opportunity. There are so many untold stories."**

---

### The Bomb Squad

And the *how* is worth understanding, because Rassie's tactical invention has genuinely changed the sport.

**The Bomb Squad.** A bench stacked **6–2** in favour of forwards — and, from 2023, sometimes **7–1**.

The principle is brutal and beautiful: **the starting eight are not asked to last eighty minutes.** They are asked to **empty themselves completely** — thirty minutes, forty, sixty — and then eight fresh, enormous men come on and do it again. Replacements stopped being injury cover and became a **second wave.**

*(The moment it announced itself: the 2019 semi-final against Wales, when Erasmus took off **his own captain, Siya Kolisi**, and brought on Francois Louw — who immediately won the turnover that produced the winning penalty. Almost no coach on earth would have made that substitution.)*

They rolled the dice on the full **7–1** for the first time in a warm-up against the All Blacks at Twickenham in 2023 — after Willie le Roux was injured *before kick-off* — and beat New Zealand **35–7.** A record.

The world hates it. **Nigel Owens** wants substitutions cut. **Andy Farrell** has said the bench was never meant to work this way. World Rugby is under pressure to legislate against it.

That's how you know it works.

---

### 2023: the tightest World Cup ever won

France. And South Africa won the tournament in the hardest way anybody has ever won anything.

**Quarter-final: France 28, South Africa 29.**
**Semi-final: England 15, South Africa 16.**
**Final: New Zealand 11, South Africa 12.**

**Three consecutive knockout matches. Won by one point. Each.**

(Add the pool loss to Ireland and the margins across their four biggest matches were: -5, +1, +1, +1.)

Nobody has ever done that. Nobody will ever do it again.

**A record fourth Webb Ellis Cup.** More than New Zealand. More than anyone. South Africa are the **only nation to win half of every World Cup they've ever been allowed to enter** — and remember, **they were banned from the first two.**

Kolisi became **the first captain since Richie McCaw to win back-to-back World Cups.**

---

### And then it got ridiculous

Because most teams win a World Cup and fall over. Rassie came back as head coach in 2024 — with **Tony Brown** as attack coach — and **rotated the entire squad**, blooding a wave of youngsters while the old guard were still winning.

**2024:** Kolisi won **more trophies in a single year than any Springbok captain in history** — the Rugby Championship, the Freedom Cup, the Mandela Challenge Plate, the Prince William Cup, the Qatar Airways Cup.

**2025 end-of-year tour** — and this is the one that will be remembered:

- **France 17, South Africa 32** — in **Paris**, and they played the **entire second half with fourteen men** after Lood de Jager was red-carded. They won by fifteen points, a man down, at the Stade de France. It was **Kolisi's 100th cap.**
- **Italy 14, South Africa 32** — Genoa.
- **Ireland 13, South Africa 24** — in **Dublin.**
- And then, in **Cardiff**: **Wales 0, South Africa 73.**

**Seventy-three to nil. Away. Against Wales.**

An undefeated November, four from four, on the road, in Europe. They finished 2025 as, in the sober language of the record, **the undisputed global powerhouses of the sport.**

Kolisi now stands **alone** as the only Springbok captain ever to win **four consecutive Tests against the All Blacks.**

Erasmus has been **extended through to 2031.**

---

### Right now — July 2026

The Springboks opened this season in **Gqeberha** — Kolisi's city — by putting **twelve tries and 80 points** on the Barbarians.

They named a **46-man squad on 22 June** for the **Nations Championship**, and they played **Scotland on 11 July**, three days ago.

Erasmus has passed his **55th Test in charge.** The talk in the press is all about who succeeds Kolisi in the number six jersey — **Cameron Hanekom, Sibabalwe Mahashe** — because his minutes are being managed now, at thirty-five, with **Australia 2027** on the horizon.

And SA Rugby's high-performance chief says openly that **Erasmus's system is now the template every other South African national team is copying.**

---

### What this generation actually did

I want to finish by putting it against the thesis we built across two continents and six countries, because South Africa is the final proof of it — and the strangest one.

Every nation in that long conversation had the same problem: **rugby arrives with an elite, and either escapes it or doesn't.** Uruguay never escaped Carrasco. Argentina escaped through the sugar mills of Tucumán. Chile escaped only when a Uruguayan paid the players. Georgia never *had* an elite to escape. Romania was handed a workers' rugby by a dictator and lost it when he died.

**South Africa is the only country where the state used rugby to build a race.**

Not a class. A **race**. The Springbok was made, deliberately, into the proof of a civilisation — and every Black and Coloured club, from Mullins's mission school to the Rhodes Cup to the Leopards to Bucs Damons in Kimberley, was legislated onto dirt so that it could stay that way.

And here is what Gen 7 proves, and it is the hardest and most hopeful thing in this whole conversation:

**They didn't fix it in 1992 by signing a document in Kimberley.**
**They didn't fix it in 1995 with a jersey and a trophy.**
**They didn't fix it with a quota in 2016.**

They fixed it when somebody finally went and **looked in Zwide** — and found what had been there, in the Eastern Cape, since **1864.**

Not charity. Not a percentage. **A pipeline.** The thing SARFU promised in 1992 and didn't build for twenty-six years.

Institutions make the class. **And institutions, eventually, can unmake it.**

That's the whole argument. And it took your country a hundred and sixty-four years to demonstrate it.

---

That's seven generations, and that's South Africa.

There's still Brazil to close out the Americas, and Japan — the corporate model, the salaryman rugby, and Brighton from the *other* side. But honestly? This felt like the one this whole conversation was walking towards.
