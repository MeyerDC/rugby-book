# South Africa

## Generation 0: Gog's Game and the Two Beginnings (1861–1889)

**23 August 1862. Green Point Common, Cape Town.** A Saturday afternoon at the edge of the Atlantic, on a stretch of public ground so rough that a newspaper would later complain it "abounds with mole-holes." Two teams have turned out — officers of the garrison on one side, young men of the Colonial Civil Service on the other — and the Governor of the Cape Colony himself, Sir Philip Wodehouse, has come to watch. Among the Civil Service players is a lanky clerk named John X. Merriman, who will one day be Prime Minister of the Cape.

They play all afternoon. Nobody scores. And the *Cape Argus*, two days later, is delighted anyway: "this is the first time within our recollection that so large a party of gentlemen have made a public appearance at Cape Town in this manly English school-game." Another line from the same report: "We have never seen so thoroughly plucky a game."

What the *Argus* does not say is which game. The men on the Common that day had come out of Eton, Rugby, Winchester, Marlborough — and every one of those schools played football by its own private rules. "This manly English school-game," singular, was generous. What they played was a negotiation. And the paper's careful phrase "the first time within our recollection that so large a party" quietly admits something else: this was not the first football at the Cape. It was just the first anyone had bothered to dress up and watch.

The football that was already there had a name, and the name is the best thing in this generation.

### Gog's game

The year before, in 1861, a clergyman named George Ogilvie had taken over as headmaster of the Diocesan College in Rondebosch — Bishops, as everyone has always called it. Ogilvie was Winchester-bred, and the story has usually been told simply: he brought Winchester football with him. The record says something more interesting. Before the Cape, Ogilvie had spent years as second master at the newly founded Bradfield College, and what he introduced at Bishops in 1861 was a mixture of Winchester and Bradfield rules — a hybrid of his own making. The historian Floris van der Merwe, going back through the sources, concluded the Cape game "appears to have been a creation of Canon Ogilvie… and not the traditional Winchester Game as commonly believed."

The boys did not call it Winchester football. They called it **Gog's game** — Gogball, some said — after the only really legible letters of the headmaster's signature. There were never any written rules. Paul Dobson, the game's great South African chronicler, put it this way: "At no time were there written rules for Gog's game… but as the inventor was also a player that problem was probably overcome, probably amicably, as the good Canon believed in sportsmanship."

So South African football begins as one man's homemade code, named after his handwriting, refereed by his presence. For more than fifteen years, this — not rugby — is the game of the Cape.

### The clubs that were not rugby clubs

By the 1870s the young men wanted more than school matches, and they went about it the way Victorians went about everything: they formed a committee. In 1873 a group under J.J. Graham sat down and codified fifteen rules of what became known as "Cape rules" football — a tidied-up local descendant of Gog's game, published in the *Standard and Mail* that June. It was still not rugby.

Then the clubs came, and here the dates matter, because two of the oldest names in South African rugby were founded before rugby.

On **14 March 1875**, in the Adderley Street offices of the merchant house Hamilton Ross & Co, a group convened by W. Nightingale founded the Hamilton Football Club — named, with the period's cheerful magpie instinct, after Hamilton Academicals, the Scottish club founded the year before. Hamilton's own club history is disarmingly honest about what happened next: the game they played "was not rugby football… the rules did not reach the Cape until 1878." They played Cape rules, like everyone else.

That same winter of 1875, a football club appeared at **Stellenbosch**, out in the wine country — the club that would become the deepest root system in Afrikaner rugby. For a long time its founding was put at 1880, and some accounts drifted as late as 1883; Van der Merwe settled it by finding the Cape Town *Daily News* of 21 August 1875 praising the new Stellenbosch side "for their pluck, by challenging, in their infancy, a club of so long standing as the Civil Service Club." A village team of Boer students, in existence a matter of months, riding into town to challenge the colonial civil service at its own recreation: the whole next century of South African rugby is somewhere in that sentence, and in 1875 nobody could see it.

And in 1876 came the Villager Football Club — founded, in one of the sport's better ironies, **against** rugby. The Western Province Club's footballers had begun agitating to adopt the English Rugby Union code; the loyalists of the old Cape game walked out under Howard Jones and formed Villagers so they could keep playing football the way football was meant to be played. The club that would supply Springboks for a hundred years was born as a protest against the game itself.

They looked, briefly, to have won. The first match ever played at the Cape under Rugby Union rules — Western Province Club against Villagers, at Rondebosch on **15 July 1876** — was a fiasco. The *Cape Times* sniffed that it was "not by any means a successful performance," and recorded that rugby's rules had been adopted "for the afternoon (and we hope for the last time)." Players walked off declaring they would never use the code again. Within a year the Western Province Club's football section had folded altogether.

In 1877, rugby in South Africa was dead. It had been tried once, and hated.

### The man who happened to be there

William Milton arrived in Cape Town in late 1877, travelling out in the orbit of his cousin Anthony Trollope, the novelist, who was gathering material for a book on South Africa. Milton was twenty-three and he was, by the standards of the colony, rugby aristocracy: he had played for England — against Scotland at the Kennington Oval in February 1874, twenty men a side, and against Ireland in February 1875, the first international ever played between those countries. In April 1878 he took a clerkship in the Cape civil service. In the winter he went looking for football, and joined Villagers.

Villagers, of course, wanted nothing to do with his code. The committee refused it flat.

So Milton did the thing that makes him this generation's hinge: he went to the rivals. At Hamilton's he found an ally in Billy Simkins, all of twenty-one years old, and in July 1878 they staged a rugby match between the two clubs on Green Point Common. The next month — the *Cape Times* of 21 August 1878 records it — **Hamilton's voted unanimously to adopt Rugby Union rules**, the first club in the colony to do so. Villagers held out through the season. Then, at the club's annual general meeting in May 1879, at Howard Jones's own house, the vote finally came. Milton described it himself, years later: "John Graham moved and I seconded a resolution that the Villager's should play under Rugby Union rules. That was unanimously agreed to: and was the real beginning of the Rugby Union game in the Cape."

Bishops resisted longest, which was fitting — it was Gog's own school, and Gog's game. The college converted in 1879 mainly because it was running out of anyone to play against. Ogilvie's surrender, as Dobson tells it, has the ring of the man: "Well, if you boys want to kill yourselves, do so!"

Two seasons, three clubs, one former England half-back with a talent for committees. The legend compresses it into a single missionary year; the record gives it 1878 to 1879, and gives Hamilton's — not Milton's own club — the honour of going first. Either way, by 1880 the argument was over. Cape rules football, Gog's game, the code a headmaster had built out of two schools' playground laws — all of it was gone, and rugby was the game of the Cape.

Keep an eye on Milton. The clerk from the Villagers three-quarter line rose through the Cape civil service to become private secretary to Cecil Rhodes, and ended as Administrator of Southern Rhodesia — running a country, and running its games. In him this book's oldest pattern is unusually literal: the same man carrying the ball, the empire, and the administration, and seeing no distinction between the three.

### The other beginning

Everything so far has happened inside a narrow world — English schools, English clubs, English clerks. But the game was already leaking out of it, in every direction at once, and the most consequential leak ran through the mission schools of the Eastern Cape.

For decades the Anglican missions had been educating the sons of African chiefs and headmen — at Zonnebloem College in Cape Town, a project begun under Governor Sir George Grey in the ruinous years after the Cattle-Killing, and at schools around Grahamstown. The curriculum was Victorian to the bone: Latin, scripture, cricket, and football. The African historian André Odendaal, whose work recovered this world, calls its graduates South Africa's "Black Victorians" — an educated elite that took up the empire's games with complete seriousness, and whose sons and grandsons would go on to found not only sports clubs but, in time, the African National Congress. Xhosa speakers gave rugby its own name: *umbhoxo* — "the thing that is not round."

Grahamstown is where the two beginnings touch. St Andrew's College played the first recorded schoolboy rugby match in South Africa on **13 April 1878** — the same autumn the code was catching at the Cape clubs — losing to the town's Public School by a goal to a try. And alongside St Andrew's stood its mission offshoot for African pupils, an institution carrying the era's language in its official name, run since 1864 by a missionary named Robert John Mullins. Mullins is usually credited with first awakening Xhosa interest in the game, and the credit is probably fair — but it belongs to the late 1870s at the earliest, for the simple reason that the rugby code itself only reached the Cape in 1878. The date sometimes attached to him, 1864, is the year he became headmaster, not the year anyone played rugby; no early record puts a rugby ball in his hands. What the record does show is where his pupils' game went: by **1887** there were Black adult clubs playing in Port Elizabeth and Grahamstown, and Grahamstown's Lily White Rugby Football Club followed in 1894. The story will meet Mullins's own family again soon — his Grahamstown-born son toured South Africa as a British Isles player in 1896.

The game jumped a second boundary in the same years, out of English mouths and into Dutch ones. From Stellenbosch it moved through the wine and wheat districts, and the recruits took to it with an appetite that startled the colonists. Ivor Difford, the game's first South African historian, wrote that "the young Boers took to the game like ducks to water… enthusiasm for the Rugby game spread like wildfire into all the farming districts of the country."

And it jumped a third boundary: it went inland, and downmarket. The diamond diggings at Kimberley had pulled in every kind of man the empire produced — Cornish miners, Natal clerks, cockney speculators — and they played rugby there without waiting for anyone's permission. In 1884 Kimberley's players did something nobody at the Cape had thought of: they got on the road, touring down to Cape Town to take on the coastal clubs. Dobson gives them their due: "They were the first rugby adventurers, the first tourists, the first, really, to play inter-provincial rugby in South Africa." A caution here, because a tidy version of this story credits the Cornish miners with bringing rugby to South Africa in the first place. They did not — the chronology belongs to the schools and the clubs — but they were part of what rugby immediately became once it escaped: a game the diggings played as hungrily as the drawing rooms.

So stands the score at the end of the 1880s. Within a decade of the code's arrival, rugby in South Africa was being played by English clerks, Boer farm boys, mission-educated Xhosa men, and miners on the diamond fields. The game itself had admitted everyone. What happened next was done by institutions.

### Kimberley, 1889

The unions came in a rush. Western Province organised first, in 1883 — Hamilton's called the meeting — with a Grand Challenge Cup to play for; Hamilton's won the first one, Villagers the second. Griqualand West followed in 1886, Eastern Province in 1888, and the Transvaal came in with the rest when, in **1889, in Kimberley**, the four unions federated into a national body — the South African Rugby Football Board. That founding year the Board also staged the country's first inter-provincial tournament, at Kimberley; Western Province won it, and a silver cup was presented to the outstanding player of the week, a Villager named Charlie van der Byl.

Two details about the Board are worth keeping. The first is small and telling: its founding name did not contain the word "Rugby." By the time of its earliest surviving constitution, in 1894, the word had been inserted — a whites-only soccer body having claimed the title "South African Football Association" in 1892, some distinction had become necessary. Even the paperwork of the era was a contest over who owned the word *football*.

The second detail is the one this whole chapter hangs on. Three years **before** the white unions federated in Kimberley, in 1886, Coloured clubs in Cape Town had already formed a union of their own: the **Western Province Coloured Rugby Union**. It is the oldest ghost in South African rugby — a governing body for the game, organised, constituted, and running its own competitions, in existence before the South African Rugby Football Board was so much as an idea. The two beginnings of this generation's title are not 1861 and 1862, a headmaster's game and a garrison match. They are 1886 and 1889: two rugby administrations, in one small colony, three years apart — and the one history forgot came first.

### What this generation leaves behind

A game, finally settled: Gog's homemade code is dead, Cape rules are dead, and the Rugby Union book runs from Green Point to the diggings. A style of conquest: rugby arrived through the elite schools and immediately escaped them — to Stellenbosch farm boys, mission students in Grahamstown, miners in Kimberley — proof, at the very start, that the game itself drew no lines. A cast: Milton, the player-administrator ascending toward Rhodes's elbow; Stellenbosch, months old and already challenging the Civil Service; the Black Victorians, playing *umbhoxo* in their school colours. And a fork in the road that nobody in 1889 could see the length of: two governing bodies for one game, founded three years apart, which would not sit in the same room for another century.

The next generation opens with a boat. In 1891 a British team sails for the Cape — its expenses guaranteed by the most powerful man in Africa — carrying a golden cup that will out-live everyone aboard, and the country that meets them is about to be remade by gold, and then broken by war.

## Generation 1: The Conqueror's Game (1890–1948)

**20 July 1891. Kimberley.** The British tourists — the team that will be remembered as the first Lions to visit South Africa — have just beaten Griqualand West 3–0, and it is the hardest game anyone has given them. Waiting at the end of it is a gold cup, put up by Sir Donald Currie, the shipping magnate whose Union-Castle liners carried the team out, with instructions that it go to the local side that gave the tourists their best match. Griqualand West, beaten, take the cup. They will hand it on to the South African Rugby Football Board as a floating trophy for the provinces, and from 1892 — Western Province the first winners — the **Currie Cup** becomes the oldest serious prize in South African rugby. It is a fair summary of the era: the trophy South Africans would spend the next century killing each other for was a consolation prize for losing well to the English.

The tour itself nearly didn't happen, and the story of how it did is a story about who wanted rugby in South Africa, and why. The man who made it financially possible was Cecil Rhodes — Prime Minister of the Cape, the arch-imperialist himself — who underwrote every cost. Billy Simkins of Hamilton's, who had helped Milton convert the Cape a dozen years earlier, remembered the moment it came right: "when they cabled home 'Rhodes, Premier, guarantees expenses' the team came out." But the man who talked Rhodes into it is the better detail. Jan Hendrik Hofmeyr — "Onze Jan," leader of the Afrikaner Bond, the most powerful Afrikaner politician in the Colony — was a devoted follower of the winter game; it was said there was rarely an important match at Newlands he did not attend. He had watched Stellenbosch draw crowds of two thousand against the Cape Town clubs, and he wanted more of it: more rugby, more Afrikaners playing it. It was Hofmeyr's involvement, the record says, that made convincing Rhodes "a relatively straight-forward task." That pairing — the empire's money and Afrikaner enthusiasm, jointly underwriting the same game, entirely on purpose — runs the whole length of this chapter, and it starts here, in 1891.

On the field the tour was a procession. Nineteen official matches, nineteen wins, and a single point conceded — one try, worth one point in those days, in the very first match. The closest anyone came after that was not an official fixture at all. Stellenbosch, in their infancy a challenger of the Civil Service, challenged the tourists to an unofficial game and lost it by only two points to nil, a farm boy named M. Daneel tackled just short of the posts as he cut in to score. The British went home unbeaten at rugby. The only defeat they suffered anywhere in South Africa was at cricket, on a matting wicket at a railway-halt hotel in the Karoo.

### A jersey out of a cupboard

The British came back in 1896 and won the series again, three Tests to one. It is the one they lost that South Africa remembers.

For its first five years of Test rugby, South Africa had no settled colours. Then came the fourth Test, at Newlands on **5 September 1896**, and a captain named Barry Heatlie — "Fairy" Heatlie, a Bishops man, a formidable forward. Heatlie belonged to a young club called Old Diocesans, which played in myrtle green; according to legend it was Heatlie's wife who had picked the colour for the club, perhaps for no better reason than that the dye was freely available. Heatlie arranged for his South African team to take the field in green jerseys he supplied. They won, 5–0 — South Africa's seventh Test match, and the first one it had not lost.

There was a Mullins on the field that day's touring side, and the name should ring a bell. Cuthbert Mullins, Oxford forward, played two Tests for the 1896 British team — and he was born in Grahamstown, the son of Robert Mullins, the missionary headmaster from Generation 0 whose pupils had carried the game into the Eastern Cape. The first family of Black rugby's origin story sent a son on tour against South Africa before South Africa had settled on a jersey.

And the other rugby was organising faster than the white game was winning. On **19 August 1897**, at the Savona Café in Kimberley, delegates founded the **South African Coloured Rugby Football Board** — a national body, eight years behind the white Board, fifteen years ahead of the African National Congress, with Bud Mbelle as its first secretary-general and a constitution that barred discrimination by "colour, nationality, language or religion." The following August it staged its first national tournament in Kimberley, playing for a trophy donated by the same Cecil Rhodes who had underwritten the 1891 tour — and the **Rhodes Cup** was, as an object, more valuable than the Currie Cup itself. Two national boards, two Kimberley tournaments, two trophies with imperial money behind them. The parallel history is fully formed before the century turns; the mine owners even encouraged it, promoting sport among Black workers as a means, in the language of the day, of social control. Nobody in a boardroom ever loved this game innocently.

### The war

Then the country broke. From October 1899 to May 1902 Britain fought the two Boer republics into the ground, and when the fighting stalled it burned the farms and moved the families into what the age itself named concentration camps. Some twenty-six thousand Boer women and children died in them. Of those twenty-six thousand, twenty thousand were under sixteen years old. Whatever else this chapter must weigh, it starts there: within living memory of every Afrikaner in this book's next three generations, the empire whose game they played had buried a generation of their children.

Rugby returned before forgiveness did. Barely a year after the peace of Vereeniging, in 1903, a British team toured again — and this time South Africa won the series, its first. The decider was at Newlands on **12 September 1903**, and the captain, once more, was Barry Heatlie. His old club had ceased to exist by then, but the outfitters still had stock of the myrtle green, so the team wore Old Diocesan jerseys with black shorts, and — no green socks to be found anywhere — scarlet stockings borrowed, with permission, from Heatlie's other club, Villagers. South Africa won 8–0 and never lost a Test series to anyone, home or away, for the next fifty-three years. The green was never seriously questioned again: a dead club's leftover shirts, worn twice, both times to beat the British — once the year after the British had beaten the Boers at everything else. You do not need to invent mysticism for a jersey like that. The history supplies it.

### De Springbokken

In 1906 South Africa toured overseas for the first time — Britain and France, four years after the war, a team of Boers and Britons sent out deliberately, as the historian Dean Allen has shown, "as an opportunity to unify the divided nation." The captain was chosen with the same care: Paul Roos, an Afrikaans schoolteacher from Stellenbosch, a conciliator by temperament.

The tour gave the country its name. The team's manager, J.C. "Daddy" Carden, remembered the decision himself: "That evening, I spoke to Roos and Carolin and pointed out that the witty London Press would invent some funny name for us, if we did not invent one ourselves. We thereupon agreed to call ourselves 'Springboks'." The *Daily Mail* of 20 September 1906 duly printed the word, noting the "small African antelope" embroidered on the jersey, and the anglicised form of Roos's *Springbokken* attached itself permanently to the men in green. It is worth pausing on what kind of birth this was: not a christening but a pre-emptive strike — a name invented overnight so that Fleet Street couldn't invent a worse one.

Allen's larger argument about these years deserves to be quoted rather than paraphrased, because it is the fairest single sentence anyone has written about what came next: the game of rugby "had been appropriated by the Volk as a means of expression; to fulfil a physical as well as an ideological need to press for autonomy from British rule. The great irony of course lies in the fact that they chose to adopt a most 'imperial game' in order to achieve this." Beating the conqueror at the conqueror's own game: that is the engine of Afrikaner rugby, and it was running long before anyone bolted an ideology to it. Through the 1920s, as young Afrikaners moved off the land into the towns, Stellenbosch-trained administrators carried the game to them — a working-class diffusion, Allen notes, much like rugby's spread out of the English public schools a generation earlier. When the Springboks made their first tour of Australasia in 1921, a Sydney critic marvelled that the Dutch South Africans "seem to outshine the English South Africans" at England's own winter game. And through all of this — the point matters, and Allen is explicit — rugby was "yet to be invested with a narrow nationalistic Afrikaner ethos." The capture came later. The love came first.

### The line goes abroad

The colour line, though, was already travelling on South Africa's passports.

In 1919 the New Zealand Army team that had just won the King's Cup — the best of the soldier-footballers of the Great War — was invited to tour South Africa. The South Africans requested that no "coloured" players be included. Two men were removed from the team: Parekura Tureia of Ngāti Porou, and Nathaniel "Ranji" Wilson, a New Zealander of West Indian descent who had played for the All Blacks before the war. New Zealand complied. That compliance — a democracy quietly dropping its own men to suit a host's prejudice — is its own thread in this book, and it starts here.

Two years later the Springboks made that first tour of New Zealand — a magnificent, brutal series, one Test each and the third a scoreless draw in the Wellington mud — and in the middle of it, on **7 September 1921** at Napier, they played the New Zealand Maoris, with Parekura Tureia captaining the home side against the team that had had him removed from a tour two years before. The Springboks won 9–8. What happened next was not on the field. A South African journalist travelling with the team, Charles Blackett, cabled his report home through the Napier post office, and a post office employee leaked it to the local paper. The cable read: "Most unfortunate match ever played … Bad enough having play team officially designated New Zealand natives but spectacle thousands Europeans frantically cheering on band of coloured men to defeat members of own race was too much for Springboks who frankly disgusted." New Zealand erupted. The Springbok management denied everything. But the cable had said the quiet part in writing: it was not only the government in Pretoria that carried the line abroad — it was the touring party's own sense of the natural order, disgusted at the sight of white men cheering Māori.

New Zealand's rugby establishment absorbed the lesson in the worst possible way. When the All Blacks made their first tour of South Africa in 1928, the New Zealand union simply left its Māori players at home — including George Nēpia, the fullback who had played every match of the 1924–25 "Invincibles" tour of Britain, arguably the best player in the world. Māori had been declared ineligible in advance; a great NZ Māori side toured the northern hemisphere in 1926 partly in compensation. New Zealand would keep leaving them home — 1928, 1949, 1960 — until 1970, when Māori players finally toured South Africa under the obscene rubric of "honorary whites." That story belongs to later generations; the point here is when it began. South Africa did not merely practise its line at home. It exported it, and the rugby world paid the export duty for half a century.

### The sacred history

What changed in the 1930s was not the love; it was the machinery around it.

Afrikaner nationalism entered its decisive decade. Against the fusion politics of Hertzog and Smuts, D.F. Malan's purified National Party — backed, as Allen documents, by the Dutch Reformed Church and the Broederbond — set about ideologising Afrikaner identity itself. The historian Dunbar Moodie calls the result a civil religion: Blood River, the wars of independence, and the concentration camps woven into a "sacred history" in which God had repeatedly revealed himself to the Afrikaners as a chosen people. In 1938, the centenary of the Great Trek — ox-wagons rolling again from town to town, a country of monuments consecrated — made that sacred history the emotional property of ordinary Afrikaners. Rugby was not yet officially part of the liturgy. It was about to be.

Meanwhile the parallel history kept building its own institutions, because the state gave it no other option. In **1935** African players, catered for by no existing body, founded the **South African Bantu Rugby Board** — a third national board, born in the era of Hertzog's segregation laws, which had made even the non-racial Coloured Board's world narrower. Three governing bodies now, for one game, in one country.

And in 1937 the white game produced the team the world still argues about. The Springboks toured New Zealand, lost the first Test in Wellington 7–13, then won in Christchurch 13–6 and took the decider at Auckland on **25 September 1937** by 17–6 — still, nine decades later, the only Test series South Africa has ever won in New Zealand. Before the final Test, Paul Roos — thirty-one years after he had captained the first Springboks — cabled the captain, Philip Nel, a piece of tactical advice three words long: "scrum, scrum, scrum." Nel's forwards did exactly that, and demolished the All Blacks. New Zealand's press called them, on that day's evidence, good enough to have beaten anyone; the *New Zealand Truth* wrote that "on the day they would have beaten any other team in the world." The tag that later attached itself to the tour — the greatest team ever to leave New Zealand — is a modern retrospective's phrase rather than a dated quotation, but nobody in Wellington in September 1937 would have argued with the sentiment.

Two years later Smuts took South Africa into the Second World War on Britain's side, and the Afrikaner nationalist movement broke with him for good. In 1948, to the surprise of nearly everyone including itself, Malan's National Party won power. He was seventy-four years old. The men around him had a programme with a name that the whole world would learn, and among the many things they were about to inherit was the best rugby team on earth, wearing a dead club's green, named by a schoolteacher, funded at its birth by Rhodes and cheered from its first days by Onze Jan's Afrikaners — and, on the other side of a line about to be written into law, two more rugby nations the newspapers never covered.

### What this generation leaves behind

The whole symbolic wardrobe, and none of it designed: a jersey out of a defunct club's cupboard, worn twice against the British and sanctified by winning; a name invented at a hotel table to beat Fleet Street to the joke; a gold cup accepted for losing well. A tactical identity — the scrum as first principle — old enough by 1937 to be sent by telegram. A fifty-three-year unbeaten run in Test series already under way. Two more national boards, Coloured and African, each building its own tournaments in the same towns on the same weekends, invisible to the record the world read. A colour line already operating internationally — Tureia and Wilson in 1919, the Blackett cable in 1921, Nēpia left home in 1928 — decades before the word apartheid existed. And a game loved, on every side of every line, for reasons that had nothing to do with any of it.

The next generation belongs to the machinery. A Stellenbosch professor is about to become the most powerful man in world rugby without leaving his office, a government is about to write the line into law, and the green jersey is about to mean something it had never been asked to mean before.

## Generation 2: The Machinery (1948–1969)

**6 August 1955. Ellis Park, Johannesburg.** Somewhere between ninety-five thousand and a hundred thousand people — nobody ever settled the number — are wedged into a ground built for far fewer, a world record for a rugby international, to watch the Springboks open a series against the British Lions. They see one of the great Test matches: the Lions lead, the Springboks storm back, and with the clock gone Theunis Briers scores in the corner to make it 22–23 — one conversion to win it, and behind him a metronomic fullback, Jackie van der Schyff. He misses. The Lions win by a point, the series will end drawn two-all, and van der Schyff never plays for South Africa again — remember that, because this generation has a habit of never forgiving.

Somewhere in that crowd is a thirty-seven-year-old lawyer from Soweto named Nelson Mandela.

That picture — the biggest rugby crowd in history, the future president of the country present and invisible in it, a scoreboard that for once did not obey — is this generation in miniature. It is about the building of a machine — a rugby machine, and around it a legal one — and about the first afternoons on which the machinery failed.

### The professor

The rugby machine had an engineer, and his name runs through the rest of this chapter. Danie Craven — born in Lindley in the Free State in 1910, Springbok scrum-half from 1931, capped in five different positions, inventor-populariser of the dive pass — was by 1949 the national coach; by 1956 he was president of the South African Rugby Board, an office he would hold for thirty-seven years, through every convulsion this story has left to tell, until his death in 1993. Along the way he collected three doctorates — ethnology in 1935, psychology in 1973, physical education in 1978, forty-three years between first and last — and the permanent nickname "Doc." At Stellenbosch, where he ran the rugby and the Physical Education department, he built the laboratory of South African rugby: the university club that fed the Springboks their halfbacks and their doctrine.

His teams made the case that the doctrine worked. In 1949 the All Blacks came — without their Māori players, the New Zealand union having ruled, in its own words, that "in view of the domestic policy of South Africa, the players cannot be other than wholly European" — and the Springboks beat them in all four Tests, a prop named Okey Geffin kicking five penalties in the first Test alone and thirty-two of South Africa's forty-seven points in the series. In 1951–52 Craven took the Springboks to Britain and France: thirty-one matches, thirty wins, a Grand Slam over the four home unions, and a 44–0 demolition of Scotland at Murrayfield so complete that South Africans still call it simply "the Murrayfield match" — and a try, in 1951, was worth three points.

What did that mean, back home? Here the historians should speak, because this is where an earlier kind of writing about South Africa reaches for cartoons. Albert Grundlingh, the Stellenbosch historian who has studied Afrikaner rugby more closely than anyone, puts it precisely: "support for the Springboks was on the same continuum as membership of the National Party" — not identical, not universal, but continuous; the same current ran through both. The scholars John Nauright and David Black complete the thought: Springbok success "came to symbolise both the actual and potential achievements of the Afrikaner people." A 15-man team beating another 15-man team was never what it was about. A nation half a century off a lost war was beating, over and over, everyone the empire could send.

### The laws

The legal machine was newer than people assume, and the dates matter. The National Party took power in 1948, but — as the historian Cobus Rademeyer has shown — for its first eight years it had no formal sports policy at all. What it had was the general architecture: the Group Areas Act of 1950 deciding where people could live, the Reservation of Separate Amenities Act of 1953 deciding which grounds, gates and grandstands they could enter — and sport, played on public ground, simply inherited the geography. Only in 1956, under Strijdom, did the government declare a sports policy proper: no mixed sport, and no mixed teams touring South Africa. The fields did not need a special law. The law already covered the fields.

And the rugby institutions were captured from inside. The Afrikaner Broederbond — the secret society working, since the Boer War's aftermath, to win back Afrikaner dominance of economic, cultural and political life — ran through the game's administration. Wilkins and Strydom, whose exposé *The Super-Afrikaners* remains the standard accounting, found that virtually everyone in the higher echelons of South African rugby was a Broederbond member — down to the referees of Test matches. Craven himself was long dogged by the most notorious sentence attributed to any rugby administrator — "there will be a black Springbok over my dead body." He denied ever saying it, and no one has ever produced the place, the date or the audience. It is quoted everywhere and sourced nowhere, and this book will not pretend otherwise. What needs no attribution is what the institutions did, which is the next twenty years of this chapter.

### The machine stutters

Then, within four years, the machine took three blows — one sporting, one sporting, one to the whole edifice.

In 1956 the Springboks went to New Zealand and lost a series for the first time anywhere in the twentieth century — New Zealand's first-ever series win over South Africa, celebrated there like a coronation. In 1958 France arrived, drew at Newlands, and then won 9–5 at Ellis Park under Lucien Mias — and with that, a fact so large it had become invisible was gone: from 1896 to 1958, sixty-two years, no touring side had won a Test series in South Africa. The fortress fell to a French team nobody had rated.

And in 1960 history stopped being about rugby. On 3 February, in Cape Town, Harold Macmillan told the South African parliament that a "wind of change" was blowing through the continent. Six weeks later, at Sharpeville, police fired into a crowd of pass-law protesters and killed sixty-nine people. The world's view of South Africa changed permanently that March — and three months after Sharpeville, the All Blacks arrived anyway. New Zealand had again left its Māori players at home; more than 150,000 New Zealanders had signed a petition against the tour — "No Maoris, No Tour" — and their prime minister had let it proceed regardless. The Springboks won the series 2–1. The pattern was set for two decades: the rugby world would mutter, petition, and come.

Inside the white game itself, that same winter, something small and telling happened. For the one-off Test against Scotland at Port Elizabeth on 30 April 1960, the selectors did something unprecedented: they gave the captaincy to a man who had never played a Test — Des van Jaarsveldt, a Bulawayo man, sixty-two times a provincial player for Rhodesia, the only Rhodesian ever to lead the Springboks. He won, 18–10, and scored a try. He was never picked again. He spoke English, not Afrikaans; he gave his team talks in English; he declined to lead the customary captain's prayer; and his appointment, the tributes would admit sixty-five years later, "unsettled many" in a rugby culture that had become Afrikaner property. First cap, last cap. Keep him in mind whenever this chapter is read as one people wronging another: here is the machine discarding a winning captain — white, loyal, victorious — for being the wrong sort of white man. Closed institutions do not express a people. They enforce a type, on whoever is nearest. Van Jaarsveldt went home, coached Rhodesia, and outlived nearly everyone in this generation; when he died in July 2025, at ninety-six, he was the oldest living Springbok.

The machine, it should be said, still won: the 1960–61 Springboks toured Europe and took a fourth Grand Slam. The rugby had never been better. The ground under it had never been worse.

### The courtroom and the golfer

The resistance in these years came less from stadiums than from courtrooms and clubhouses, and its foot-soldiers were not rugby men.

In October 1962, in the case of *The State versus Brandsma and others*, white, Coloured and Indian sportsmen were prosecuted — under the Group Areas Act and the liquor laws — for the crime of a football match between an Indian team from Durban and a mixed team from Pietermaritzburg. The magistrate's ruling opened an unexpected crack, and through it, the following year, walked a golfer: Sewsunker "Papwa" Sewgolum, an Indian caddie from Natal, who entered the 1963 Natal Open under the judgment's protection and beat a hundred and thirteen white golfers to win it. The government's sports policy had just been defeated, on its own soil, by one man with a back-to-front grip.

The organisations were assembling too. The South African Sports Association, founded in 1958, set out to build non-racial sport; in 1962 came SANROC — the South African Non-Racial Olympic Committee — created to carry the case against white sport to the world, its founding president the poet Dennis Brutus, whom the state would ban, shoot in the stomach during an escape attempt, and imprison on Robben Island. It worked anyway: South Africa was excluded from the Tokyo Olympics of 1964, the beginning of an isolation that would eventually reach every sport but one. Rugby held out longest, and this book will spend two generations on why.

### The week and the dam

In July 1964, in East London, Craven launched the thing that carries his name to this day: Craven Week, a national tournament for schoolboys, fifteen teams in its first year — an idea brought to him years earlier by the Springbok flanker Piet Malan, timed to the Board's seventy-fifth anniversary. It became, and remains, the greatest schools rugby institution on earth. It was also, like everything else on that ground in that year, whites only, and it stayed whites only for sixteen years. Both facts are true, and this chapter will come back to the year the second one changed — because the man who changed it was Craven.

Then came the day the machinery of rugby and the machinery of state collided in public. On **4 September 1965**, in Christchurch, a struggling Springbok side — losers that year to Ireland for the first time, to Scotland, twice to Australia, and already twice to the All Blacks — produced the tour's one great afternoon and won the third Test 19–16. On the same day, at a dam in the Transvaal, Prime Minister Hendrik Verwoerd stood before the National Party's youth league and took that friendship apart. Craven, in New Zealand, had given an interview the press read as accepting that Māori players might finally tour South Africa with the 1967 All Blacks. Verwoerd's Loskopdam speech was the public correction: "Our position has not changed. As we behave in other countries, we expect that they will behave here based on our customs — and I want to add: and everyone knows what it is." One contemporary wit said the speech had the timing and co-ordination of a camel with four left feet — the government had just kneecapped its own national obsession on the day of its best win in years. But it stood. In February 1966 New Zealand's prime minister, Keith Holyoake, answered in a single sentence that would take fifteen more years to become his country's policy: "In this country we are one people; as such we cannot as a nation be truly represented in any sphere by a group chosen on racial lines." The New Zealand union declined the 1967 tour. For the first time, the rugby world had said no — not marched, not petitioned: declined the fixture.

The decade closed with the affair that showed how far the state would go. Basil D'Oliveira — a Coloured cricketer from Cape Town who had emigrated to England in 1960 because his own country would not let him play first-class cricket — became an England player of obvious Test quality, and in 1968 the question of whether he could tour South Africa with the MCC broke the cricketing world in half. He was omitted, to English fury and nineteen resignations from the MCC; then a bowler withdrew injured and D'Oliveira was called up after all; and Prime Minister Vorster declared that this was no longer a cricket team but "a team of the anti-apartheid movement SANROC," and refused to receive it. The tour died. The historian André Odendaal, himself a first-class cricketer once, judges that nothing in South African cricket's history compares with it for bitterness. It was cricket, not rugby — but every rugby administrator in South Africa understood what had just been demonstrated: the government would rather lose the fixture, any fixture, than bend the line. Craven's world had eleven years of that lesson still to learn.

### What this generation leaves behind

A rugby machine at full power — four straight over the All Blacks in '49, two Grand Slams, the dive pass, Stellenbosch as the game's seminary — and the precise, sourced description of what its victories meant, in Grundlingh's words: the same continuum as the National Party card. A legal machine younger than the myth says, formalised only in 1956, enforced thereafter without sentiment — against a mixed football match in Pietermaritzburg, against a golfer with a wrong-way grip, against England's cricketers, and against its own winning captain, an English-speaking Rhodesian who never got a second Test. A week of schoolboy rugby that would outlast the state that built it. Two first-ever defeats — 1956, 1958 — that ended a sixty-two-year fact. And a friendship, the oldest and deepest in the sport, cracked at a dam in the Transvaal on the same afternoon its rugby was winning in Christchurch.

But this generation has told you only half of its own years, because through all of it — the trials, the boards, the tournaments of the men the newspapers never covered — another rugby history was running in parallel, and in 1959 it split, spectacularly, over the question of what to do about everything you have just read. The next generation runs the same clock again, from the other side of the line.

## Generation 3: No Normal Sport (1959–1979)

**3 July 1971. Athlone Stadium, Cape Town.** A final is being played on the Cape Flats — Western Province against Eastern Province, Western Province winning it 19–6 — for a brand-new trophy called, plainly, the South Africa Cup. The competition it continues is one of the oldest in the country's rugby: the biennial national tournament that Black and Coloured rugby had staged since 1898, twenty-eight editions across eight decades, almost always in Kimberley. But the old gold cup is not on the table, and its absence is the point. Two years earlier, the men who ran this rugby had voted to retire it — because it was the Rhodes Cup, donated by Cecil John Rhodes, and, in the words of the historian Hendrik Snyders, the decision "was based on a rejection of the political legacy of Rhodes as an imperialist and his company's exploitation of black workers in the mining sector."

The geometry of that decision closes a circle this chapter opened long ago. Rhodes underwrote the 1891 tour that gave white rugby the Currie Cup; the same Rhodes gave Black rugby its cup in 1897 — a better object, as it happens, than the Currie Cup itself. White rugby kept its trophy and never asked a question about the money. Black rugby looked at its own — the older tradition, the more valuable metal — and decided that some names cost more than gold is worth. They did not abandon their history; they renamed it, and kept playing. The tournament ran on as the South Africa Cup for another twenty years, and nobody's name was on it.

That is the kind of decision this generation is made of. It is the generation in which the rugby the newspapers never covered had to answer a question white rugby never faced: what do you do about a game you love when everything around it is wrong? Two answers emerged, from two men, and they were opposites. The rest of this generation is their argument.

### The split

Go back to 1959 — before Sharpeville, before the republic, before the government had offered non-white sport anything at all — and the argument begins, as arguments in small organisations often do, with two men who could not stand each other. Fourteen unions, representing more than ten thousand players, walked out of the old South African Coloured Rugby Football Board and formed the **South African Rugby Football Federation**. The record is blunt about the immediate cause: a bitter power struggle between the Board's general secretary, Abdullah Abass, and the man who became the Federation's president, **Cuthbert Loriston**. It was not, at its birth, a schism of principle. The principle arrived afterwards — and then it swallowed everything.

Because once the two bodies existed, they had to decide what they were for, and they chose opposite poles. Loriston's Federation chose participation: the phrase attached to him by those who honoured his memory was that he wanted to play "normal sport in an abnormal society" — take the fixtures, take the facilities, work within the state's rules and get boys onto good fields now. The old Board went the other way, and in 1966 it made the change visible: it dropped the word "Coloured" from its name — a designation, as one chronicler put it, that had been in line with colonial practice since the nineteenth century — and became simply the **South African Rugby Union**. Its president from that year was Abass himself: **Abdul Razzaq Abass**, Kimberley-born, a son of the same diamond city where both national boards had been founded, and he would hold the post for seventeen years. Under him SARU's answer hardened into the negation of Loriston's phrase, and in time into the most famous slogan South African sport ever produced: there could be **no** normal sport in an abnormal society.

Normal sport; no normal sport. Every non-white sportsman and sportswoman in the country would eventually have to pick one of those two sentences and pay its price.

### The world arrives at the argument

Through the 1960s and 70s, white South African rugby carried on as though none of this existed — and found, tour by tour, that the world was choosing sides in the same argument.

When the Springboks toured Britain and Ireland in 1969–70, they met something no rugby team had met before: a campaign. The **Stop The Seventy Tour** movement, chaired by a nineteen-year-old South African exile named **Peter Hain**, organised demonstrations at every single match. Protesters invaded pitches; tacks were strewn on playing fields; the team bus was hijacked; grounds were ringed with barbed wire to keep the game in and the world out. In Dublin, ten thousand people marched on Lansdowne Road. The rugby tour survived, barely — and that was the campaign's genius, because its true target was next in the calendar: the 1970 cricket tour of England, which was duly cancelled. Sport by sport, the doors were closing. Rugby's stayed open longest, and the reason is one this chapter has already given, in the historian Cobus Rademeyer's words: rugby had long since become a second religion to the Afrikaner, and the governments of New Zealand and Britain knew what the fixture was worth to Pretoria — and Pretoria knew what it was worth to itself.

How much it was worth had just been demonstrated in the most spectacular way imaginable: **the governing party of apartheid split over rugby**. Prime Minister Vorster's April 1967 sports policy had made a single concession that mattered — Māori players would be acceptable in a touring All Blacks side, though a mixed Springbok team remained "still unacceptable." For the National Party's right wing this was surrender. At the Transvaal party congress of September 1969, out of more than a thousand delegates, Albert Hertzog and seventeen colleagues refused to endorse the sports policy; they were suspended, and founded the Reconstituted National Party — the HNP — with the Māori question in the coming tour, the record says plainly, "a central point in this dispute." Let that register: the first formal fracture in the party of apartheid, and the fault line ran through a rugby team's squad list. In 1970 the All Blacks duly arrived with three Māori players — Sid Going among them — and the Samoan-heritage wing Bryan Williams, all reclassified for the duration under the rubric the state invented for the occasion: *honorary whites*. The Springboks won the series 3–1. The obscenity of the label outlived the scoreline by decades.

The white game's fields, meanwhile, staged their last great festivals of the old world. In 1974 the British Lions came under Willie John McBride and did not lose a game — twenty-one wins and a draw in twenty-two matches, the first three Tests taken, the fourth drawn 13–13 — an "Invincibles" tour remembered as much for the notorious "99" call, McBride's doctrine of simultaneous retaliation ("one in, all in": a referee cannot send off fifteen men), as for the rugby. And in 1976 the All Blacks toured again — landing on 30 June, **a fortnight after Soweto**. On 16 June the police had opened fire on schoolchildren protesting the imposition of Afrikaans in their classrooms; the count usually given is at least 176 dead, with estimates running to several hundred. The tour went ahead anyway, through a country in flames — the All Black Ian Kirkpatrick was tear-gassed in Cape Town along the way — and the Springboks won 3–1. This time the world did not confine itself to petitions. At the Montreal Olympics that July, after the IOC refused to act against New Zealand — rugby, it explained, was not an Olympic sport — **twenty-two African nations walked out of the Games**, many of their athletes already in the village, two days before the opening ceremony. Whole national teams, years of training, surrendered over a rugby tour to a country most of them would never visit. It remains, arguably, the largest sporting sacrifice ever made over another country's game.

### The council

The organisation that turned the slogan into a system had been founded three years earlier, on **17 March 1973**, at the Himalaya Hotel in Durban: the **South African Council on Sport** — SACOS — nine federations of the disenfranchised, under the presidency of Norman Middleton. Its founding mission was written down plainly: "to strive for non-racial sports structures from school level upwards and to generate opposition to and to expose discrimination in sport, in sport sponsorship and facilities in South Africa." In December 1976, the Supreme Council for Sport in Africa recognised SACOS as the authentic voice of South African sport — which meant, in practice, that the townships' sports council now held a veto over South Africa's readmission to world sport, exercised through the very African nations that had just walked out of Montreal.

The state answered with the tools it had. SACOS officials were branded "sports terrorists." Hassan Howa — the Cape Town cricket administrator who became SACOS's most famous president and the slogan's most famous voice — was refused a passport, year after year, so that the man the world's federations most wanted to hear could never arrive to be heard.

And SACOS turned its discipline inward, which is where this chapter owes the reader the other side of the resistance's ledger. In 1977 it adopted the **Double Standards Resolution**: no one registered with a SACOS body could also play in, or associate with, the state's "multinational" structures. It kept the movement from being hollowed out one ambitious player at a time — and it fell hardest not on ministers or administrators but on sportsmen, who could be expelled from their own non-racial clubs for taking the one paid contract or the one provincial trial that would ever be offered them. A movement built to defend players from an unjust system ended up policing players on behalf of a just one. Both descriptions are true; neither cancels the other. Principle, held that long against that much, always sends somebody the bill.

### The fork

Because the state, by now, was offering deals. In November 1977, under the "multinational" dispensation, Loriston's Federation and the African players' body — the South African Rugby Association, heir to the old Bantu board of 1935 — amalgamated with Craven's white Board into a single, nominally non-racial South African Rugby Board, affiliated to the International Rugby Board, its grounds opened to all races, its national trials open for the first time to players of colour. Real things followed for real players: Errol Tobias of Caledon — a name that owns much of the next generation — was picked for an SA Country XV alongside Turkey Shields; Hennie Shields made the Gazelles; Timothy Nkonki travelled to Buenos Aires to play in the centenary celebrations of Club Atlético San Isidro, of all places — the very cricket-club-turned-rugby-club world this book began in.

SARU refused. Abass would not bring ten thousand players into a structure whose fields were open but whose country was not; SARU stayed out, stayed with SACOS, and stayed poor. From 1977 the map of South African rugby was fixed for a decade: the SARB inside the world's game, carrying the state's flag and a handful of Black and Coloured pioneers; SARU outside it, carrying the principle and the bill.

### What this generation leaves behind

Two sentences — *normal sport in an abnormal society* and its negation — that between them contain every choice available to a Black sportsman in apartheid South Africa, and the knowledge that both were made by serious men for serious reasons, two decades before the world was watching. A tournament that killed its own trophy rather than keep an imperialist's name, and kept playing for a nameless one. A stadium's worth of institutional memory in one man: Abdul Razzaq Abass of Kimberley, secretary, president, refuser of deals — the city that founded both boards would one day put his name on its ground. A campaign run by a nineteen-year-old that closed cricket's door and showed the world rugby's could be reached. A ruling party broken in two by a squad list. Twenty-two nations walking out of an Olympics. And a discipline — no normal sport — that held the line and hurt its own, in a proportion this book declines to settle, because the men who paid it never agreed on it either.

The next generation opens inside the white game's fortress at the moment the fortress finally cracks: a Coloured flyhalf-turned-centre from Caledon runs out in the green and gold, a tour lands in New Zealand behind barbed wire and under a light aircraft, and a board that has spent a century saying no to everything begins, in secret, to say yes.

## Generation 4: Barbed Wire (1980–1989)

**25 July 1981. Hamilton, New Zealand — and every living room in white South Africa.** For the first time in history, a rugby match is being broadcast live into South African homes: the Springboks against Waikato, holders of the Ranfurly Shield, ten All Blacks in their ranks, twenty-seven thousand people in a ground dressed for a siege — barbed wire on the fences, farmers' trucks parked flank-to-flank as barricades, policemen in tracksuits and rugby boots because riot gear was thought, in the words of one officer, "a bit too confrontational for New Zealand society."

The viewers in South Africa never see a match. They see four hundred protesters tear down the wire, swarm the pitch, and lock arms in the centre of the field. They see police — ordered not to swing batons in front of the world's cameras — drag them away one at a time while the crowd bays; they see spectators vaulting the fences to take justice into their own hands; and then, with a stolen light aircraft rumoured to be heading for the stadium, they see the match called off. The protest movement had named the operation Everest, and it had just summited. In his cell, a Robben Island prisoner named Nelson Mandela — the invisible man in our Ellis Park crowd of 1955 — heard the news; he would say later that it was as if the sun had come out.

The historian Paul Dobson caught what that afternoon did to the country watching at home: "the vehemence of the opposition shocked many South Africans who believed that rugby men really wanted to play with them." That is this generation in one sentence. It is the decade in which white South Africa's game found out, live and in colour, what the world actually thought of it — and the decade in which the game's own establishment, before the politicians and ahead of its own public, went looking for the exit.

### The door cracks

It had opened, strangely enough, in hope. In 1980 the British Lions toured — Bill Beaumont's Lions, the last of the old-style visits — and the Springboks, kicked to victory in Test after Test by the metronomic boot of Naas Botha, won the series 3–1. That same year Craven finally forced through what had been proposed and shelved two years earlier: Craven Week, sixteen years white, was opened to every race. And on **30 May 1981**, at Newlands, the thing that had been unthinkable for ninety years happened quietly, in a 23–15 win over Ireland: **Errol Tobias** of Caledon — a builder by trade, a product of the Coloured rugby that this book has been following since Kimberley in 1897 — ran out at centre beside Danie Gerber, the first Black man ever to play for the Springboks. He won again the following week in Durban, 12–10.

Everybody at the time read his selection with both eyes open, and so does this book. To the government's critics, and to most of the non-racial movement, Tobias was window dressing — one man of colour, chosen at the precise moment South African rugby most needed a photograph. To Tobias himself it was the thing he had trained his whole life to do, and he said so, then and ever after. Both readings sat on his shoulders at once, which is why his generation deserves its name: the man in the middle.

### The tour

Then came New Zealand, and the winter that gave this generation its barbed wire. The 1981 tour began, of all places, with a welcome: at Gisborne the local Māori community received the Springboks formally at the marae — and the president of the Māori Council, Graham Latimer, told them to their faces: "we will not make another such welcome unless your government changes its apartheid policy." Twenty thousand came to the match, more people than lived in the town. Then Hamilton, and the cancelled match you have already seen. Four days later, in Wellington, a march on parliament met a line of police trainees — the regulars were away guarding the team — armed with short wooden batons, and when the crowd pushed, the rookies swung. The newspapers next morning carried photographs of batons raining down on demonstrators, among them schoolchildren still in their uniforms. The Battle of Molesworth Street, New Zealanders call it. After that, both sides armoured up — the police in riot gear at last, the protesters in crash helmets and pillows strapped on with tape — and the tour ground on through a country at war with itself, splitting families and friendships in ways New Zealand still talks about.

It ended at Eden Park on **12 September 1981**, in the most bizarre Test match ever played. Two-thirds of the New Zealand police force was deployed in Auckland that day. Around two thousand protesters — the radical remnant, the moderates having long since made their point — fought running street battles that the tour's chronicler judges "a riot, rather than a protest": about two hundred arrests, dozens injured. None of them got into the ground. What got into the ground was an aeroplane. A light Cessna made pass after pass over the pitch, dropping leaflets, flares, and — its signature — flour bombs, one of which flattened the All Black prop Gary Knight. The Welsh referee, Clive Norling, called the captains together and asked whether they wanted to continue; they played on, under the flight path, the Springbok backs (their captain Wynand Claassen admitted) watching the plane instead of the ball. And with the score 22–22 deep into injury time, it was Norling, not the Cessna, who broke South African hearts: a penalty most South Africans dispute to this day, kicked by Allan Hewson. 25–22, series to New Zealand — the Springboks' first series defeat there since the friendship began. At the end of it the team took the mounted springbok head that tradition awards to the best provincial side of a tour and gave it, instead, to the New Zealand police.

There was one more act, and it belongs in a spy novel. The tour's American leg had drawn such protest that the final Test against the US Eagles was played in secret: half the squad sent to a baseball museum as decoys, the Test team dressed in match kit under civilian clothes, driven lying flat in minibuses to a private polo field at Glenville, New York, where 250 State Troopers hid in the bushes and the goalposts were still going up as the players arrived. The pitch dropped two metres from one end to the other. Thirty-five people watched — most of them substitutes, troopers, or friends of the field's owner — the smallest crowd ever to attend a Springbok Test. South Africa, playing downhill in the second half, won 38–7, and flew home from a tour that had required three governments, two riot squads and a secret polo field to complete.

And Tobias? He had carried the heaviest baggage on that tour and played none of the Tests — midweek games only, while New Zealand's journalists asked him, over and over, whether a jersey mattered more than a vote, and management held him up in answer to questions about pass laws that, as it happened, did not even apply to him. Midway through, he asked to go home. Craven talked him out of it. In 1984 he got his reward, and it was not symbolic: installed at fly-half against England, he ran the Springboks to 33–15 in Port Elizabeth and 35–9 at Ellis Park — alongside Avril Williams of Paarl, two Black men in the green and gold at once, another first. Six Tests, six wins; no Springbok fly-half of his era did more with fewer chances. Then the fixtures ran out — for everyone.

### The empty years

Here the record needs setting straight, because the tidy version says rugby expelled South Africa, and it never did. Athletics expelled South Africa — the IAAF, in 1976. The Olympic movement expelled South Africa. **The International Rugby Board never did.** South Africa kept its seat in the world game's parliament through the entire apartheid era; Craven himself had chaired the IRB three times. What happened after 1984 was quieter and, in its way, more damning of everyone: the invitations simply stopped. England's 1984 visit was the last by an established nation for eight years. The World Cup was invented in 1987 and South Africa — a founder member of the board that sanctioned it — was not asked. Nor in 1991. Left off the guest list; never put out of the house.

New Zealand's own establishment tried one last time to come. The 1985 All Blacks tour of South Africa was signed and sealed when two Auckland club lawyers, Patrick Finnigan and Philip Recordon, sued their own union — arguing that a tour of apartheid South Africa breached the NZRFU's constitutional duty to "promote, foster and develop the game." The Court of Appeal let the case proceed, an injunction landed on 13 July 1985, and the union cancelled within days. Rugby politics by other means: two club members had done what governments and petitions could not. The response was the decade's ugliest fixture — in 1986 virtually the entire All Black squad, minus only David Kirk and John Kirwan, toured South Africa anyway as the unofficial "Cavaliers," unsanctioned by anyone. South Africa treated it as real — won it 3–1, too, after the Cavaliers' captain Andy Dalton had his jaw broken by a punch in the second match — and the rugby public pretended, for one last winter, that the world had come back. It hadn't. The Cavaliers went home to bans, and South African rugby went back to its rut: crowds shrinking, players ageing, the Currie Cup asked to feed a hunger it was never built for. By 1987 Craven himself dismissed everything he had tried — the front organisations, the media congresses, the rebel tours — as "the ineffectual old road."

### Harare

So the most powerful man in South African rugby did the thing that, in the world he came from, was closest to treason. He opened talks with the African National Congress.

It moved in steps: a meeting between his board and SARU — Abass's old union, SACOS's rugby wing — at the Cape Sun hotel in Cape Town on 7 May 1988; contacts with the exiled ANC leadership through Louis Luyt and the former Springbok Tommy Bedford; and then, on **15 and 16 October 1988, in Harare**, the meeting itself. Around the table: Craven and Luyt for the white board; Ebrahim Patel and Ismail Jakoet for SARU; and for the ANC — a banned organisation, whose members it was a crime to consult — Alfred Nzo, Barbara Masekela, Steve Tshwete, and a rising diplomat named Thabo Mbeki. The joint statement, preserved in the Board's own archive, reads with the flatness of all documents that change everything: the parties "agreed that South African rugby should come under one non-racial controlling body. They agree to work together to achieve these goals."

The reaction told you what had happened. President P.W. Botha called Craven a traitor — a word that, the record says, hurt him to the end of his days, and spurred him on. The government threatened to seize his passport and Luyt's. His own deputy president and half his board condemned him; the police rugby club raged that the head of rugby had sat down with the enemy their members died fighting. The Afrikaans press savaged him; the English press cheered. And Craven, seventy-eight years old, put an ultimatum to his own board — anyone unwilling to abandon racism in rugby should leave — and had the SARB write non-racialism into its constitution.

Do not mistake this for a conversion, because the scholarship is precise about what it was. Craven went to Harare, as the historian of the tour puts it, "not to negotiate a new settlement for the country, but to negotiate a way through which South African rugby stood a chance of returning to the international domain." As late as 1990 he remained opposed to universal franchise. His war was never against apartheid the political order; it was against what apartheid was doing to rugby. It happens — more often than either the hagiographies or the indictments allow — that a man does the right thing at real cost for reasons narrower than the thing itself. The ANC, for its part, understood the transaction perfectly: rugby was the Afrikaner's second religion, and the church had just asked them for a blessing. They gave it — and in giving it, demonstrated to every white South African paying attention who now held the keys to everything they missed.

The proof came within a year. In 1989 the Board turned one hundred, and for its centenary it got what a decade of scheming had failed to deliver: a **World XV**, sanctioned by the International Rugby Board itself, played two matches against the Springboks that August and September. Ten Welshmen came, eight Frenchmen, six Australians, four Englishmen, a Scot — every traditional rugby nation on earth supplied players except one. New Zealand, the country the tour of 1981 had torn in half, refused to a man. Archbishop Desmond Tutu, watching the Irish union release its players to celebrate the white board's birthday, said that support for the tour — while black children "were being beaten, tear-gassed and detained without trial" — was "obscene." He was right about the moment. And Grundlingh is right about the mechanism: the party was possible at all, he notes, largely because of the goodwill Harare had bought. The same handshake that made Craven a traitor at home had made his board respectable abroad. Both facts are the truth about that handshake.

### What this generation leaves behind

A first: Errol Tobias, six Tests, six wins, carrying a country's argument on his back in both directions — window dressing to some, a wedge in the door as he saw it, and by 1984 simply the best fly-half on the field. A tour that broke a friendship and half-broke two countries, remembered in flour and barbed wire, which put the truth on live television where white South Africa could not unsee it. A legal precedent set by two Auckland club men that did what governments wouldn't. The correction of a myth: rugby never expelled South Africa — it just stopped calling, which says more about rugby than an expulsion would have. And a handshake in Harare, made for rugby's reasons and history's benefit, that ended with the president calling the game's patriarch a traitor while the world's players flew in for his board's birthday.

The next generation opens with the wall coming down — a president unbanning the banned, two rugby bodies becoming one, the All Blacks landing at last at a ground called Ellis Park where a crowd will sing an anthem it has been asked not to sing — and then, in the winter of 1995, an afternoon that even people who hate rugby know by heart.

## Generation 5: One Jersey (1990–1995)

**15 August 1992. Ellis Park, Johannesburg.** The All Blacks are back — the first Test on South African soil since 1976, the reward for everything Harare set in motion — and it has been permitted on three conditions, set by the African National Congress and agreed by the rugby authorities: the old flag will not be flown; Die Stem, the anthem of the state that is dying, will not be played; and there will be a minute's silence for the victims of the political violence tearing the townships apart — Boipatong's dead barely eight weeks buried.

The silence is announced. And out of it, from fifty thousand throats, rises Die Stem — unaccompanied, unprompted, a cappella, sung *through* the minute of silence for the massacred. Thousands of old flags come out of jackets and bags. And then Louis Luyt — the man who sat at the table in Harare — has the anthem played over the stadium's loudspeakers after all, in open breach of the agreement his own game had made. The All Blacks win 27–24. Nobody remembers the score.

Both perspectives in this chapter were present in that crowd's noise, and this book has walked too far with each of them to flatten what happened. From one side: a people whose state, symbols and certainties were dissolving beneath them in real time, handed a minute's silence that many heard as one more instruction from their conquerors-to-be, at the one ground where they had always been unconquerable — and answering with the only sacred song they had. From the other: the non-racial movement watching men sing an anthem over a silence for murdered children, at a match played by the grace of the movement's own concession, and concluding that nothing — not Harare, not unity, not a hundred years — had changed the heart of white rugby at all. The ANC came within days of cancelling the second Test. It relented; Australia won a mudbath at Newlands 26–3; and South African rugby limped into the new world having shown, at its first opportunity, exactly how hard the new world was going to be.

### How the one body was built

The politics had moved faster than anyone dreamed. On 2 February 1990 — in the middle of a rebel cricket tour that the speech instantly rendered irrelevant — F.W. de Klerk unbanned the ANC and announced Mandela's release. The conditions the boycott movement had set out in the late eighties — the UN's Joseph Garba had demanded the abolition of the homelands, a single education system, equal access to sports facilities, the end of economic apartheid — stopped being utopian and became a timetable. And rugby, which had gone to Harare ahead of the politicians, now moved with startling speed: Louis Luyt for the white board, Ebrahim Patel for SARU, Steve Tshwete of the ANC in the chair, and the essentials of a fifty-fifty merger agreed, by the accounts of the men in the room, in barely a day of real negotiation. In **March 1992** the four bodies this chapter has spent five generations keeping apart — the SARB, SARU, the Federation, and the African association — became one: the **South African Rugby Football Union**, with Craven and Patel as joint presidents. A century and three years after Kimberley, and a hundred and six years after the Western Province Coloured union organised first, South African rugby had one governing body.

Danie Craven lived ten months of it. He died on 4 January 1993, at Stellenbosch, having run South African rugby for thirty-seven years — long enough to build the machine, defend the machine, watch the machine strangle the thing it carried, and finally, for his own reasons and at real cost, help break it open. This book has kept both truths about him in view the whole way, and lays him to rest with both: no man did more to keep South African rugby white, and no white rugby man did more to end it.

But unity had a second ledger, kept on the other side, and the braid in this chapter cannot be tied off without reading it. To much of the non-racial movement, what happened in 1990–92 was not a merger; it was an acquisition. The ANC, needing white South Africa persuadable, had launched its own sports wing — the National Sports Congress — which displaced SACOS and its hard line almost overnight; the moratorium politics of two decades were traded for readmission at speed. Christopher Merrett, who had served the non-racial movement for years, wrote its epitaph in one line: unity, done that fast, amounted to "a takeover by the old apartheid federations with a few faces of colour on boards." The scholar John Nauright reached the same verdict in cooler prose: Black administrators became ceremonial figureheads beside the old officials, who cited their experience and kept the desks. The players of SARU — the ten thousand who had held the line, the Eric Damonses who were never capped and never would be — got a unified badge and almost nothing else. When this chapter's last generations weigh what 1995 did and did not change, that ledger is where the weighing starts.

### The winter

And yet. There are perhaps five afternoons in this book that people who care nothing for rugby know about, and South Africa now staged the greatest of them.

The 1995 World Cup came to South Africa — the country's first, the readmitted nation as host — and the Springboks opened it at Newlands on 25 May by beating the champions, Australia, 27–18, a result that mattered less for the table than for the permission it gave a nervous country to believe. The pool stage gave the tournament its ugliest night — a brawl-wrecked match against Canada in Port Elizabeth that got the wing Pieter Hendricks suspended — and, through that suspension, its story: back into the squad came **Chester Williams** of Paarl, nephew of Avril, the third Black Springbok in history, who had been forced out on the tournament's eve by a hamstring and now returned to score **four tries** in the quarterfinal against Samoa at Ellis Park. The semifinal in Durban was played in the wettest conditions in World Cup history, kickoff delayed more than an hour while ground crews fought a waterlogged Kings Park; France came within a scrambled tackle of ending the fairy tale before Ruben Kruger's try and Joel Stransky's boot got South Africa out of the flood, 19–15.

And then 24 June 1995, Ellis Park, and an afternoon that has passed out of sport into folklore — every element of it, improbably, true. The South African Airways 747 that thundered over the stadium minutes before kickoff, sixty-three thousand people beneath it, "GOOD LUCK BOKKE" painted on its belly — Captain Laurie Kay and his crew had rehearsed the run for a week in simulators and light aircraft. And then the moment 1992 was answered: before kickoff the new country's two anthems were sung back to back — *Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika*, the hymn of the liberation movement, and Die Stem, the song that had been sung over the minute's silence three years earlier — joint anthems of the same state now, carried by the same sixty-three thousand voices, neither one sung against the other. What the crowd of 1992 had done in defiance, the crowd of 1995 did in chorus. The opponent: the greatest attacking force the game had ever seen, the All Blacks of twenty-year-old Jonah Lomu, who had trampled England in the semifinal and was expected to trample everyone. He never crossed the line. In the twelfth minute he broke clear with only Joost van der Westhuizen to beat, and the scrumhalf went in low, around the calves, Mark Andrews arriving behind — and the Springboks had their proof that the impossible tackle could be made. No tries were scored by anyone, all afternoon. Joel Stransky kicked everything South Africa got — three penalties, a drop goal, twelve-all at the end of normal time — and then, deep in extra time, struck the drop goal that every South African alive that day can still see in the air. 15–12.

Nelson Mandela came to the podium wearing the Springbok jersey — the number 6 of the captain, François Pienaar, and the team cap — the emblem of everything his movement had fought, worn by the man it had jailed, given back to the country as a gift nobody had the standing to refuse. He had visited the changing room before kickoff, where he found Chester Williams and told him: "I'm proud of you. Go make the rest of South Africa proud too." Pienaar would remember the moment the president turned and "my number was on his back." At the presentation Mandela thanked the captain for what the Springboks had done for their country, and Pienaar gave the answer the day deserved: "Mr President, we could never have done what you have done for South Africa." A minute later, asked by a journalist what it had been like to have sixty-three thousand fans behind the team, he finished the thought: "We didn't do it for 60,000 South Africans, but for 43 million South Africans."

That is the convergence this chapter's braid has been travelling toward since two boards were founded three years apart in the 1880s: one team, one trophy, the President in the jersey, both anthems sung, the world watching a country get, for one afternoon, the version of itself it wanted.

### The week and the years

The afternoon was real. But Chester Williams — the one Black face in that team photograph — earned the right to speak about the years, and this book made a promise about paving over facts. In a 2002 biography he alleged that inside the squad of the fairy tale he had been shunned, and racially abused by name by a teammate; and he offered the sentence that stands as the sober counterweight to everything above: "Winning the World Cup in 1995 may have unified the nation for a week. It did not change my standing within South African rugby. I was a black rugby player and that somehow separated me from the squad." The record obliges two further honesty: the fairy tale's critics quoted that sentence forever after — and Williams himself later softened it, more than once, relocating the abuse to his provincial years and at times disavowing the book's harshest claims altogether. What he never disavowed was the structural point, which the movement's veterans had made before him and the next generations would prove: an afternoon is not a transformation. The unified body still ran the old game with the old men; the townships and the SARU heartlands still had the old fields; and the jersey Mandela had blessed still had to decide, over the next thirty years, whom it actually belonged to.

### What this generation leaves behind

The one body, at last — SARFU, March 1992, four boards into one, joint presidents from the two rugby histories — and the true price of it, paid mostly by the side with less to sell. Two afternoons at the same ground, three years apart, that between them hold the whole question: a minute's silence sung over in 1992, and both anthems sung in 1995. A drop goal, a 747, a jersey on a president's back — the most successful act of political theatre in the history of sport, and the word *theatre* doing, as Chester Williams testified, real work. Craven dead two years before the final, and Abass's SARU dissolved into the very union he had spent a lifetime refusing — the two patriarchs' life's works merged, on terms neither had chosen. And a warning, issued by the winners' own wing: a week of unity is a week.

The next generation belongs to the professionals — the game goes openly paid within weeks of the final — and to the hangover: the dynasty that didn't come, the transformation that crawled, a coach who put the World Cup on the floor of a Paris dressing room, and a kid from the Zwide township in Port Elizabeth starting to play.

## Generation 6: The Professionals (1995–2017)

**19 September 2015. Brighton Community Stadium, England.** The champions of 1995 and 2007 are playing Japan, and the scoreboard says Japan 29, South Africa 32, with the clock dying. Japan win a penalty inside the South African half. Three points ties the game; everyone in the ground assumes the kick. And the Japanese captain, Michael Leitch, waves the tee away — the Springboks are a man down, and he has not come this far for a draw. Japan go to the corner, keep the ball alive through the phases, and the wing Karne Hesketh dives over in the left corner with the clock red. **Japan 34, South Africa 32.** It is, by the near-universal verdict of everyone who saw it, the greatest upset in the history of rugby union, and it happened to the country this chapter is about.

To understand how the afternoon of 24 June 1995 became the afternoon of 19 September 2015, you have to follow two stories at once — what happened to the rugby, and what happened to the question underneath the rugby, which never changed: whose team is this?

### The money arrives first

Professionalism did not creep up on South African rugby; South African rugby ran to meet it. Two days *before* the 1995 final — while the country was painting 747s — Louis Luyt called a press conference to announce that he, with the media magnates' man Sam Chisholm, had negotiated a television deal with Rupert Murdoch's News Corporation: US$555 million over ten years, for a new southern-hemisphere competition run by South Africa, New Zealand and Australia. Two months later, on 26 August 1995, the International Rugby Board's delegates, meeting in a Paris hotel, bowed to what the southern unions had already made inevitable and declared the game open. A century of amateurism — the ideology this whole book keeps meeting, the code that had structured who could afford to play everywhere from Kimberley to Cardiff — ended within nine weeks of Mandela in the No. 6 jersey. The timing is the point: South African rugby entered the new country and the new economy in the same winter, run by the same man who had played Die Stem over the minute's silence. What it did not enter with was a plan for the forty-three million.

### Streaks and pits

The rugby, at first, was magnificent. Under Nick Mallett the Springboks of 1997–98 won **seventeen consecutive Tests**, equalling the world record, and took the country's first Tri-Nations title in 1998 with a perfect four from four. The machine, professionalised, looked like the old machine with better conditioning. But the question underneath was already being asked in parliament and pressrooms: three years after unity, the team photograph had barely changed, and the arguments about targets, quotas and "merit" — words that would organise South African rugby's politics for the next quarter-century — settled into their permanent trenches. Both positions were real, as they have been all chapter: a **transformation** demand rooted in the plain fact that a unified nation's team drew on one-tenth of its people; and a fear, among those who loved the team as it was, that the jersey was becoming a political instrument — as if, this book is obliged to note, it had ever been anything else, under any of its owners.

Then the professional era produced its ugliest artefact. Before the 2003 World Cup, coach Rudolf Straeuli sent the squad to a paramilitary boot camp the players came to call **Kamp Staaldraad** — Camp Steel-wire. The details, when they leaked, read like an interrogation manual: players stripped naked and made to crawl the veld, starved, made to pump up rugby balls in a freezing lake, eggs broken over heads; and — the image nobody in South Africa has forgotten — the squad naked in a pit, ordered to sing the national anthem while "God Save the Queen" and the haka were blared at them through loudspeakers. As psychology it was medieval; as symbolism it was almost too neat — the new South Africa's team, naked in a hole, being taught that the anthem was a weapon and the enemy was everywhere. The team it produced lost its World Cup quarterfinal 29–9 to New Zealand in Melbourne — out before the semifinals for the first time — and Straeuli was forced out when the photographs surfaced.

### The second star

The recovery took four years and was built by Jake White. His 2004 side won the Tri-Nations; his 2007 side went to France and won the World Cup with a completeness the country's rugby had not shown since the isolation-era teams the world never saw. In the pool stage, on 14 September 2007 at the Stade de France, they beat the defending champions England **36–0** — a scoreline men on both sides still speak about quietly. In the final, at the same ground on 20 October, they beat the same opponents 15–6 in a game remembered for its one moment of doubt: England's Mark Cueto sliding into the corner, minutes into the second half, and the video referee finding — after an eternity — a boot's width of touchline under Danie Rossouw's tackle. No try; four penalties from Percy Montgomery and a monstrous one from François Steyn; a second William Webb Ellis Cup. John Smit lifted it. **Os du Randt**, the ox of a prop who had scrummed against the All Blacks in 1995, became the only Springbok with two World Cup winners' medals, twelve years apart — one man bridging Mandela's final and this one.

And the tournament's face, tries-wise, was South African and Black: **Bryan Habana**, the fastest wing of his era, scored **eight tries** — equalling Jonah Lomu's single-tournament record — on his way to a career of 124 Tests and 67 tries that only Japan's Daisuke Ohata (69) has ever bettered, with a career World Cup haul that would eventually equal Lomu's fifteen. A generation after Chester Williams stood alone in the team photo, the best finisher in the world game was a Black South African, and no one could call him window dressing. The scoreboard doesn't lie, and for once it was doing the transformation argument's work.

In January 2008 the union took the next step, and the country argued about it exactly as hard as you would expect: **Peter de Villiers** was appointed the **first Black head coach of the Springboks**. Whatever anyone said about the politics of his appointment, his 2009 stands with any season in Springbok history: the British and Irish Lions beaten 2–1 in a ferocious series — sealed at Loftus Versfeld by Morné Steyn's penalty **from inside his own half with the final kick** — and the Tri-Nations won with a clean sweep of the All Blacks, three from three. For a season, the oldest measures of Springbok greatness — beat the Lions, beat New Zealand — were all met at once, under a coach apartheid would have barred from the touchline.

### Brighton and the floor

The 2010s ate all of it. The side aged under Heyneke Meyer, and in 2015 the World Cup opened with the afternoon this generation started on: Brighton, Leitch's refusal of the draw, Hesketh's corner, Japan 34–32 — the two-time champions beaten by a team the sport had never taken seriously, in the biggest upset the game has known. No Springbok side had ever lost to a so-called minnow; the aura built across every generation of this chapter broke in eighty minutes on the English south coast. What deserves to be said for that team is what it did next, because the recovery was its own kind of character: Samoa 46–6, Scotland 34–16, the United States 64–0, and a quarterfinal against Wales won 23–19 by Fourie du Preez in the corner with five minutes left. The semifinal, against the eventual champion All Blacks at Twickenham, was lost by two points, 20–18, in the rain — as close as anyone got to New Zealand that year. Brighton was a catastrophe; it was not a collapse.

The collapse came after. Under Allister Coetzee the Springboks of 2016–17 hit the lowest floor in their history: a first-ever defeat to Italy, 20–18 in Florence; a slide to sixth in the world rankings, the worst position the team had ever occupied; and, on 16 September 2017 at Albany, the unthinkable made a scoreline — **New Zealand 57, South Africa 0** — the heaviest defeat ever suffered by a Springbok team. A hundred and twenty-six years of history in this chapter, and it had never once contained a South African side that could be beaten by fifty-seven points to nil. The unified game's second decade had produced a team that was neither transformed enough to answer the old question nor good enough to postpone it.

### What this generation leaves behind

A game professionalised in the same winter as the miracle, by the same hands as the anthem breach — money arriving faster than meaning. A world-record streak, a camp in a pit, and the full amplitude in between: 36–0 and 57–0 against the same two rivals inside a decade. A second World Cup, won with a completeness that briefly silenced every argument, and a Black superstar whose sixty-seven tries closed the era in which Black excellence in the jersey could be called symbolic. A first Black coach, and the best single season since readmission. And, at the bottom of it all, Brighton and Albany — proof that the question this chapter has carried since 1886 could no longer be deferred by winning, because the winning had stopped.

The last generation of this chapter begins with a phone call to Japan, a director of rugby coming home, and a decision that would have been unthinkable in every previous generation of this book: the captaincy of the Springboks given to a Black man — a flanker from the Zwide township in Port Elizabeth named Siya Kolisi.

## Generation 7: The Inheritance (2018–2026)

**9 June 2018. Ellis Park, Johannesburg.** This ground has carried most of the weight of this chapter. It held the hundred thousand of 1955 and the missed kick that was never forgiven; it heard Die Stem sung over a minute's silence in 1992; it watched a president in a green No. 6 jersey lift a cup in 1995. Now, for a Test against England, the Springboks run out behind their captain — and for the first time since a group of white men in Kimberley founded a board for this game in 1889, the captain of South Africa is Black: **Siya Kolisi**, flanker, of Zwide township, Port Elizabeth. South Africa fall far behind early, and win 42–39.

The man who made the appointment had been in the job for three months. **Rassie Erasmus**, unveiled as coach on 1 March 2018 and never merely a coach — analyst, provocateur, institutional politician, the most consequential Springbok figure since Craven — had named Kolisi captain on 28 May, and a week earlier had done something almost as radical in its way: for the previous Test, an outlier fixture against Wales in Washington DC on 2 June, he had picked **seven new caps in the starting fifteen, with six more uncapped men on the bench**, and accepted a 22–20 defeat as the price of finding out who could play. Among those debutants was a 27-year-old wing from a village called Tsholomnqa in the Eastern Cape, who had lost his mother at fourteen and walked twenty kilometres a day to school, and who had come up not through Craven Week and the famous rugby schools but through the game's back roads: **Makazole Mapimpi**. He is eighteen months from the most famous try in South African history.

### Yokohama

Erasmus's project moved with unnerving speed. In 2019 the Springboks won the Rugby Championship — their first title of any kind since 2009 — and went to the World Cup in Japan as a team nobody quite believed yet, a scepticism the All Blacks appeared to confirm by beating them 23–13 in the pool. No team had ever lost a pool match and won the tournament. This one did it the South African way: with a scrum, a maul, a wall of tacklers, the fastest outside backs in the competition — and an invention of Erasmus's that entered the game's vocabulary. He loaded his replacements' bench with forwards, six of them to two backs, and sent a fresh, near-complete second pack into the last half hour of matches to break what the first pack had bent. The squad called it the **Bomb Squad**, and the name stuck to the whole rugby world. Bench splits had been tinkered with before; nobody had built a team's identity around the eighth-to-eightieth-minute arms race, and nobody has un-built it since. So they ground through the knockouts to a final in Yokohama on **2 November 2019** against England — the same England who had dismantled the All Blacks a week earlier and arrived as favourites.

With a quarter of an hour left, England broken at the scrum and chasing the game, the thing happened that had never happened in the history of the tournament: a South African scored a try in a World Cup final. And it was Mapimpi — the kick-chase, the gather, the score in the corner — followed minutes later by Cheslin Kolbe's slalom past three Englishmen. **32–12.** In two finals and two victories, 1995 and 2007, South Africa had never crossed the line; the village wing crossed it first. And then the image that closed a circle a century wide: Siya Kolisi, first Black captain, lifting the Webb Ellis Cup — no borrowed jersey required this time, no gesture from a statesman needed to make the shirt mean the whole country. The captain simply was the country. Twenty-four years, almost to the season, after Pienaar's forty-three million.

### The ugly winter

Honesty about this generation requires its ugliness too, and 2021 supplied it. The British and Irish Lions came to a country closed by the pandemic and played a whole series in empty stadiums — three Tests of trench rugby with nobody there to hear them. The Lions took the first 22–17; South Africa answered 27–9; and the decider went 19–16 to the Springboks on a late penalty from, of all people, **Morné Steyn** — recalled at thirty-seven to do to the Lions in 2021 precisely what he had done to them from his own half in 2009. As a sporting story it was almost too neat.

Around it, though, the era showed its other face. After the first Test, a sixty-two-minute video assembled by Erasmus — dissecting, clip by clip, the refereeing decisions that had gone against his team — found its way onto the internet. It was rugby's ugliest public assault on a match official in the professional era, and World Rugby's misconduct panel treated it as such: Erasmus was banned from all rugby activity for two months and from match-day involvement for nearly a year, to October 2022. His admirers called the video calculated advocacy for a team the officiating establishment habitually shortchanged; the sport's authorities called it an attack on the game's foundations. This book, having watched a century of South African institutions bend rules they found inconvenient, declines to find the episode out of character — for the man or for the tradition he inherited. The genius and the transgression came in one package. They usually had.

The same period delivered a long-deferred piece of history to someone else: on 9 July 2022, at Bloemfontein, **Wales won a Test on South African soil for the first time ever**, 13–12 — one hundred and sixteen years after the countries first met. Every fortress in this book falls eventually; that one had outlasted them all.

### One point, three times

The 2023 World Cup in France compressed this team's whole character into three scorelines. Beaten in the pool by Ireland, 13–8, the champions again took the hard road — and won the quarterfinal against the hosts **29–28**, the semifinal against England **16–15** on a Handré Pollard penalty with the clock nearly dead after RG Snyman's try, and the final against New Zealand, on **28 October 2023**, by **12–11**. A record fourth title — no other nation has more than three — won by a cumulative margin, across three knockout matches, of three points. Kolisi lifted the cup again. You can argue about the greatest team in the history of international rugby; you cannot argue about the toughest. And the four stars above the springbok now belonged, inescapably and equally, to both of this chapter's histories: a team built by an Afrikaner rugby intelligence out of Stellenbosch's hundred-year doctrine, captained from Zwide, its most famous try scored by a man from Tsholomnqa.

### The present tense

The machine has kept running. Jacques Nienaber, the head coach of record for the 2023 triumph, left for Leinster; Erasmus took the title back openly in 2024, contracted through 2027, and won that year's Rugby Championship. In 2025 the Springboks beat the All Blacks **43–10 in Wellington — the heaviest defeat New Zealand has suffered in its history**, eight years after Albany's 57–0 ran the other way — and still could not conquer Eden Park, falling 24–17 at the one ground where no Springbok team has won since 1937. They finished 2025, as they had spent most of the decade, ranked first in the world. Off the field the oldest question of all — who owns the game — returned in its modern dress: in December 2024 the union's members voted down a private-equity offer of seventy-five million US dollars for a fifth of the Springboks' commercial rights, seven provinces against six, a Victorian federal structure strangling twenty-first-century money in a manner Bill Simkins and the men of 1889 would have recognised instantly.

As this chapter closes, in the third week of **July 2026**, the picture is this: the inaugural Nations Championship is underway, and the Springboks have opened it by beating England 45–21 in the first week of July and Scotland 42–28 at Loftus Versfeld on the 11th; tomorrow, 18 July, they meet Wales at Kings Park in Durban. They are ranked first in the world. Rassie Erasmus, now the longest-serving head coach in Springbok history by matches, has just passed his fifty-fifth Test in charge. And Siya Kolisi is nursing a hamstring and fighting — his word — for his place, while Pieter-Steph du Toit leads in his absence. Champions of the world, twice running; first in the rankings; arguing about money and succession. It is, by the standards of every other generation in this chapter, an astonishingly ordinary set of problems.

### What this generation leaves behind

A captain from Zwide with two World Cups, whose appointment answered — as much as any single fact can — the question this chapter has carried since two boards were founded three years apart in the 1880s. A try in Yokohama scored by a man who walked to school, in the one fixture where South Africa's greatest teams had never scored at all. A fourth star, won by one point three times over. A director of rugby whose genius and whose sixty-two-minute video belong to the same biography, in the manner of most of this chapter's patriarchs. The fall of the last fortress, to Wales, a hundred and sixteen years late. And a country whose rugby argument has not ended — transformation targets, equity votes, the unconquered ground in Auckland — but has changed register: it is now, recognisably, an argument about a game, conducted by a nation that owns it together. That took a hundred and sixty-five years. It was the whole distance from Gog's handwriting to Kolisi's armband, and this book knows of no harder road travelled by any rugby country on earth.

South Africa's story ends here, for now, at the top of the world. The next chapter goes back to where every thread in this one started — to England: the country that invented the game, codified it, exported it with the empire, and then spent a century and a half explaining to itself why the colonies kept winning.
